An OKR pairs an objective, a short qualitative statement of what you want to achieve, with 2–5 key results: measurable outcomes that show whether you got there. Teams set OKRs for a fixed period, usually a quarter, and grade them at the end.

In short

Each quarter, set no more than 3–5 objectives as plain-language goals and give each one 2–5 key results that measure outcomes from a starting value to a target. At the end of the quarter, grade every key result from 0.0 to 1.0: committed OKRs are expected to reach 1.0, and aspirational ones to average about 0.7. Label every OKR as one or the other, and keep the scores out of performance reviews so teams keep setting ambitious targets.

A direction in words, proof in numbers

OKRs give a team focus, a shared target and a way to tell whether it got there

Without a shared goal system, teams set vague goals such as “improve the product”, work on whatever seems urgent, and cannot tell at the end of the quarter whether they succeeded. OKRs fix this in three ways. They create focus, because there are no more than 3–5 objectives. They create alignment, because everyone sees the same goals. And they create accountability, because the key results are numbers you grade when the quarter ends.

Andy Grove developed OKRs at Intel and described the approach in his book High Output Management (1983). Google adopted them in 1999, when John Doerr, who had learned them at Intel, introduced them there.

The objective at the top of this page is one anyone at Larkwell could repeat, and it has no number in it: the numbers live in the key results. Weekly Active Learners counts learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week. It is Larkwell’s North Star Metric, and it appears as a key result, never as the objective. D30 retention is the share of new accounts still active on day 30 after sign-up. Monthly subscriber churn is the share of paying subscribers who cancel each month. Holding it at or below 7% is a promise, so that key result is committed, while the other two are aspirational stretches.

Without OKRs

The goal is “grow”. Each team works on something different, nobody has said what success looks like, and at the end of the quarter no one can say whether it went well.

With OKRs

Objective (aspirational): delight new users in their first week. Key result: D7 retention, the share of new users active on day 7 after sign-up, rises from 19% to 24%. Everyone works toward the same number. At the end of the quarter D7 is 22%: a score of 0.6, real progress, and a clear lesson about what worked.

What makes a good objective and a good key result

Four rules for each half of an OKR, the two kinds of OKR, and how a key result is graded

Objectives say where you want to go

Qualitative
Inspiring words, not a number. “Delight new users”, not “get 50,000 users”.
Ambitious
A stretch that pushes the team. If you reach every aspirational target in full, you are not aiming high enough.
Time-bound
One quarter, sometimes a year. The deadline creates urgency.
Few
3–5 at most, and fewer is better. With more than 5 objectives, nothing is a priority.

Key results prove you got there

Quantitative
Always a number, written “from X to Y” where a baseline exists: “increase D7 retention from 19% to 24%”. The starting value shows how ambitious the target is and makes grading simple.
Outcomes, not outputs
“D7 retention reaches 24%” is an outcome. “Ship 5 onboarding features” is an output: a task that may or may not change anything for users.
2–5 per objective
Enough to cover the objective from more than one side, few enough that people remember them.
Gradeable
At the end of the quarter each one gets a score from 0.0 to 1.0. Grading, below, shows how.

Key results usually come from metrics the company already tracks. Its North Star Metric, or one of the input metrics that drive it, makes a natural key result: Larkwell’s “Weekly Active Learners from 40,000 to 50,000” sits under an objective written in words. Pair an input-metric key result with a counter-metric, such as the completion rate of new lessons for lessons published, so the team cannot hit it by damaging something else. The projects that move a key result, such as redesigning onboarding, are initiatives. They belong in the team’s plan, not in the OKR.

When an output is a fair key result

Some deliverables are the promise itself: a compliance deadline, a contract, a set of lessons another team is waiting for. These can be key results in a committed OKR, because the team fully controls them. Label them as committed and as a deliberate exception, and put an outcome key result next to each one so you can still tell whether the work helped.

Committed or aspirational

Every OKR is one of two kinds, and they are graded differently. Label each OKR, or each key result when one objective mixes both, so no one mistakes a stretch for a promise.

Committed

A promise. The team expects to reach 1.0 and plans the quarter around it; anything less needs an explanation.

Example: “Ship GDPR compliance by March 31.”

Aspirational

A stretch beyond what the team knows how to do. The expected average score is about 0.7, and that counts as success.

Example: “Rank first in the App Store’s Education category.”

You may also hear “roofshot” and “moonshot”, terms from X, Alphabet’s research lab. They describe how ambitious a goal is, a big step or a giant leap. They do not say whether an OKR is committed or aspirational, so do not use them as labels for the two kinds.

Grading each key result from 0.0 to 1.0

At the end of the quarter, score each key result by the share of the distance it covered from its starting value toward its target. The raw value on its own does not tell you that: 22% means one thing if you started at 19% and another if you started at 21%.

(Actual−Start) ÷(Target−Start) =Score

Take the sample objective from the contrast above, an illustrative aspirational objective whose D7 target matches Larkwell’s Onboarding team. D7 retention was meant to rise from 19% to 24% and ended at 22%. The team covered 3 of the 5 points it aimed for: (22 − 19) ÷ (24 − 19) = 0.6. A value past the target scores 1.0, and a value below the start scores 0.0. The same formula works when the target is lower than the start, such as cutting production time from 5 days to 3.

An objective’s score is the average of its key results. Round every score to one decimal place.

What a score means

  1. 1.0DeliveredThe bar for a committed OKR. On an aspirational one, reaching it every quarter means the targets are too easy.
  2. 0.6–0.9StrongSuccess for an aspirational OKR, where the expected average is about 0.7. For a committed OKR, a miss to explain.
  3. 0.3–0.5PartialReal movement, but well short. Find out what blocked it.
  4. 0.0–0.2MissedLittle or no movement. Either the bet was wrong or the work never started.

The score starts a conversation

Grade quickly and honestly, then spend the time on why. A 0.4 with a clear lesson is worth more to next quarter’s plan than a 1.0 nobody can explain.

Write one sentence, then connect the levels

The OKR sentence, and how goals link across an organization

John Doerr’s test for an OKR is a single sentence. It forces you to connect the goal with measurable proof. If you cannot fill in both blanks, the OKR needs more work.

I willobjective as measured bykey results

Cascade and align

OKRs link levels of an organization. The company sets a few OKRs, and each team writes OKRs that move the level above. A higher-level key result often becomes a lower-level objective or key result, and a team adds others it can move directly. The chain can go one level further, to individuals, but many organizations stop at team level: individual OKRs read like personal targets and are easily pulled into performance reviews.

A cascade should align teams, not dictate to them. If every OKR is handed down, teams follow orders instead of committing, and the people closest to the work are never asked what they could achieve. Doerr suggests that roughly half of a team’s OKRs should come from the team itself. Leaders set the direction; teams propose how they will support it, and the two sides agree before the quarter starts.

How to read the set

Four of the six key results measure a change. The other two, publishing 8 new beginner song lessons and building 80% of new lessons on the reusable template, are outputs and deliberate exceptions. They are committed because the team fully controls them, and each sits beside outcome key results that show whether the work helped: week-1 completion and recognized songs for the lessons, production time for the template.

The team promises what it controls and stretches on what it can only influence. Lesson quality and use of the template are in its hands, so they are committed. Completion, learners playing a song they recognize and faster production depend on many things going right, so they are aspirational.

The key results sit in a chain. Publishing 8 lessons is an output the content team promises. The input metric it moves is quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, from 120 to 128. More good beginner songs should change a behavior, week-1 lesson completion, and that behavior drives the company’s North Star Metric, Weekly Active Learners. Every two weeks the team picks work that moves a key result, such as building the reusable lesson template.

    When OKRs fit, and when they do not

    They need a stable enough direction to plan a quarter against

    Use it when

    • You are setting goals for the quarter and need teams aligned on them
    • Several teams need to coordinate toward shared goals
    • The company is doing too many things at once and needs focus
    • You need to turn a strategy into measurable goals for the next quarter
    • An interviewer asks how you set goals or measure success

    Skip it when

    • You are a startup still looking for product-market fit. Goals change too fast for a quarterly cycle; set goals for a few weeks at a time instead.
    • Leaders plan to tie the scores to pay or reviews. Settle that first, or every target will be a safe one.
    • You need a delivery plan. OKRs say what should change, not who builds what and when; pair them with a roadmap and a delivery method such as Scrum or Shape Up.
    • The work is steady operations with no change intended. Track it as a health metric rather than an OKR.

    Six ways OKRs go wrong

    Most failures come from how the targets are set and used, not from the format

    1. Writing key results as tasks

      “Launch the redesigned onboarding” is a task. The team can finish it while nothing improves for users, and still mark the key result as done.

      Fix: write the outcome the work should change, for example “activation from 45% to 55%”. Keep deliverables for committed OKRs where the deliverable is the promise, and label them as the exception.

    2. Setting too many

      Take 8 objectives with 5 key results each: that is 40 things to track. Nobody remembers them, and with more than 5 objectives nothing is a priority.

      Fix: no more than 3–5 objectives, each with 2–5 key results. When in doubt, cut one.

    3. Sandbagging

      Setting easy targets to look good. If every key result scores 1.0 every quarter, the team is not stretching.

      Fix: label stretch goals aspirational and expect about 0.7 on average. Save committed OKRs for real promises.

    4. Tying OKRs to pay or performance reviews

      When a bonus depends on the score, people set safe targets to protect it, and ambitious goal-setting stops. Doerr makes the same point in Measure What Matters (2018).

      Fix: keep OKR scores out of compensation and performance reviews. Use them to learn and to plan the next quarter.

    5. Setting them and forgetting them

      OKRs written in week 1 and read again in week 13. A key result that went off track in the first month is only noticed at grading time.

      Fix: check progress on each key result every week or two, and hold a review halfway through the quarter.

    6. Mixing up committed and aspirational

      Without labels, a 0.7 on a committed key result looks like success, and a 0.7 on an aspirational one looks like a miss. Teams argue about the grade instead of learning from it.

      Fix: label every OKR, or every key result when one objective mixes both, and grade each type against its own expectation: 1.0 for committed, about 0.7 on average for aspirational.

    Planning a quarter and keeping it on track

    A 90-minute planning workshop, then short check-ins until you grade

    Must attend
    The team lead or PM (facilitates) and the people who will own key results
    Useful to have
    A data analyst for baselines, and someone from the level above to check alignment
    Prepare
    Last quarter’s graded OKRs, this quarter’s company or parent OKRs, and current values for the metrics you might use
    1. 0–15 min

      Review last quarter

      Read the scores and agree on one or two lessons to carry forward.

    2. 15–30 min

      Read the level above

      Go through the company or parent OKRs. Which key results can this team move?

    3. 30–50 min

      Draft objectives

      Everyone writes candidates silently, then the group clusters them and keeps 3–5 at most.

    4. 50–75 min

      Write key results

      Give each objective 2–5 key results written from X to Y, confirm a baseline exists for each, and label the OKR committed or aspirational.

    5. 75–90 min

      Test and commit

      Read each OKR aloud as one sentence, name an owner for every key result, and share the draft with the level above.

    Through the quarter

    1. Check in every week or two

      Each owner updates the key result’s current value and says whether it is on track. A 15-minute check-in is enough.

    2. Review at the halfway point

      Change or drop a key result that has turned out to measure the wrong thing, and write down why.

      Change a target because you learned something, not because it looks hard to reach.

    3. Grade and look back

      Score every key result, average them for each objective, and discuss what you learned before you draft the next quarter.

    Check your understanding

    Eight questions on writing, scoring and using OKRs

    1. What is an objective in OKRs?

      An objective describes direction in words, such as “delight new users in their first week”. The key results add the numbers.

    2. Which of these is the strongest key result?

      It measures an outcome and gives both a start and a target, so it is easy to grade. Shipping a flow and running experiments are outputs, and “improve retention” has no number.

    3. An aspirational key result ends the quarter with a score of 0.6. How should the team read it?

      Aspirational OKRs are set beyond what the team knows how to reach, so the expected average score is about 0.7, and 0.6 is close to it. A committed OKR at 0.6 would be a miss, because committed OKRs are promises to reach 1.0.

    4. Why should OKR scores stay out of compensation and performance reviews?

      When a bonus depends on the score, people sandbag: they set targets they know they will hit. That removes the stretch the whole system depends on.

    5. How do OKRs connect the levels of an organization?

      Company OKRs set direction, and each team writes OKRs that move the level above. In Larkwell’s cascade, the Onboarding team supports the company’s Weekly Active Learners and D30 retention key results with activation and D7 retention key results of its own. Some organizations take the chain down to individual OKRs, but many stop at teams so the scores stay out of performance reviews. Teams should propose many of their own OKRs rather than have them all handed down.

    6. Larkwell’s North Star Metric is Weekly Active Learners, now 40,000. How should it appear in the company OKR?

      The objective stays qualitative: “Make Larkwell the app adult beginners keep playing with”. The North Star Metric (NSM), or one of its input metrics, becomes a key result, here Weekly Active Learners from 40,000 to 50,000. Making the NSM the objective would put a number where words belong, and the projects that move it are initiatives, not key results. The NSM is long-lived, while OKRs are set each quarter, so each quarter can set a new target for it.

    7. A key result aims to cut production time per lesson from 5 days to 3. It ends the quarter at 4 days. What is its score?

      (4 − 5) ÷ (3 − 5) = −1 ÷ −2 = 0.5. The team covered half the distance from the start to the target. The formula works the same way when the target is lower than the start.

    8. Larkwell’s content team lists “Publish 8 new beginner song lessons” as a key result. When is that acceptable?

      Key results are normally outcomes, but a committed key result may be an output when the output itself is the promise. Label it as a deliberate exception and pair it with outcomes, here week-1 lesson completion and learners playing a song they recognize, so the team can tell whether the lessons worked. The input metric the lessons move is quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, from 120 to 128.
