Blue Ocean Strategy has you chart the factors an industry competes on, then change which of them you offer: eliminating and reducing some to cut cost, raising and creating others to add value. The aim is a leap in value for buyers at a lower cost to you, and with it demand no rival is serving.

In short

Draw the strategy canvas first, with one value curve per offering across the factors the industry competes on. If yours has the same shape as your rivals’, you’re in a red ocean, fighting for the same customers however much higher your curve sits. Pay for every Raise or Create with an Eliminate or Reduce, and redraw the canvas when imitators arrive, because every blue ocean turns red in the end.

A red ocean splits existing demand; a blue ocean creates new demand

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne set out the method in Blue Ocean Strategy (Harvard Business School Press, 2005; expanded edition 2015). They start from what most companies do: try to be slightly better than their rivals on the same factors, with more features, a lower price or better advertising. They call this a red ocean, existing market space where rivals compete on the same factors and fight over the same customers. Margins shrink as rivals match each gain.

Their alternative is to stop measuring yourself against rivals and change the factors you compete on, so that you’re playing a different game. That new, uncontested market space is a blue ocean, open water with plenty of fish and nobody else fishing it.

The method has two main tools. The strategy canvas shows how offerings compare on the factors an industry competes on. The four actions framework asks which of those factors to eliminate, reduce, raise and create.

Value innovation

Raise the value buyers get and lower your costs at the same time. Kim and Mauborgne call this value innovation. Most strategies accept a trade-off between the two; this one refuses it.

Diverge from rivals

If your strategy looks like your competitors’, you are in a red ocean. Benchmarking pulls everyone toward the same offer, so look for where you can differ instead.

Look to noncustomers

Serving current customers better keeps you in the same market. Find the people who are not customers of the industry yet and ask why not.

On a strategy canvas, the shape of each curve is the strategy

A strategy canvas is a chart of the factors an industry competes on and each offering’s level on them. It is the diagnostic at the center of the method: you draw it before you change anything, and again to show the move you propose.

Horizontal axis
The factors the industry competes on and invests in, such as price, range or service
Vertical axis
The offering level buyers receive on each factor, from low to high
Value curve
One offering’s levels across the factors, drawn as one line per company or group of similar companies

When the value curves share a shape, the industry is a red ocean: players differ in how much they offer, not in what they offer. A curve that sits higher on every factor is still in it. When one curve has a clearly different shape, that player has found a blue ocean. To test any move, draw your own curve next to your rivals’ and compare the outlines.

Reading the [yellow tail] canvas

The canvas plots the US wine market that Casella Wines entered when it launched [yellow tail] there in 2001. Premium and budget wines competed on the same seven factors, from price and wine jargon on the label to vineyard prestige and a wide range of wines. Their curves rise and fall together at different heights, separated mostly by price.

The [yellow tail] curve breaks that pattern. It sits at the bottom on wine jargon, mass marketing and aging quality, and low on vineyard prestige, wine complexity and range of wines. It sits high on three factors the industry had ignored, where premium and budget wines are both low: easy drinking, ease of selection, and fun and adventure. Range of wines and ease of selection are separate factors that move in opposite directions, because fewer wines made the choice easier. On price, [yellow tail] sits above budget wines and below premium ones.

Four actions reshape the value curve

Do not skip the cost side

Raise and Create tend to fill up first. Eliminate and Reduce mean letting go of things the industry assumes are essential, and that is where you start to diverge from rivals. The method ranks none of the four above the others; it asks for all of them together.

The four actions tell you how to change a curve. Kim and Mauborgne also give three tools for deciding where to point them: six paths across market boundaries, three tiers of noncustomers and a map of the buyer’s experience. Each one makes you look past the boundaries your industry has drawn around itself.

Three tiers of noncustomers

Noncustomers are the people outside your industry’s market today. Many of them already meet the need another way. Kim and Mauborgne sort them by how far they are from the market.

1

Soon-to-be

On the edge of the market. They use the industry’s offer minimally and are ready to leave when something better appears.

2

Refusing

They looked at the market’s offer and consciously chose against it, using an alternative or nothing at all.

3

Unexplored

In markets distant from yours. Nobody in the industry has thought of them as potential customers.

The buyer utility map

The buyer utility map is a grid that shows where an offer could create value. Its columns are the stages of the buyer’s experience and its rows are the levers of utility. Most industries crowd into a few cells, usually productivity during use, so the empty cells are where to look.

Larkwell’s blue ocean move

Worked example

A subscription app in a category where every app looks the same

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), sells subscriptions in a crowded category. Most apps for learning an instrument compete on the same factors: the size of the song library, the subscription price, gamification, the number of instruments covered and the quality of tutorial videos. In an app store they look alike, and because the experience is interchangeable, learners switch or give up easily. It’s a red ocean.

Noncustomers of piano-learning apps, by tier
TierWho they areWhat is missing for them
1. Soon-to-beSubscribers who still open the app once a week, mostly out of guilt, and are close to canceling. At Larkwell’s 7% monthly churn, about 4,200 subscribers a month move from this group to canceling.It feels like a game, not like learning
2. RefusingLearners who tried an app and canceled, people who chose a human teacher because apps do not feel like real lessons, and adults who always wanted to play but believe they cannotCredible teaching, and a confident first step
3. UnexploredCare homes, adult education classes and music therapists who run group sessions and have never been treated as app customersA product built for the person leading a group, not a solo learner

Larkwell trades breadth for depth

Rivals sell more songs and more instruments. Larkwell’s curve sells the feeling of actually learning to play, not just tapping along.

It adds value and cuts cost together. Practice plans, real-time feedback and milestones give learners something free video tutorials cannot match, while a smaller, curated library and less video production lower the cost.

The Create column speaks to tiers 1 and 2. Milestones tied to real songs answer subscribers who feel they are only playing a game. Practice plans built from a learner’s own mistakes answer people who chose a teacher because apps do not feel like real lessons, and Play Along gives hesitant adults music to play with from the start. Tier 3 would need a product built for group leaders, so this move leaves it for later. Before building anything, test with both tiers whether this is what would keep them or bring them in.

    Six ways a blue ocean move goes wrong

    1. A higher curve, not a different one

      Benchmarking leads teams to offer more of every factor. A curve that is higher everywhere but the same shape is a better red-ocean offer at a higher cost, not a new value curve.

      Fix: draw the proposed curve on the canvas beside your rivals’ before you commit. A good one sits low on several factors and high on a few; if yours rises everywhere, go back to Eliminate and Reduce.

    2. ERRC filled with features

      Listing product features instead of the factors the industry competes on produces a feature roadmap, not a strategy.

      Fix: draw the strategy canvas first and make every ERRC item a factor on it.

    3. Value without cost, or cost without value

      Value innovation needs both columns at once. A grid with only Raise and Create items adds cost and keeps you in the industry’s game, just with more on offer. A grid with only Eliminate and Reduce items is cost-cutting: a cheaper version of the same curve, which rivals can match.

      Fix: check that the Eliminate and Reduce items pay for the Raise and Create items. For every factor on the canvas, ask what would happen if you dropped it or did far less of it, and check that at least one Create item is something no rival offers.

    4. Asking only current customers

      Current customers describe the industry’s existing curve. They ask for more of the factors the industry already competes on, which pulls you back toward the shared curve.

      Fix: talk to people in each of the three noncustomer tiers before filling the grid, starting with people who tried the category and left.

    5. Treating the blue ocean as permanent

      Kim and Mauborgne note that successful moves attract imitators. Once rivals copy your new factors, the ocean eventually turns red.

      Fix: watch rivals’ value curves by redrawing the canvas regularly, and plan the next move before imitation closes the gap.

    6. Treating famous cases as proof

      The well-known examples were chosen after they succeeded. A grid that looks convincing on paper has not yet shown that noncustomers will buy.

      Fix: find the riskiest assumption in your grid, usually that a noncustomer tier will switch, and test it cheaply before committing.

    Test yourself on Blue Ocean

    1. What does Blue Ocean Strategy suggest instead of beating competitors?

      The method looks for buyers and factors no rival is contesting, so rivals stop being the yardstick. Undercutting on price is still a fight over a factor every player already competes on.

    2. What are the four actions in the ERRC framework?

      The third action is Raise, pushing a factor well past what rivals offer. Retain isn’t one of the four: anything you leave off the grid simply stays where it is.

    3. On a strategy canvas, what marks a blue ocean?

      A blue ocean curve dips where rivals invest and peaks where they don’t, so its outline differs from theirs. Beating every rival on every factor keeps their outline and only costs more.

    4. Why do the Eliminate and Reduce questions matter?

      Dropping or shrinking factors the industry overserves is what lowers your cost base. The method puts the four actions in no order, so the cost side matters for what it saves, not because it comes first.

    5. Who are “noncustomers” in Blue Ocean Strategy?

      Noncustomers sit outside the whole industry’s market, from people about to leave it to people nobody has considered. Someone buying from a rival is still the industry’s customer, just not yours.

    6. A learner tried a piano app, canceled after a month and now takes weekly lessons with a private teacher. Which tier of noncustomer is she for piano apps?

      She weighed what piano apps offer and walked away to a teacher, which puts her in the second tier. Paying a teacher doesn’t make her an app customer; noncustomers often meet the need some other way.

    7. A team’s ERRC grid lists more lessons, higher video quality and a larger song library under Raise, a live chat with a teacher under Create, and nothing under Eliminate or Reduce. What is the main problem?

      Every item adds cost and nothing takes cost out, and most of them push factors rivals already compete on, so the curve climbs without changing shape. More Create items would add cost too; the grid is missing its Eliminate and Reduce side.

    8. After Blue Ocean thinking suggests a move, which framework helps turn it into concrete choices?

      Playing to Win turns a direction into decisions: which markets to enter and what will let you win in them. Porter’s Five Forces usually comes before Blue Ocean, judging whether a market is attractive rather than deciding what to do in it.

    9. A year after your launch, two rivals copy the factors you created. What does that tell you?

      Imitation follows any move that works, so being copied is a cue to redraw the canvas and look for the next gap. Dropping the new factors would throw away the lead you still hold while rivals catch up.
