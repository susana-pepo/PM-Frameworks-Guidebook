The Pyramid Principle puts the main point first and supports it with a small group of ideas, each supported in turn by the ideas below it. Product managers use it for stakeholder updates, briefs, case interviews and any message that asks someone to decide.

In short

The rule of thumb is two to four reasons under the answer, each with its own evidence. Check every group for overlaps and gaps, and open with a sentence or two of Situation and Complication the reader already accepts, so nothing is in dispute before your point. Someone who stops reading after the answer still has it; the rest is there for the skeptic.

Turn the order you worked in upside down

Most people write in the order they worked: “I researched X, then analyzed Y, then compared Z, and my conclusion is…”. This is bottom-up order. The reader has to wait until the end to learn what you recommend, and has to hold every step in mind on the way.

A busy reader, such as an executive weighing several requests, wants the reverse: what you recommend, why, and the evidence. That is top-down order, and it is what the pyramid builds.

Here is one proposal written both ways: Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), wants to add bridge lessons to its beginner course.

Bottom-up

“We looked at the lesson data and found that 80% of week-one drop-offs happen in lessons 3–5. Then we saw that learners replay those lessons 4× as often as the others. Then we ran a two-week pilot, and completion of those lessons rose 25%. So we think we should maybe consider adding bridge lessons.”

The recommendation arrives in the last sentence, and it arrives hedged.

Top-down

“We should add bridge lessons between lessons 3 and 5. Three reasons: that is where 80% of week-one drop-offs happen, learners already replay those lessons 4× as often, and a pilot raised their completion by 25%.”

The reader has the answer and three reasons in about ten seconds.

Three levels, each supporting the one above

Built out as a full pyramid, the same proposal groups its case into three reasons (learners want to review, it is cheap to build, a pilot shows it works), each with two pieces of evidence beneath it. Minto gives each level a name.

Governing thought
The single main point at the top: your answer or recommendation.
Key line
The ideas directly below it. Each answers the question the governing thought raises in the reader’s mind, usually why or how.
Support
The facts, data and examples under each key-line idea.

A newspaper story

The headline is the conclusion, the first paragraph says why, and the rest gives detail. You can stop reading at any point and still have the main message. A pyramid works the same way.

Three rules hold the pyramid together

The answer goes at the top of the pyramid, straight after a short introduction, never at the end and never buried in a paragraph. Starting with the answer is the pyramid’s governing principle, not one of its rules. Below the answer, Barbara Minto sets out three rules for how the ideas fit together. She developed them from her training work at McKinsey and published them in her book The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking.

1

Each idea summarizes the ideas below it

Ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them. Each point raises a question, and the group below answers it. Every piece of evidence must support the point it sits under.

2

Ideas in a group are the same kind

Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea: all reasons, all steps or all problems, never a mix. A quick test: you can name the whole group with one plural noun, such as “three reasons” or “four risks”.

3

Ideas in a group are in a logical order

Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered. One order per group lets the reader follow it and notice when a piece is missing. There are four to choose from.

Two to four points under each idea

As a rule of thumb, support the answer with two to four grouped points. Fewer than two leaves it unsupported; more than four is hard for a reader to hold at once. Each group gets its own summary sentence, and that sentence is the idea above it.

Test each group for overlaps and gaps

MECE, short for mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, is the test for a grouping: no overlaps and no gaps.

Mutually exclusive
No two points say the same thing. If “user data” and “survey results” make the same point, merge them.
Collectively exhaustive
Together the points make the whole case. If someone asks “but what about X?” and X is a major consideration you missed, the group is not exhaustive.

Four ways to order a group

Between levels, the logic is either deductive, a chain of reasoning that ends in “therefore”, or inductive, a set of similar ideas that together support the point above. Within a group, the ideas follow one of four orders. A deductive group is in argument order; an inductive group is ordered by time, by structure or by degree.

The four orders for the ideas in a group
OrderWhat it followsExample
DeductiveAn argument: a premise, a second premise, thereforeDrop-offs cluster in lessons 3–5; bridge lessons ease those lessons; so bridge lessons should cut drop-offs
ChronologicalTime: first, second, third, or past, present, futureA rollout in three phases
StructuralThe parts of a whole, or placesOne point per team, region or product area
ComparativeDegree or importance, from most to leastThree risks, the largest first

SCQA sets up the question your answer resolves

Before the answer come a few sentences that tell the reader why it matters. Minto’s SCQA introduction moves from a situation the reader accepts to the complication that disturbs it, which leaves them asking the question your answer settles. Keep it to facts they know and agree with, since the introduction reminds rather than informs. The cards use Larkwell’s bridge-lessons proposal.

S

Situation

What does the reader already know and agree with?

“Most new Larkwell learners finish their first two lessons.”

C

Complication

What changed, or what is wrong?

“But 80% of week-one drop-offs happen in lessons 3–5, where difficulty jumps, and only 12% of new users are active on day 7.”

Q

Question

What question does that raise?

“How do we smooth the difficulty curve without making the course easier?”

A

Answer

What is your main point?

“Add short bridge lessons between the jumps.”

Test the introduction for disputes

Read the Situation and Complication as the reader. If they could reply “that is not true” or “I did not know that”, the sentence is an argument, not an introduction. Move it into the support, where evidence can back it.

A Slack message that asks the CPO for a decision

Worked example

Last year Larkwell was losing most new learners in their first week. A product manager wrote to the chief product officer (CPO) asking to add bridge lessons that quarter.

The decision fits in the first four lines

The recommendation and its target arrive in line four, after three lines the CPO already agrees with, so nothing is in dispute before the point. A CPO who stops at line four can still say yes.

Each reason is the same kind of idea, a reason to act, and each states its point before its figure. Together they answer the three questions a CPO would ask: do learners want this, what does it cost, and does it work. A two-page analysis that ended with the recommendation would hold the same facts, but the CPO would have to read all of it to find the point.

    Use the pyramid whenever you deliver an answer or ask for a decision

    Use it when

    • Writing stakeholder updates and executive emails
    • Writing PRDs and product briefs
    • Sending a Slack message that needs a decision
    • Answering a case interview question: lead with your answer, then the grouped reasons
    • Writing meeting agendas and kickoff documents
    • Giving a presentation or readout where the audience wants the answer

    Skip it when

    • Answering a behavioral interview question. Use the STAR Method instead.
    • Answering a product design question in an interview. Use the CIRCLES Method instead.
    • Brainstorming, where you want divergent ideas rather than a conclusion
    • Delivering an emotional or sensitive message, where leading with the conclusion can feel cold
    • Leading an audience that needs to reach the answer for themselves
    • Presenting to a skeptical audience that must be persuaded first. Use the narrative arc instead.

    When you can’t tell which structure the audience needs, lead with the answer.

    Six ways a pyramid falls over

    Each one makes the reader work to find the answer or to trust it

    1. Bottom-up order

      Walking through the research before the conclusion makes the reader wait for the point.

      Fix: put the governing thought first and turn the research into the support beneath it.

    2. Mixed groupings

      Reasons, actions and facts in one group break the rule that a grouping holds one kind of idea.

      Fix: check that every idea in a group is the same kind, and that the idea above it summarizes them.

    3. Overlaps and gaps

      Supporting points that overlap, or that leave an obvious case out, invite challenge.

      Fix: merge points that make the same case, then ask what a skeptic would raise that none of them covers.

    4. No order within a group

      A random list is harder to follow, and harder to check for a missing piece.

      Fix: give each group one order, whether an argument, a sequence in time, the parts of a whole or a ranking, and keep to it.

    5. A long introduction

      An SCQA that tells the reader new things delays the answer and starts an argument before you have made your point.

      Fix: give the Situation and the Complication a sentence each, using only facts the reader would nod along to.

    6. A hedged answer

      “We might want to consider…” at the top leaves the reader without a position to accept or reject.

      Fix: state the recommendation plainly, with “We should…” or “I recommend…”, and put the caveats in the support.

    Test yourself on the Pyramid Principle

    1. What does the Pyramid Principle put first?

      Your recommendation is the governing thought, and it comes before any of the case for it. The brief SCQA lead-in that precedes it only reminds the reader of what they know, so it is no place for new background.

    2. MECE stands for mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. What does it test?

      The two halves ask two things of a group: does any point repeat another, and is anything important missing? Putting a figure behind each point is a question of evidence, which MECE leaves alone.

    3. What do the letters in SCQA stand for?

      The sequence runs from a setting the reader shares, to the problem in it, to the question that problem raises, to your reply. Summary would open with the conclusion the introduction is meant to lead up to, and Action swaps your answer for a task.

    4. Which of these is the weakest fit for the Pyramid Principle?

      “Tell me about a time…” asks for a story of your own actions, and STAR is built for that. The VP update, the sign-off request and the brief each deliver a conclusion, which is exactly what the pyramid is for.

    5. A PM opens with “I analyzed X, then Y, then Z, so my conclusion is…”. What is the main problem?

      Narrating the work in the order you did it holds the recommendation back until the last line, and attention may run out first. Opening with the Complication would still leave the answer at the end.

    6. As a rule of thumb, how many points should support the answer?

      A single point can’t carry a case, and past four the reader starts losing hold of the first ones. Five to seven sounds thorough, but those points usually fold into fewer, broader groups.

    7. Your three reasons are “Churn is rising”, “A competitor launched” and “Hire two engineers”. Which rule does the group break?

      Rising churn and a competitor’s launch are facts about the market, while hiring two engineers is something you would do, so the group mixes two kinds of idea. Reordering the three would not fix that; the hiring belongs up in the recommendation, or in a group of actions of its own.

    8. You are explaining a rollout that happens in three phases. Which order suits the group?

      Phases happen one after another, so a sequence in time is the order the reader expects. A rollout may touch several teams, but grouping it by team hides what has to happen first.
