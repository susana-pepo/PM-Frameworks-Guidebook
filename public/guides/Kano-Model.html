The Kano model sorts features by how their presence, or their absence, affects customer satisfaction. The evidence comes from users themselves, through a short survey, and the result tells you what kind of investment each feature is before anyone ranks the list.

In short

Ask users two questions about each feature: how they would feel if it were there, and how they would feel if it were not. The pair of answers puts the feature in one of five categories. Fix broken Must-haves first, invest in Performance features, add one or two Delighters, and cut what nobody notices or wants. Keep the survey to 8–12 features; 30 responses give a directional result, and 100 give confidence.

Five ways a feature affects satisfaction

Noriaki Kano and his colleagues described these categories in 1984, in a paper titled “Attractive quality and must-be quality.” Each card gives the name this guide uses, with Kano’s own term at the foot.

M

Must-have

Expected, and noticed only when missing

Features users take for granted. Providing them earns you no credit, but leaving them out makes people angry.

Hotel: clean towels, working wifi. App: login works, pages load fast.

Kano’s term: must-be quality

P

Performance

More is better, and satisfaction scales with it

Battery life, storage space, delivery speed: satisfaction rises in proportion to how well you deliver them. These are the features users consciously compare between products.

Hotel: room size, breakfast quality. App: storage, search accuracy.

Kano’s term: one-dimensional quality

D

Delighter

Unexpected, and a pleasant surprise when present

Things users did not know they wanted. Their absence causes no dissatisfaction, because nobody expected them, but their presence earns a response out of proportion to the effort.

Hotel: a handwritten welcome note. App: smart autocomplete, playful animations.

Kano’s term: attractive quality

I

Indifferent

Nobody notices either way

Features that cost effort and leave customers feeling the same whether they are there or not, so cut them or keep them cheap. Survey only what customers actually meet: internal work they never see is not a Kano question.

Hotel: the brand of the soap dispenser. App: a detailed version-history screen.

Kano’s term: indifferent quality

R

Reverse

Some users would rather not have it

Features a segment of users actively does not want. Adding them lowers satisfaction for that group. They often appear in trade-offs between simplicity and power.

Hotel: loud background music. App: forced social features, gamification.

Kano’s term: reverse quality

How satisfaction moves in each category

Read the horizontal axis as how fully the need is met, not as money spent: an expensive feature can still be barely there from the user’s point of view.

Must-have (M)
Starts deep in dissatisfaction while absent and rises toward neutral as you provide it, but never above. A Must-have cannot delight anyone; providing it only stops the disappointment.
Performance (P)
A straight line through the center. Each step of improvement adds a proportional amount of satisfaction, which is where products compete.
Delighter (D)
Sits on neutral while absent, because nobody expects it, then climbs steeply once provided. It never drops below neutral. A small investment can produce an outsized response.
Indifferent (I)
Runs flat along the neutral line, however much you provide.
Reverse (R)
Slopes downward: the more you provide, the less satisfied this group of users becomes.

Categories drift over time

What delights today tends to be expected tomorrow, as customers get used to it. Built-in GPS on phones went from Delighter to Must-have within a few years: first a surprise, then something people compared between products, then something they noticed only when it was missing.

  1. DelighterA surprise nobody asked for
  2. PerformanceCompared, and wanted in larger amounts
  3. Must-haveExpected, and missed when absent

Re-run the survey every year

A category you measured last year may already have moved. Do not wait the full year when a competitor launches the same feature or it becomes standard across your market: those are the moments a Delighter turns into an expectation.

Two questions for every feature

One question imagines the feature present, the other imagines it absent. Both offer the same five answers, in the same order.

Functional question: the feature is present

“How would you feel if the product had this feature?”

  • I like it
  • I expect it
  • I am neutral
  • I can tolerate it
  • I dislike it

Dysfunctional question: the feature is absent

“How would you feel if the product did not have this feature?”

  • I like it
  • I expect it
  • I am neutral
  • I can tolerate it
  • I dislike it

The evaluation table

Cross the two answers to classify the feature. Find the row for the answer to the functional question and the column for the answer to the dysfunctional one.

M Must-have, P Performance, D Delighter, I Indifferent, R Reverse, Q Questionable: the two answers contradict each other, which usually means the respondent misread a question. Research papers often write A (attractive) for D and O (one-dimensional) for P.

Three patterns worth knowing by heart

  • Must-have: the user dislikes its absence and merely expects its presence.
  • Performance: the user likes its presence and dislikes its absence.
  • Delighter: the user likes its presence and is neutral about its absence.

From many answers to one category

  1. Classify each response

    Look up every respondent’s pair of answers in the evaluation table.

  2. Count by category

    Tally the M, P, D, I, R and Q results for each feature.

  3. Take the most frequent category

    The category with the most responses is the feature’s category, even when it falls short of half. Report the percentages alongside the label.

  4. Check Better and Worse

    When two categories are close, the two coefficients below show which way the feature leans.

The Better and Worse coefficients, also called customer satisfaction coefficients, come from Charles Berger and colleagues (1993). They summarize how much a feature can raise satisfaction and how much its absence can lower it. Reverse and Questionable responses are left out of both.

Better = ( D + P ) ÷ ( D + P + M + I )

Worse = −( P + M ) ÷ ( D + P + M + I )

Say 120 users of a hotel app answered the pair for an AR room preview: 54 Delighter, 18 Performance, 6 Must-have, 36 Indifferent, 4 Reverse and 2 Questionable. Delighter is the most frequent category, at 45%. Better is (54 + 18) ÷ 114 = 0.63 and Worse is −(18 + 6) ÷ 114 = −0.21: providing the preview lifts satisfaction a good deal, and leaving it out costs little.

Reading Better and Worse together

Better runs from 0 to 1 and Worse from 0 to −1. A common reading treats 0.5 as the dividing line: a high Better with a low Worse behaves like a Delighter, both high like Performance, a low Better with a high Worse like a Must-have, and both low like Indifferent.

Classifying eight features for a hotel booking app

Worked example

Eight hotel app features and their Kano categories
Feature Category Why
Secure paymentMust-haveUsers abandon the booking if payment fails. Nobody praises a card that simply works.
Fast page loadMust-haveWhen pages are slow, users leave before they book. Pages that load in an acceptable time earn no praise, because that is expected.
More hotel photosPerformance5 photos are good, 20 are better, 50 with video are best. Satisfaction scales with the number.
Price comparisonPerformanceMore sources compared means more trust and higher satisfaction.
AR room previewDelighterNobody expects it. Seeing the room in AR before booking is a memorable surprise.
AI trip plannerDelighter“Plan my weekend in Paris” is a pleasant surprise, and nobody misses it when it is absent.
Change the app iconIndifferentMost users do not care about customizing the icon, so it is development time with no return.
Mandatory reviewsReverseForcing a review after every stay annoys people.

Payment and page speed come before any Delighter

  1. Make sure the two Must-haves, secure payment and fast page load, work. Nothing else matters to a user who cannot book.
  2. Invest in the Performance features, more photos and price comparison, because that is where you compete.
  3. Add one or two Delighters, such as the AR room preview, to stand apart.
  4. Cut the Indifferent and Reverse features: the icon picker and mandatory reviews.

Speed is a Must-have up to a threshold and Performance beyond it. Until pages load in an acceptable time, users are annoyed and leave; past that point, further gains can add satisfaction the way a Performance feature does. The decision here is whether pages reach an acceptable load time, so the table keeps fast page load as a Must-have. Ask about an attribute at the level you are actually deciding on.

    Kano serves product strategy and is too slow for sprint planning

    Use it when

    • You are setting product strategy and choosing what type of features to invest in
    • You can survey 30 or more real users, enough for a directional result
    • You need to separate table stakes from differentiators
    • You are building a new product and mapping its feature landscape
    • You are analyzing competitors and where they invest

    Skip it when

    • You need to rank 15 features for the next sprint. Use RICE instead, or ICE if you have no usage data.
    • You have no access to real users to survey
    • The decision cannot wait: a Kano survey takes 1–2 weeks
    • It is internal tooling and your own team is the user
    • It is a very early MVP: ship it and learn from real usage

    Kano is the slowest of the four prioritization frameworks and gathers the most direct evidence from users, but it sorts features into types instead of ranking them. It runs alongside ICE, Value vs Effort and RICE when you need to know how a feature affects satisfaction. Compare all four.

    Six ways a Kano study goes wrong

    1. Skipping the survey and guessing

      PMs often classify features on gut feel, but the PM is not the user. Your Delighter might be your users’ Indifferent.

      Fix: run the paired survey with real users. The point of Kano is user data, not PM intuition.

    2. Treating categories as permanent

      Features drift from Delighter toward Must-have as customers get used to them (see The curve). A category measured once goes stale.

      Fix: repeat the survey on a fixed schedule and compare each feature with its last result. A feature that has moved category needs a new plan.

    3. Neglecting Must-haves because they are dull

      Teams enjoy building Delighters. But if the Must-haves are broken, Delighters will not save you: users leave anyway.

      Fix: audit the Must-haves first, and invest in Delighters only once the Must-haves are solid.

    4. Writing confusing survey questions

      Users find the dysfunctional question, how they would feel if the feature were absent, hard to parse. A confusing survey produces bad data and the wrong categories.

      Fix: use plain language and test the survey on three people first. Consider phrasing it as “Imagine this feature did not exist.”

    5. Collecting too few responses

      With 5 responses, one outlier changes the category. You need enough data for the results to hold.

      Fix: as a rule of thumb, collect at least 30 responses for a directional result and 100 or more for confidence. Segment by user type if you can.

    6. Using Kano to rank

      Kano tells you the type, not the order. Knowing that two features are both Must-haves does not tell you which to build first.

      Fix: classify with Kano, then rank within each category with RICE or Value vs Effort.

    Running a Kano study

    An async survey over 1–2 weeks, then a 60-minute workshop

    Phase 1: the survey

    Write the questionnaire: two questions for each of 8–12 features. Past 12, survey fatigue lowers the completion rate. Send it to 30–100 users through any online form or survey tool. Classify every response with the evaluation table, then work out each feature’s most frequent category and its Better and Worse coefficients.

    Question pair for one feature

    If [feature] were in the product, how would you feel?
Imagine [feature] did not exist. How would you feel?

Answer each with one of:
I like it
I expect it
I am neutral
I can tolerate it
I dislike it

    Phase 2: the workshop

    Must attend
    PM, design lead, engineering lead, and whoever ran the survey
    Useful to have
    UX researcher, product marketing, customer support lead
    Prepare
    Each feature’s category with its raw percentages and its Better and Worse scores
    1. 0–10 min

      Present the results

      Show the category for each feature with the raw numbers: “72% of answers classify it as a Must-have, 18% as Performance.” A percentage persuades more than “we classified this as a Must-have,” and it moves the debate from opinion to evidence.

    2. 10–25 min

      Discuss surprises

      Spend the time on features where the team assumed one category and users said another, such as a supposed Must-have that users called a Delighter. These blind spots teach the team the most.

    3. 25–40 min

      Audit the Must-haves

      Are any broken or missing? Write down who fixes each one, and by when.

    4. 40–50 min

      Plan Performance investments

      Which Performance features will set you apart, and how much should you invest in each? These are your competitive levers.

    5. 50–60 min

      Pick Delighter bets

      Choose one or two Delighters to prototype. They are high risk and high reward, so validate them cheaply before committing.

    Leave ranking out of this workshop. Hold a separate RICE session to rank the features within each category.

    Test yourself on Kano

    1. Users expect a feature but never praise it; they notice it only when it is missing. Which category is it?

      A feature that earns no credit when it works but draws complaints when it fails is a Must-have, like hot water in a hotel room. A Performance feature would win more satisfaction the better it got, and this one never wins any.

    2. A user answers “I like it” if the feature is present and “I am neutral” if it is absent. Which category?

      Enjoying a feature without missing it marks a Delighter. Performance would need “I dislike it” for the absent question, because users of a Performance feature feel its loss.

    3. Your team wants to build a flashy new feature, but login fails 10% of the time. What comes first?

      Users who cannot log in never reach the new feature, so the broken Must-have caps whatever a Delighter could add. Working on both in parallel only slows the fix while those users keep leaving.

    4. What happens to Kano categories over time?

      What surprised users once becomes normal, so a Delighter slides toward Performance and then toward Must-have. Treating a result as fixed is the mistake: last year’s Delighter may already be an expectation.

    5. Your team has 12 survey responses for a feature and wants to act on its category. By this guide’s rule of thumb, how many responses does a directional result need?

      Thirty is the floor for a directional read; 100 is the level for confidence, not the minimum to act. With a dozen answers, one odd respondent can flip the category.

    6. Kano gives you the category but not the order. What do you use to rank features within a category?

      Kano gives each feature a type but does not order features within one. The category order only says to handle Must-haves before Delighters; it cannot choose between two Must-haves, and a second Kano survey would only return the same categories. RICE or Value vs Effort orders features that share a type.

    7. A feature has a Better coefficient of 0.2 and a Worse coefficient of −0.8. How does it behave?

      A Worse near −1 says its absence hurts a lot, and a Better of 0.2 says its presence adds little, which is how a Must-have behaves. An Indifferent feature would score low on both, and this Worse is high.
