An executive summary is a short, decision-ready brief for leadership. It opens with the recommendation, then gives only the context, data, options, risks and request needed to make the decision. It is written last and read first.

In short

The recommendation goes in the first sentence, and the whole thing fits on one page, about 350 words. Keep 3–5 decisive numbers, 2–3 options including doing nothing, and 2–3 risks that each come with a mitigation. The ask says who decides what, and by when. When the draft runs long, cut Context first.

The decision comes first, the support follows

Leaders read many documents, and they rarely read one from start to finish. They read the first page, and often only that. A summary that leads with the decision respects that. If the page is clear and makes its case, the reader approves it or asks questions; if it is unclear, it gets put aside or sent back.

That makes the executive summary the most important page of anything you send upward. It turns weeks of research into a one-page decision document, and the full analysis moves to an appendix for anyone who wants the depth.

The habit has a name in US military writing: BLUF, bottom line up front. The conclusion and the request come first, and everything after them is support. It is the Pyramid Principle’s “start with the answer” applied to a whole document.

The information dump

Ten pages. The conclusion is on page 8 and there is no clear recommendation. The leader has to read everything to work out what you are asking for, so the document slides down the list.

The executive summary

One page. The recommendation is in the first sentence, followed by the key data, the options, the risks and a clear ask. The leader can decide in about three minutes.

Why Amazon writes memos instead of slides

Amazon replaced slide decks in its senior meetings with six-page narrative memos that everyone reads in silence at the start of the meeting, and its new-product proposals use the PR/FAQ, a document that opens with a mock press release (Bryar and Carr, Working Backwards, 2021). Neither is an executive summary. In his 2017 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos described great memos as written, rewritten, shared with colleagues for comment and edited again days later. Your one-pager will rarely get a silent reading slot, but the discipline carries over: compressing your thinking onto a page forces you to make it clear.

Six sections in a fixed order, each with a length budget

  1. Recommendation

    What you propose and why, in the first sentence of the first paragraph. If the reader reads nothing else, they have the answer.

    Budget: 2–3 sentences

  2. Context

    Why this matters now: the problem or opportunity, in just enough detail for someone who has not been following the project. Think of it as the Situation and Complication from SCQA, compressed to one or two sentences. The Question is implied and the Answer is the Recommendation, so Context only explains why a decision is needed.

    Budget: 1–2 sentences

  3. Key data

    The evidence behind the recommendation, and only the decisive part of it: the points that would change the decision if they were different.

    Budget: 3–5 data points

  4. Options considered

    The alternatives you weighed, including doing nothing, each with its time and cost, and the one you recommend marked. Leaders want to see that you thought broadly before you chose.

    Budget: 2–3 options

  5. Risks and mitigations

    What could go wrong and how you would handle it, including what you have already tested and the fallback if it fails. Acknowledging risks yourself builds trust in the rest of the page.

    Budget: 2–3 risks, each with a mitigation

  6. Ask and next steps

    What you need from the reader, stated precisely: who must decide, what resources you need, when the work starts and when you need the decision. Never leave the ask open.

    Budget: 1–2 sentences

Four rules for getting it onto one page

1

Write it last, place it first

Do all the analysis first, then compress it onto one page. The summary is the distillation, not the starting point: you cannot summarize what you have not fully thought through.

2

Hold the one-page limit

One page is about 350 words. If it spills onto a second page, you have not compressed enough. Cut context first, because leaders know more than you think. Then cut redundant data and remove qualifiers until every sentence is doing a job.

3

Apply the “So what?” test

For every sentence, ask why the reader should care. If a data point does not drive toward the recommendation, cut it. “Our NPS is 42” invites the question. “Our NPS dropped from 58 to 42 after the redesign” answers it.

4

Bold the scannable path

Someone who reads only the bold text should get the core message in about 30 seconds. Bold the recommendation, the key numbers, the option names and the ask.

A one-pager for Larkwell’s Practice Mode

Worked example

The Practice team asks the CPO for one six-week cycle

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), has 60,000 paying subscribers and $540,000 of monthly recurring revenue (MRR). The number its Practice team wants to move is day-30 (D30) retention, the share of new accounts still active 30 days after sign-up. The team has a 20-page analysis of why learners leave, and this page goes on top of it.

Everything a CPO needs to decide in three minutes

Nothing on the page sends the CPO to the 20-page analysis to understand the request. The analysis sits in the appendix for anyone who wants to check a number.

Each key number comes with a comparison (9% against an 11% target, practice sessions up from 0.4 to 1.2 a week in the beta, each point of churn priced at $5,400 of MRR), so each one passes the “So what?” test. The page also claims only what it can defend: it prices a point of churn and lets the A/B test show how many points Practice Mode removes.

Read only the bold and you still get the decision: build the Practice Mode MVP, lift D30 retention from 9% to 11%, approve one designer and two engineers, decide by Friday. The bold numbers in Key data add the evidence, and the whole pass takes about 30 seconds.

    Write one for any document that goes up to leadership

    Use it when

    • A product proposal needs leadership approval
    • You are preparing a quarterly business review
    • You are writing a PRD, as its first page
    • You are reporting a post-mortem or retrospective
    • Any document will be shared upward in the organization

    Skip it when

    • It is a quick Slack update. Lead with the answer using the Pyramid Principle directly.
    • You are answering a behavioral interview question. Use the STAR Method.
    • You are presenting to a room that needs persuading. Use the narrative arc, then send the one-pager afterward. If the audience just wants the answer, lead with it using the Pyramid Principle.
    • It is an internal team document that will not go to leadership
    • You are brainstorming or exploring, and there is no recommendation yet

    Six ways a one-pager fails

    1. Burying the recommendation

      Opening with background makes the leader hunt through the page for your position, and many will stop before they find it.

      Fix: open with the recommendation in the first sentence, before any context.

    2. Including all the data

      Every extra number dilutes the ones that decide the question, and the page spills onto a second sheet.

      Fix: keep only the 3–5 points that drive the decision, run each through the “So what?” test, and link the rest in the appendix.

    3. No clear ask

      “Thoughts?” is not an ask. Without a named decision and a date, the page gets read and nothing happens.

      Fix: say who must decide what, by when, and what it costs: “CPO: approve one designer and two engineers for the next cycle, about $80,000, by Friday.”

    4. Hedging language

      “We might want to consider…” weakens the recommendation: it tells the reader you are unsure, so they will be too.

      Fix: write “I recommend we…”, and put your doubts in Risks, where they can be handled.

    5. Offering no alternatives

      A single option looks like a decision already made, and a page with no risks looks unexamined. The reader stops weighing your case and starts looking for what you left out.

      Fix: show 2–3 options, including doing nothing, each with its cost, and pair each risk with a mitigation.

    6. Writing it first

      A summary drafted before the analysis is finished drifts away from where the analysis ends up, and the page argues for a conclusion the appendix no longer supports.

      Fix: write it last, once the recommendation is settled, then check every number against the analysis.

    Test yourself on the executive summary

    1. When should you write the executive summary?

      A summary compresses conclusions, so it has to wait until there are conclusions to compress. Drafting it first to frame the work feels efficient, but the page then argues for where the analysis started instead of where it ended.

    2. BLUF, bottom line up front, is a US military writing convention. What does it put at the top of an executive summary?

      The bottom line is your answer plus what you want the reader to do, so someone who stops after one sentence still knows both. Key data is tempting because it feels persuasive, but it is the support that follows, and leading with it makes the reader build the conclusion alone.

    3. How many data points should the Key data section include?

      Three to five numbers are enough to carry the case while each one still gets noticed. Every number you have sounds thorough, but the ones that would not change the decision crowd out the ones that would.

    4. You need a staffing decision from your VP. Which ask is strongest?

      It names the decision, the resources and the deadline, so the VP can say yes or no by a set date. “Let me know your thoughts” has a date but no decision, “approve… soon” has a decision but no date, and “we will need” asks for nothing.

    5. Why include the options you considered?

      Set beside the rejected options and the reasons for rejecting them, your pick reads as a choice instead of a default. You still recommend one, so the options back up your answer; they do not hand the decision back to the leader.

    6. Your draft runs onto a second page. What do you cut first?

      A leader already knows most of the background, so a shorter Context costs the least, and Key data is the next place to trim. Dropping Risks or Options also saves space, but it removes the proof that the plan was examined, and the recommendation and the ask are never cut.

    7. Which line passes the “So what?” test?

      A change with a cause tells the reader why to act. A single number with no comparison, however well sourced, leaves them asking “so what?”

    8. A colleague drafts the Context section as a full SCQA introduction that ends with the answer. The recommendation already opens the page. What should Context become?

      With the recommendation opening the page, Context only has to say why a decision is due now, which is the Situation and Complication cut to a sentence or two. Keeping the full SCQA makes the reader meet the answer twice and spends lines the page cannot spare.
