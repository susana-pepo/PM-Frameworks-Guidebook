Dual-track agile runs discovery and delivery side by side in the same team: discovery tests ideas cheaply to find out what is worth building, and delivery builds and ships what discovery has de-risked. It keeps a team from spending engineering time on ideas nobody has checked.

In short

Every week, the product trio of product manager, designer and tech lead runs small experiments on upcoming ideas while the whole team builds and ships ideas that have already been tested. An idea moves into delivery when evidence answers its value, usability, feasibility and viability risks, not after a fixed lag. Less engineering time goes into ideas that do not work.

Learning and building at the same time

Running discovery and delivery in sequence is what turns a team into a feature factory

In many teams, the Product Owner writes stories, engineers build them, and nobody checks whether users need them. The result is a feature factory: a team that ships feature after feature without moving the metrics those features were meant to move.

The root cause is that discovery and delivery happen in sequence. First the team researches for weeks, then it builds for weeks, then it learns the idea was wrong. Dual-track agile runs the two kinds of work in parallel instead.

Single track

Research, then wait. Write the spec, then wait. Build, then wait. Ship, and only then learn the idea was wrong. Months pass before the team finds out.

Dual-track

The team tests its next ideas while it builds the ones it has already tested. By the time an idea reaches engineering, its biggest risks are already smaller, and ideas that fail have cost days of testing rather than Sprints of building.

Not a relay between two teams

Discovery is not a separate group passing work across a wall, and it does not run a fixed number of Sprints ahead. The people who test an idea are the people who build it, and they move between both kinds of work during the week.

Desirée Sy described parallel design and development tracks at Autodesk in 2007, with designers working a cycle ahead of developers. Jeff Patton and Marty Cagan later popularized the name “dual-track” and stressed that the two tracks are continuous and run by one team. That later version is the one this guide teaches.

Dual-track agile is not a delivery method of its own. A team keeps the way it already delivers, Scrum Sprints or Shape Up cycles, and adds continuous discovery alongside it, done by the same people.

When an idea crosses over

An idea moves into delivery when experiments have answered its four risks well enough to bet on it. It is not a handoff: the tech lead who ran the feasibility spike is in the room when the team plans the build. Keep only a small stock of ready items, enough that delivery always has work but not so much that the evidence goes stale before anyone builds it.

Four risks, tested in hours or days

Find the riskiest question about an idea and answer it with the cheapest test

Marty Cagan groups everything that can go wrong with a product idea into four risks. Discovery exists to reduce them before delivery spends real effort. Start with the risk that worries you most.

V

Value risk

Will users want this?

Whether customers will choose it, use it or pay for it.

Ways to test: user interviews, fake door tests or a landing page MVP

U

Usability risk

Can users figure it out?

Whether people can work out how to use it without help.

Ways to test: prototype usability tests with about 5 users, or a Wizard of Oz test

F

Feasibility risk

Can we build this?

Whether the team can build it with the time, skills and technology it has.

Ways to test: a technical spike of 1–2 days on the hard part, or an architecture review

B

Business viability risk

Does it work for the business?

Whether it fits the business model, the law, sales, support and the company’s budget.

Ways to test: a stakeholder review, a legal check or a look at the unit economics

Write the idea as a hypothesis

A hypothesis says who will do what, and why. The “because” is what makes it testable: the experiment checks whether the reason holds.

Keep each experiment small

Each experiment should take hours to days, not weeks. A rough prototype tested with a handful of users often shows whether an idea works before any engineering starts. Testing with about 5 users is a common rule of thumb for finding most usability problems in one round of testing (Jakob Nielsen and Tom Landauer, 1993; Nielsen, 2000). It applies to usability problems, not to every question discovery asks.

Enough evidence to bet, not certainty

You do not need to remove all risk, only enough to make a confident bet. If usability was the open risk and 4 of 5 users complete the task in your prototype, that is enough to build it, measure it and iterate. Discovery reduces risk; delivery confirms the result at scale.

What the two tracks show

One team did both. The tech lead’s spike was discovery work, done by someone who was building the onboarding flow that same week.

Discovery shrank the idea before anyone built it. The interviews and the spike turned a vague request into a version the team can build in 3–4 weeks, and dropped one that would have taken 6 months or more.

Ideas cross over when the evidence is in. Play Along joins the Product Backlog because its tests passed, not because a Sprint was fixed for it in advance. How long an idea waits before the build depends on the idea and on what else is ready; there is no set gap.

Delivery still checks the result at scale. The new onboarding ships as an A/B test, because a prototype test with 5 people cannot show the effect on activation, which stands at 45% of new accounts against a target of 55%.

    Saying it in an interview

    Before building anything, I’d run a quick discovery cycle: define the hypothesis, identify the biggest risk, run the cheapest experiment that answers it, and build it only if the result meets the success criteria we set in advance. Meanwhile the team keeps shipping work we have already tested.

    When dual-track agile fits, and when it does not

    It pays off when building the wrong thing is expensive and users are easy to reach

    Use it when

    • The product manager, designer and tech lead have time for discovery every week
    • Uncertainty is high and building the wrong thing is costly
    • You want to stop being a feature factory
    • The team is mature enough to run two kinds of work in parallel
    • You can reach users for testing easily, as with most consumer products

    Skip it when

    • The team is so small that the product manager also does delivery work
    • The requirements are clear and already validated
    • The product is in a maintenance or bug-fix phase. A flow-based method such as Kanban (not covered in this guide) suits that better.
    • You have no access to users to test with
    • The team cannot take on the load of two tracks at once

    Five ways dual-track goes wrong

    Most of them turn the two tracks back into a sequence

    1. Discovery as a handoff

      A discovery group that passes a “validated backlog” to a separate delivery group recreates a small waterfall. Context is lost at the handoff, and the builders never saw the evidence. Jeff Patton calls this “duel track” rather than dual-track.

      Fix: have the same team work both tracks every week.

    2. Leaving engineers out of discovery

      Without the tech lead, feasibility risks surface only once delivery has started, when they are expensive to fix.

      Fix: run discovery as a product trio, with the tech lead as a full member.

    3. Tests that never fail

      Discovery that never kills an idea is confirming, not testing. Without criteria set in advance, any result can be explained as a success.

      Fix: write down before each test what result would stop the idea.

    4. Testing only usability

      A prototype that users can operate can still be something nobody wants, or something the business cannot support.

      Fix: list each idea’s value, usability, feasibility and viability risks and test the riskiest first.

    5. Big-batch discovery

      Weeks of research before the first test delay learning, and the team ends up back in a single track.

      Fix: keep each experiment to hours or days.

    Check your understanding

    Nine questions on the two tracks, the four risks and running discovery

    1. What problem does dual-track agile solve?

      Run in sequence, a team either researches while engineers wait or builds without research. Running both tracks continuously de-risks future work while current work ships.

    2. Which four risks does discovery test?

      These are Marty Cagan’s four product risks: will users want it, can they use it, can we build it, and does it work for the business. Address the biggest one first.

    3. How far ahead of delivery should discovery run?

      Dual-track is two continuous kinds of work in one team, not a schedule. Fixing a set gap as a rule turns discovery into a handoff. Keep a small stock of ready items so delivery has work and the evidence stays fresh.

    4. When is an idea ready to move into delivery?

      Readiness comes from evidence that the idea is valuable, usable, feasible and viable, well enough to bet on, not from estimates, approval or paperwork.

    5. A product manager skips discovery and sends ideas straight to engineering. What is the likely result?

      Many ideas do not move the metric they were meant to move, so testing an idea cheaply before building it saves engineering time. Building is a realistic test, but it is the most expensive one, and experience does not replace evidence.

    6. A trio’s discovery has approved every idea it tested this quarter. What is the most likely problem?

      Discovery that never kills an idea is confirming, not testing. Without a result written down before each test that would stop the idea, any outcome can be read as a success. Short, frequent experiments are what discovery is meant to run.

    7. A prototype test meets its criteria: 4 of 5 learners complete the flow, and the tech lead’s spike puts the build at 3–4 weeks. What should happen next?

      Discovery needs enough evidence to bet, not certainty. Once the criteria set in advance are met, the idea goes into the Product Backlog, and the same team builds it and measures the result at scale. A fixed waiting period or a handoff to another team turns the two tracks back into a sequence.

    8. Who leads discovery in dual-track agile?

      The tech lead is a full member of discovery, so feasibility is tested early. Other engineers join as needed, and the people who learned from the tests also build the result.

    9. How does dual-track agile relate to Scrum?

      Scrum provides a way to deliver; dual-track adds continuous discovery next to it, worked by the same people. The two are complementary.
