Dual-track agile gives one team two continuous kinds of work. Discovery tests ideas cheaply to find out what is worth building, and delivery builds and ships what discovery has de-risked, so less engineering time goes into ideas that do not work.

In short

An idea is ready for delivery once tests have answered its four risks: value, usability, feasibility and business viability. Each test should take hours or days, and a usability round needs only about 5 users to find most of the problems.

Run in sequence, discovery and delivery make a feature factory

In many teams, the Product Owner writes stories, engineers build them, and nobody checks whether users need them. The result is a feature factory: a team that ships feature after feature without moving the metrics those features were meant to move.

Single track

Research, then wait. Write the spec, then wait. Build, then wait. Ship, and only then learn the idea was wrong. Months pass before the team finds out.

Dual-track

The team tests its next ideas while it builds the ones it has already tested. By the time an idea reaches engineering, its biggest risks are already smaller, and ideas that fail have cost days of testing rather than Sprints of building.

Not a relay between two teams

The people who test an idea are the people who build it, and they move between both kinds of work during the week. Discovery is never a separate group passing finished research across a wall.

Desirée Sy described parallel design and development tracks at Autodesk in 2007, with designers working a cycle ahead of developers. Jeff Patton and Marty Cagan later popularized the name “dual-track” and stressed that the two tracks are continuous and run by one team. This guide follows the later version.

Dual-track agile is not a delivery method of its own. A team keeps the way it already delivers, Scrum Sprints or Shape Up cycles, and adds continuous discovery alongside it.

When an idea crosses over

An idea moves into delivery when experiments have answered its four risks well enough to bet on it. There is no fixed gap: discovery does not run a set number of Sprints ahead. Nor is there a handoff, because the tech lead who ran the feasibility spike is in the room when the team plans the build. Keep only a small stock of ready items, enough that delivery always has work but not so much that the evidence goes stale before anyone builds it.

Four risks decide whether an idea is worth building

Marty Cagan groups everything that can go wrong with a product idea into four risks. Discovery exists to reduce them before delivery spends real effort. Start with the risk that worries you most.

Value risk

Will users want this?

Whether customers will choose it, use it or pay for it.

Ways to test: user interviews, fake door tests or a landing page MVP

Usability risk

Can users figure it out?

Whether people can work out how to use it without help.

Ways to test: prototype usability tests with about 5 users, or a Wizard of Oz test

Feasibility risk

Can we build this?

Whether the team can build it with the time, skills and technology it has.

Ways to test: a technical spike of 1–2 days on the hard part, or an architecture review

Business viability risk

Does it work for the business?

Whether it fits the business model, the law, sales, support and the company’s budget.

Ways to test: a stakeholder review, a legal check or a look at the unit economics

Write the idea as a hypothesis

A hypothesis says who will do what, and why. The “because” is what makes it testable: the experiment checks whether the reason holds.

Keep each experiment to hours or days

A rough prototype tested with a handful of users often shows whether an idea works before any engineering starts. Testing with about 5 users is a common rule of thumb for finding most usability problems in one round of testing (Jakob Nielsen and Tom Landauer, 1993; Nielsen, 2000). It applies to usability problems, not to every question discovery asks.

Enough evidence to bet, not certainty

You do not need to remove all risk, only enough to make a confident bet. If usability was the open risk and the prototype meets the pass mark you set before testing, say 4 of 5 users completing the task, build it, measure it and iterate. Discovery reduces risk; delivery confirms the result at scale.

Worked example

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), has an Onboarding team that works in two-week Sprints. In Sprints 15 and 16 the team built and shipped its redesigned onboarding, cut from 7 steps to 3, and finished the inline microphone and keyboard check carried over from Sprint 14. In the same two Sprints the trio ran discovery on the next idea, Play Along: should early lessons let learners play along with a backing track?

The interviews showed that “with someone” meant two different products, and the tech lead’s spike put a price on each. That spike was discovery work, done in the same week the tech lead was building the new onboarding, so one team ran both tracks.

Play Along went into the Product Backlog in Sprint 16 because the prototype cleared its pass mark. No Sprint had been reserved for it, and when it gets built depends on what else is ready.

The onboarding work shows the other half: delivery still checks results at scale. The 3-step flow ships as an A/B test, because a prototype test with 5 people cannot show the effect on activation, which stands at 45% of new accounts against a target of 55%.

    Saying it in an interview

    Before building anything, I’d run a quick discovery cycle: define the hypothesis, identify the biggest risk, run the cheapest experiment that answers it, and build it only if the result meets the success criteria we set in advance. Meanwhile the team keeps shipping work we have already tested.

    Dual-track pays off when building the wrong thing is expensive and users are easy to reach

    Use it when

    • The product manager, designer and tech lead have time for discovery every week
    • Uncertainty is high and building the wrong thing is costly
    • You want to stop being a feature factory
    • The team is mature enough to run two kinds of work in parallel
    • You can reach users for testing easily, as with most consumer products

    Skip it when

    • The team is so small that the product manager also does delivery work
    • The requirements are clear and already validated
    • The product is in a maintenance or bug-fix phase. Kanban suits that better.
    • You have no access to users to test with
    • The team cannot take on the load of two tracks at once

    Choosing an execution framework sets dual-track agile beside Scrum, Shape Up and OKRs.

    Five ways dual-track goes wrong

    1. Discovery as a handoff

      A discovery group that passes a “validated backlog” to a separate delivery group recreates a small waterfall. Context is lost at the handoff, and the builders never saw the evidence. Jeff Patton calls this “duel track” rather than dual-track.

      Fix: have the same team work both tracks every week.

    2. Leaving engineers out of discovery

      Without the tech lead, feasibility risks surface only once delivery has started, when they are expensive to fix.

      Fix: run discovery as a product trio, with the tech lead as a full member.

    3. Tests that never fail

      Discovery that never kills an idea is confirming, not testing. Without criteria set in advance, any result can be explained as a success.

      Fix: write down before each test what result would stop the idea.

    4. Testing only usability

      A prototype that users can operate can still be something nobody wants, or something the business cannot support.

      Fix: list each idea’s value, usability, feasibility and viability risks and test the riskiest first.

    5. Big-batch discovery

      Weeks of research before the first test delay learning, and the team ends up back in a single track.

      Fix: run a first test within days, and let its result decide what to research next.

    Test yourself on dual-track agile

    1. What problem does dual-track agile solve?

      The problem is sequencing: when learning and building take turns, engineers either wait for research or build ideas nobody has checked. Slow pipelines and escaped bugs are delivery problems, and dual-track leaves the delivery method as it is.

    2. Which four risks does discovery test?

      Cagan’s four risks ask whether users want the idea, can use it, whether the team can build it and whether the business can back it. Reach, impact, confidence and effort are RICE’s factors, which rank ideas instead of testing them.

    3. How far ahead of delivery should discovery run?

      An idea moves when its own tests are done, so the gap differs from one idea to the next. Holding discovery exactly one Sprint ahead sounds tidy, but it brings back the scheduled handoff that dual-track removes.

    4. When is an idea ready to move into delivery?

      Evidence on all four risks is what makes an idea safe to bet on. A story-point estimate says what the build costs, which tells you nothing about whether users want the result.

    5. A product manager skips discovery and sends ideas straight to engineering. What is the likely result?

      Without cheap tests first, the team learns an idea was wrong only after paying to build it, and many shipped features leave their metric where it was. Building is the most realistic test, but also the most expensive way to learn what a prototype could have shown.

    6. A trio’s discovery has approved every idea it tested this quarter. What is the most likely problem?

      If nobody wrote down beforehand which result would stop an idea, any result can pass, so a perfect approval rate says more about the tests than the ideas. Lots of short experiments are the intended pattern, not the fault.

    7. A prototype test meets its criteria: 4 of 5 learners complete the flow, and the tech lead’s spike puts the build at 3–4 weeks. What should happen next?

      The test cleared the bar the team set before running it, so the idea is a reasonable bet, and real use will show what a 5-person test cannot. Holding out for 5 of 5 chases a certainty discovery never offers. A fixed wait or a handoff to another team would turn the two tracks back into a sequence.

    8. Who leads discovery in dual-track agile?

      With the tech lead in the trio from the start, technical surprises show up before the build instead of during it. A separate research team would pass its findings to builders who never saw the evidence.

    9. How does dual-track agile relate to Scrum?

      They don’t compete: Scrum says how to deliver, and dual-track adds discovery beside whatever delivery method a team uses. A Scrum team keeps its Sprints and events, and the same people run weekly discovery alongside them.
