Jobs to Be Done finds out why people behave as they do, design thinking explores and tests what to build, and User Story Mapping decides what ships first. The CIRCLES Method works from an interview prompt alone: it structures your spoken answer to a product design question.

In short

Start at the question you can’t answer yet: why, what or when. In real work the first three often run in that order, each one’s output feeding the next, but you can enter at any point. Plan on 8–12 switch interviews for a full Jobs to Be Done study and 4–8 people in the room for a story map, while a CIRCLES answer fills about 20 minutes of a 35-minute interview question.

Three frameworks shape a real product over hours or weeks, while CIRCLES shapes a 20-minute interview answer

CIRCLES Method
For a product design question in a PM interview. Seven steps answer a prompt such as “Design a product for X”: Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer’s needs, Cut through prioritization, List solutions, Evaluate tradeoffs and Summarize your recommendation. It gives the interviewer what they mean by “walk me through your thinking”.
Jobs to Be Done
For churn, positioning and finding the real competition. It looks at the progress a person is trying to make in a particular situation. People “hire” a product to make that progress and “fire” it when something serves them better, so the focus moves from who they are to what they want, and the needs nobody is meeting come into view. The idea is often summed up as “People don’t want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole”, a line usually credited to Theodore Levitt, who credited Leo McGinneva.
Design thinking
For new products and redesigns where nobody has framed the problem yet. A human-centered way to solve a problem you don’t fully understand: you get to know the person before you design for them. The Stanford d.school’s model has five modes: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype and Test. Teams move back and forth between them rather than through them once, and each cycle ends with a prototype tested with real users.
User Story Mapping
For turning a long backlog into releases that work end to end. The map lays the user’s journey left to right and the possible stories for each step top to bottom, in priority order. Lines drawn across it cut it into releases, so every release covers the whole journey, and a flat backlog becomes a plan the whole team can read.

The rules behind the picker

  1. Are you answering a product design question in an interview, such as “Design a product for X”?

    • YesUse CIRCLES. Its seven steps give you a structured answer.
    • NoYou are making real product decisions. Go to question 2.

  2. What do you need to figure out?

    • WhyWhy users behave as they do, for example why they churn. You need to understand their motivations: use Jobs to Be Done.
    • WhatWhat to build. The problem is fuzzy and needs exploring: use design thinking.
    • WhenWhat ships first. You know what to build and need to sequence it into releases: use User Story Mapping.
    • EverythingStart with Jobs to Be Done and build the story map once the job is clear. With weeks to spare, run design thinking between the two.

The third question, time, sets the size of the work. With only hours, Jobs to Be Done shrinks to 5–6 short conversations, and design thinking to a 200-minute workshop that starts from research you already have. With weeks to months, a “what to build” problem gets Jobs to Be Done research before the design thinking cycle. Story mapping is one 2-hour workshop whatever time you have.

Eight situations and where to start

Some of these involve Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults sold by subscription (figures are illustrative).

  • “Design a fitness app for seniors.”

    A PM interview. You have 35 minutes, and the interviewer wants to see structured thinking, not a shipped product.

    CIRCLES: walk through all seven steps so the interviewer can follow how you think.

  • “Larkwell subscribers cancel after their first month: why?”

    You have data showing the churn but not its cause. You need to understand what users were trying to achieve.

    Jobs to Be Done: run switch interviews to learn what job people hired Larkwell for and why they “fired” it.

  • “Leadership says we need a social feature, but nobody knows what that means.”

    A vague directive, an unclear problem and no research yet. Stakeholders each picture something different when they say “social”.

    Design thinking: empathize with users first, define the real need, then ideate and prototype options.

  • “The backlog for a new product area has 40 features.”

    Engineering asks what to build first. You know the features but need to organize them into releases that make sense.

    User Story Mapping: lay out the user journey, place each feature under the step it supports and draw release lines.

  • “Larkwell is considering a version for children.”

    A new audience with different needs and no existing data. You need to learn whether the idea is viable and what the product would look like.

    Jobs to Be Done first, to learn what job parents and children would hire a piano-learning app for. Then design thinking to explore solutions.

  • “How would you improve Spotify’s Discover Weekly?”

    A product improvement question in the same 35-minute interview format. The interviewer expects structure.

    CIRCLES: comprehend the feature, identify the customer and report their needs, then list solutions and evaluate the tradeoffs with metrics.

  • “New team members do not understand how the whole product fits together.”

    This is staff onboarding. The product has grown piece by piece, and no single view of the user experience exists.

    User Story Mapping: build the map together as a team exercise. Making the map is the onboarding, because everyone walks the whole journey.

  • “Users are asking for an offline mode. Should we build it?”

    The request is specific, but you are unsure about the need behind it.

    Jobs to Be Done: investigate the job. Is it “practice on the subway” or “avoid using mobile data”? The answer might not be offline mode at all.

Most real problems need two or three, each feeding the next

The full product pipeline

Jobs to Be Done finds the job, design thinking turns it into a tested solution, story mapping slices that solution into releases, and agile sprints build Release 1.

Three common combinations

Jobs to Be Done, then story mapping

A feature-rich product that is losing focus

Jobs to Be Done reveals the core job. The story map organizes the features around that job, and the release slices make sure every version delivers on it.

Larkwell’s piano app has grown to dozens of features. Interviews show the job is “feel progress fast”, and the story map reprioritizes everything around it.

Design thinking, then story mapping

A new product or a major redesign

Design thinking generates and validates solutions. The story map turns the validated ideas into a release plan.

In Larkwell’s onboarding redesign, testing shows that “play a first song in five minutes” wins, and the story map plans the first three releases of the new onboarding.

All three, in sequence

Entering a new market

Jobs to Be Done interviews reveal the job in the new market, design thinking explores what the product looks like for that job, and a story map plans the first release.

For Larkwell’s proposed children’s version, that means interviews with parents, design workshops with children, then a story map for the first release.

CIRCLES stays outside the pipeline

CIRCLES doesn’t combine with the other three in day-to-day work, and it isn’t a compressed form of design thinking. The two follow a similar path, from users to needs to solutions, but they were developed separately. Design thinking is research and prototyping with real users over days or weeks. CIRCLES structures a spoken answer of about 20 minutes, usually in an interview, with no users in the room, so it can’t stand in for the research the other three do.
