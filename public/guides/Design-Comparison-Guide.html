Jobs to Be Done, design thinking and User Story Mapping each answer a different design question at a different stage of product work; the CIRCLES Method structures a spoken answer to a product design question in an interview. This guide shows when to reach for each one and how their outputs feed one another.

In short

Use Jobs to Be Done when you do not know why people behave as they do, design thinking when you do not know what to build, and story mapping when you know what to build but not what ships first. In a PM interview, structure your answer with CIRCLES. In real work the first three often run in that order, each one feeding the next.

What each framework is for

The four in brief, and how long each one takes

7 steps

CIRCLES Method

A seven-step structure for answering product design interview questions such as “Design a product for X”: Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer’s needs, Cut through prioritization, List solutions, Evaluate tradeoffs and Summarize your recommendation.

Best for a product design question in a PM interview

Job stories

Jobs to Be Done

Looks at the progress a person is trying to make in a particular situation. People “hire” a product to make that progress and “fire” it when something serves them better. It moves the focus from demographics to motivations, and shows the real competition and the needs nobody is meeting.

Best for churn, positioning and finding the real competition

5 modes

Design thinking

A human-centered way to solve a problem you do not fully understand yet. The Stanford d.school’s model has five modes: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype and Test. Teams move back and forth between them rather than through them once, and each cycle ends with a prototype tested with real users.

Best for new products and redesigns where nobody has framed the problem yet

2D map

User Story Mapping

Lays the user’s journey left to right and the possible stories for each step top to bottom, in priority order. Lines drawn across the map cut it into releases, so every release covers the whole journey. It turns a flat backlog into a map the whole team understands.

Best for turning a long backlog into releases that work end to end

Each one answers a different question

Jobs to Be Done asks what job people need done, design thinking asks what solution serves them, and story mapping asks what ships when. CIRCLES asks how to lay out a design answer so an interviewer can follow it. Treat the first three as a sequence you can enter at any point: start at the question you cannot yet answer.

How long each one takes

These are typical times, longest first. The four differ by orders of magnitude, from minutes to weeks, so the right one depends partly on how much time you have. Each framework’s own guide gives the details.

  1. WeeksDesign thinking2–6 weeks for a full cycle, from empathy research to a tested prototype
  2. WeeksJobs to Be Done1–3 weeks for a full study of 8–12 switch interviews
  3. HoursStory mappingOne 2-hour workshop with 4–8 people
  4. MinutesCIRCLESAbout 20 minutes for the seven steps of a 35-minute interview question, leaving about 15 minutes for follow-up

Start from what you need to figure out

Two questions lead to a starting framework

  1. Are you answering a product design question in an interview, such as “Design a product for X”?

    • YesUse CIRCLES. Its seven steps give you a structured answer.
    • NoYou are making real product decisions. Go to question 2.

  2. What do you need to figure out?

    • WhyWhy users behave as they do, for example why they churn. You need to understand their motivations: use Jobs to Be Done.
    • WhatWhat to build. The problem is fuzzy and needs exploring: use design thinking.
    • WhenWhat ships first. You know what to build and need to sequence it into releases: use User Story Mapping.

The path picks a starting point

In practice you will often use two or three together. Once Jobs to Be Done has found the job, you might move to design thinking to explore solutions, then build a story map to plan delivery. See using them together, or answer up to three questions in the picker.

Eight situations and where to start

Find the one closest to yours

Several of these use the same running example: Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults, sold by subscription.

  • “Design a fitness app for seniors.”

    A PM interview. You have 35 minutes, and the interviewer wants to see structured thinking, not a shipped product.

    CIRCLES: walk through all seven steps so the interviewer can follow how you think.

  • “Larkwell subscribers cancel after their first month: why?”

    You have data showing the churn but not its cause. You need to understand what users were trying to achieve.

    Jobs to Be Done: run switch interviews to learn what job people hired Larkwell for and why they “fired” it.

  • “Leadership says we need a social feature, but nobody knows what that means.”

    A vague directive, an unclear problem and no research yet. Stakeholders each picture something different when they say “social”.

    Design thinking: empathize with users first, define the real need, then ideate and prototype options.

  • “The backlog for a new product area has 40 features.”

    Engineering asks what to build first. You know the features but need to organize them into releases that make sense.

    User Story Mapping: lay out the user journey, place each feature under the step it supports and draw release lines.

  • “Larkwell is considering a version for children.”

    A new audience with different needs and no existing data. You need to learn whether the idea is viable and what the product would look like.

    Jobs to Be Done first, to learn what job parents and children would hire a piano-learning app for. Then design thinking to explore solutions.

  • “How would you improve Spotify’s Discover Weekly?”

    A product improvement question in the same 35-minute interview format. The interviewer expects structure.

    CIRCLES: comprehend the feature, identify the customer and report their needs, then list solutions and evaluate the tradeoffs with metrics.

  • “New team members do not understand how the whole product fits together.”

    This is staff onboarding. The product has grown piece by piece, and no single view of the user experience exists.

    User Story Mapping: build the map together as a team exercise. Making the map is the onboarding, because everyone walks the whole journey.

  • “Users are asking for an offline mode. Should we build it?”

    The request is specific, but you are unsure about the need behind it.

    Jobs to Be Done: investigate the job. Is it “practice on the subway” or “avoid using mobile data”? The answer might not be offline mode at all.

Most real problems need two or three

Each framework’s output becomes the next one’s input

The full product pipeline

In real work the three research and planning frameworks chain together, and each step hands something concrete to the next. The notes below show what passes along at each handoff.

  1. Jobs to Be DonePasses on job stories and the four forces of progress.
  2. Design thinkingPasses on a POV statement and a tested prototype.
  3. User Story MappingPasses on release slices, starting with Release 1.
  4. ExecuteTakes Release 1 into the sprint backlog.

Three common combinations

The examples continue with Larkwell, the fictional piano-learning app from the scenarios.

Jobs to Be Done, then story mapping

A feature-rich product that is losing focus

Jobs to Be Done reveals the core job. The story map organizes the features around that job, and the release slices make sure every version delivers on it.

Example: Larkwell’s app has grown to dozens of features. Interviews show the job is “feel progress fast”, and the story map reprioritizes everything around it.

Design thinking, then story mapping

A new product or a major redesign

Design thinking generates and validates solutions. The story map turns the validated ideas into a release plan.

Example: Larkwell’s onboarding redesign. Testing shows that “play a first song in five minutes” wins, and the story map plans the first three releases of the new onboarding.

All three, in sequence

Entering a new market

Jobs to Be Done interviews reveal the job in the new market, design thinking explores what the product looks like for that job, and a story map plans the first release.

Example: Larkwell’s proposed version for children. Interviews with parents, design workshops with children, then a story map for the first release of the children’s version.

CIRCLES stays outside the pipeline

CIRCLES does not combine with the other three in day-to-day work, and it is not a compressed form of design thinking. It structures a spoken answer of about 20 minutes, mostly in interviews. CIRCLES and design thinking follow a similar path, from users to needs to solutions, but they were developed separately: design thinking is research and prototyping with real users over days or weeks, while CIRCLES involves no real users, so it does not replace the research the other three do.
