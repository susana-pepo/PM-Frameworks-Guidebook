A user story map lays the user’s journey left to right and the possible stories for each step top to bottom, in priority order. Lines drawn across the map cut it into releases, so every release covers the whole journey.

In short

Release 1, the walking skeleton, puts the simplest story under every user step, so a new user can get from the first screen to value. As rules of thumb, a map has 3⁠–⁠7 activities and 8⁠–⁠15 user steps across the backbone, and every release gets its own success metric. Priority orders the stories within a column; the backbone always stays in journey order.

A map instead of a list

A traditional backlog is a prioritized list: item 1, item 2, item 3, all the way down to item 200. It tells you what to build next, but it loses why you are building it and how the user experiences it. Jeff Patton, who popularized story mapping, describes it in User Story Mapping (with Peter Economy, 2014) as an alternative to the flat backlog: a flat backlog loses the story of what the user is doing, and a map keeps it.

Flat backlog

  1. Email login
  2. Lesson player
  3. Progress tracker
  4. Payment flow
  5. Google sign-in
  6. Streak counter
  7. Family plan

…down to item 200. What journey does this create? There is no way to tell, because every story stands alone.

Story map

The same stories, each hung under the user step it serves: email login under Enter email, the streak counter under See results, the family plan under Start a free trial.

Take the top story in every column and you have a complete journey, from the store listing to a trial checkout, thin but usable.

Across is the story, down is the plan

↔

Horizontal: the story

Time runs left to right, so the top rows read as a sentence: the learner discovers the app, signs up, takes a lesson, tracks progress and subscribes. This axis is the narrative.

↕

Vertical: the priority

Each column runs from the story a step can’t do without to the extras. Under Play the first song, the play-along lesson comes first, feedback on playing next and challenges last. This axis is the plan.

Release 1 is a walking skeleton

The walking skeleton is Release 1: the thinnest version of the whole journey that works end to end. Each step gets its most basic story, but no step is skipped, so a user can walk the whole path. The term comes from Alistair Cockburn (Crystal Clear, 2004); Patton uses it for the first slice of the map.

Each cut across the map is a release slice: a horizontal line drawn across every column, with everything above it shipping in that release. Agile teams often call this a vertical slice, because it cuts through every layer of the system, from interface to database; on the map it is drawn as a horizontal band. What to avoid is a release that runs down one column instead of across all of them, such as perfecting login while every later step stays empty.

Later slices build on Release 1 and add stories only to the steps they improve. That’s why the map has blank cells in Releases 2 and 3: an earlier release already covers the step, and the product stays usable after every release. Release 1 also isn’t the list of must-have features. It takes the thinnest story under each step, which usually includes some must-haves and leaves others for later.

Four layers, from goal to stories

1

Goal

What does the user want to achieve?

The big outcome the user is after. It sits at the very top and frames everything below it, like the title of a film: the whole story in one line.

The map’s goal is “Learn to play an instrument online”. Others: “Find and book a vacation rental”, “Manage my team’s sprint work”.

One per map

2

Activities

What are the big phases of the journey?

The big things the user does, grouping the steps below them. Think of them as the chapter headings of the user’s story.

The map has five, from Discover to Subscribe. Other maps use Onboard, Learn, Practice or Share. Each one holds several specific steps.

3–7 per map, as a rule of thumb · top row of the backbone

3

User steps

What does the user do in each phase?

The specific things the user does within each activity, sometimes called user tasks, written from the user’s perspective, not the system’s. Read left to right in narrative order, they tell the user’s story.

Example, under Sign up: Enter email → Choose instrument → Set goal → Pick level.

8–15 per map, as a rule of thumb · second row of the backbone

4

Body

Which stories deliver each step, and in what order?

The user stories that hang below each step: the details, options and alternatives for doing it, arranged top to bottom by priority. Lines across the body cut it into release slices.

In the map, Enter email gets email and password login in Release 1 and Google sign-in in Release 2. Apple sign-in or a one-time email link could come in a later slice.

Below the backbone · sliced into releases

What counts as the backbone

Activities and the user steps under them together form the backbone: the top two rows, the spine the rest of the map hangs from. Activities sit at the top of the backbone, not above it. The backbone runs in journey order and priority sorts only the body. If you score stories with a framework such as RICE, the scores order the stories within each column and leave the backbone alone.

Mapping Larkwell’s piano onboarding

From a list of user steps to three release slices, each with its own success metric

Worked example

A new learner’s first session with Larkwell

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), listens through the phone’s microphone or a MIDI keyboard and gives feedback on each note. Today 45% of new accounts activate, meaning they complete a first song lesson within 24 hours of signing up, and 19% are still active on day 7. The onboarding team is rebuilding the first session as a new flow, the “first song in five minutes” redesign, and maps it from scratch to decide what ships first. Even parts Larkwell already has, such as its store listing and note feedback, go in the slice where the new flow first uses them.

Goal
“Start learning piano with Larkwell and play a first song in the first session”
Persona
An adult beginner, 35, curious, with no music background, who downloaded the app after seeing a social media ad

  1. List the user steps

    Walk through the journey one step at a time. What does the user do, from first touch to the first moment of value?

    Download app → Open app → Create account → Set up instrument → Take placement quiz → Start first lesson → Complete first song → See results

  2. Group them into activities

    Cluster the eight steps into four activities. Together the two rows are the backbone.

    Four activities and the user steps inside them
    ActivityUser steps
    DiscoverDownload app
    OnboardOpen app → Create account → Set up instrument → Take placement quiz
    First lessonStart first lesson → Complete first song
    ReviewSee results

  3. Fill the body

    Under each step, brainstorm every way it could be implemented, from simplest to most sophisticated.

    Under “Create account”:

    • Email and password (simplest)
    • Google sign-in
    • Apple sign-in
    • Skip sign-up, so people can try before committing

    Under “Set up instrument”:

    • Choose microphone or MIDI keyboard (simplest)
    • An instrument check that confirms the app can hear the keys

    Under “Start first lesson”:

    • Play-along with visual cues (simplest)
    • Real-time feedback on each note
    • A video demonstration before each section
    • AI tempo adjustment

  4. Slice into releases

    Draw three lines across the body, one under each release, and give every slice a metric that says whether it worked.

    Three release slices for Larkwell’s onboarding
    ReleaseBuild timeWhat the user gets, left to rightSuccess metric
    Release 1: walking skeleton4 weeksApp store listing, a simple welcome, email sign-up, a choice of microphone or MIDI keyboard, quiz skipped, one guided play-along lesson, a “Lesson complete” screenA new user can sign up and finish one lesson
    Release 2: lovable6 weeksApp store screenshots and preview, animated onboarding, Google sign-in, an instrument check, three-question quiz, five starter lessons with real-time note feedback, progress dashboardActivation of at least 50% and D7 retention of at least 21%
    Release 3: delightful8 weeksReferral sharing, Apple sign-in, an option to skip sign-up, detailed quiz, AI tempo adjustment, video demos, streaks and achievements, social sharingActivation of 55% and D7 retention of 24%

Release 1 proves the journey works; Releases 2 and 3 move activation and retention

After four weeks a new learner can get from the app store to a finished lesson. Instrument setup is a single choice between microphone and MIDI keyboard and the placement quiz is skipped, which is as thin as those steps can get, but neither step is missing.

The next two releases add an instrument check, note feedback in the new lessons, a three-question quiz and streaks to steps that already work, so no release ships a dead end.

Only then do the numbers have to move. Release 2 needs activation to go from 45% to at least 50% and D7 retention from 19% to at least 21%. Release 3 aims for 55% and 24%, the onboarding team’s targets for the quarter.

A map pays off for multi-step journeys a team must agree on

Use it when

  • You are planning a new product or feature and need to see the full journey before cutting scope
  • Your backlog has lost its story: the team ships features but cannot explain the user journey
  • You are planning releases and need to decide what goes in Release 1, 2 and 3
  • You are aligning a cross-functional team, so designers, engineers and PMs see the same picture
  • You are in a PM interview and want to show structured product thinking when asked to plan a feature

Skip it when

  • It is a quick bug fix. A broken button does not need a map.
  • The work is a single feature that is small and isolated
  • You are optimizing, such as A/B testing within an existing flow rather than redesigning it
  • You do not understand the user yet. Research with Jobs to Be Done or design thinking first, then map. Five or six quick JTBD conversations on the riskiest assumption are a reasonable starting point and give the map a user to follow.
  • You are a team of one. Most of a map’s value is the shared picture the team builds while making it; working alone, a simple list may be enough.

Six ways a story map goes wrong

Most of them come from losing the user’s point of view or the team’s conversation

  1. Mapping features instead of user steps

    The backbone should describe what the user does, not what the system does. “User authentication module” is the system’s view; “Create account” is the user’s step.

    Fix: start every user-step card with a verb from the user’s perspective: “Search for…”, “Choose a…”, “Review my…”.

  2. Too many user steps

    If the backbone has 25 or more user steps, you are at the wrong level of detail and the map becomes unreadable. Aim for about 8–15 across the backbone.

    Fix: group related steps under activities, and merge steps that are really one. If “Enter email” and “Confirm password” are separate cards, combine them into “Create account”.

  3. Release 1 goes deep on one step

    Release 1 becomes “perfect login” instead of “a thin path from discovery to value”: one feature done completely before the rest of the journey exists. Users can finish that step and then have nowhere to go next.

    Fix: read the top slice from left to right. Can a real user get from the start to value? If any column is empty in Release 1, that is a dead end.

  4. Mapping alone

    Story mapping is a team activity. A PM who builds the map alone and then presents it defeats the purpose. Patton’s point is that the shared understanding built while making the map matters more than the map itself.

    Fix: always build the map with at least one designer and one engineer in the room.

  5. Mapping it once and forgetting it

    The map is made in a workshop, photographed and never touched again. Two sprints later the backlog has drifted and the map is out of date.

    Fix: keep the map in a digital tool such as Miro, FigJam or Notion. Refer to it in sprint planning (“Where does this story sit on the map?”) and update it as you learn.

  6. No metric for each release

    Every release slice needs a success metric. Without one, you cannot tell whether the release delivered value to users.

    Fix: write a hypothesis for each slice. In the Larkwell example: “Release 2 validates that a smoother setup and real-time note feedback get more new learners to a first song. Metric: activation of at least 50% and D7 retention of at least 21%.”

Running a two-hour mapping workshop

People (4–8)
PM, designer, 1–2 engineers and a customer-facing person from support or sales
Materials
A large wall or a Miro board, sticky notes in four colors, markers and a timer
Prepare
Bring user research: interviews, data and JTBD insights. Share a context document 24 hours before.
Mindset
This is collaborative storytelling, not backlog grooming
  1. 0–10 min

    Frame the goal

    State the user goal and persona and write them on a large card at the top of the board, so everyone agrees who you are mapping for and what they want. “Today we’re mapping the journey of [persona] trying to [goal].”

  2. 10–30 min

    Lay out the user steps

    Walk the journey step by step. Everyone writes steps on sticky notes; arrange them left to right, debate the order and merge duplicates. Then read the row aloud, “First they…, then they…”, and keep adjusting until it tells a complete story.

  3. 30–40 min

    Group into activities

    Cluster the steps into activities and place the activity cards in a row above them, like chapter headings, to complete the backbone. Consider splitting a cluster with six or more steps, and merging a single-step cluster into its neighbor unless it is a distinct phase of the journey.

  4. 40–70 min

    Fill the body

    10 minutes of silent writing: everyone writes stories for the different ways each step could be built, from simplest to most ambitious, and sticks them under the right step. Then 20 minutes of discussion, column by column: read, clarify, cluster duplicates and order each column by priority, with the simplest, most essential story at the top.

  5. 70–80 min

    Break

    Let it settle and photograph the current state. People often have new ideas during the break.

  6. 80–100 min

    Slice releases

    Draw a horizontal line under the top row of stories to mark Release 1, the walking skeleton. Where a column has nothing above the line, move the simplest story for that step up. Repeat for Release 2 and Release 3.

  7. 100–115 min

    Add metrics and hypotheses

    For each slice, write “Release 1 validates that [assumption]. Success metric: [specific number].” and attach it to the release line, so each slice carries a hypothesis the team can test.

  8. 115–120 min

    Wrap up

    Photograph the map and assign an owner to digitize it in Miro or FigJam. Agree on the next step (“We’ll use this map in sprint planning from [date]”) and schedule a follow-up in two weeks to review and update it.

Keep the room moving

Give any single card a two-minute timebox. If it is still unresolved, move it to a parking lot and carry on. The map is a living document, and you will come back to it.

Test yourself on story mapping

  1. What do the two axes of a story map represent?

    Reading across follows what the user does, in order; reading down goes from the most essential version of each step to the ones that can wait. Time does flow left to right, but as the order of the user’s actions rather than a schedule, and team size appears nowhere on the map.

  2. What is the walking skeleton?

    Release 1 gives every step its simplest story, so a user can get from the start to value even though nothing is polished. A bare backbone isn’t it: with no stories under the steps, there is nothing to ship.

  3. As a rule of thumb, how many activities does a story map have?

    Three to seven activities is enough to tell the journey as a handful of chapters and few enough to keep in your head. Ten to fifteen is closer to the count for the row beneath them: 8–15 user steps.

  4. A team’s Release 1 plan has email, Google and Apple sign-in plus a password reset flow, and nothing yet under the lesson and results steps. What is wrong with it?

    Every sign-in option sits under one step, so the plan is deep in one column and empty in the others, and a new user hits a dead end right after signing up. The backbone may be fine; the fault is in how the first slice was cut.

  5. Your first draft of a backbone has 27 user steps, and nobody in the room can read it as one story. What should you do?

    At 27 steps the backbone is too detailed, and clustering steps under activities, or merging pairs like “Enter email” and “Confirm password”, brings it back to a row people can read. Moving steps into the body would bury things the user actually does, which is what the backbone is there to show.

  6. Who should attend a story mapping session?

    A group of four to eight drawn from product, design, engineering and support or sales spots gaps in the journey that any one role would miss. A PM presenting a finished map skips the conversation that gives the team a shared picture of the journey.

  7. After the workshop, a team scores every story in the body with RICE. How should the scores change the map?

    RICE can rank the stories under a step, so its scores decide which one sits highest in each column. Reordering the activities by score would scramble the journey order the backbone exists to keep.

  8. What sits at the top of the backbone?

    The backbone is the two rows of activities and the user steps beneath them, so activities form its top edge. The goal frames the backbone from above and isn’t part of it.
