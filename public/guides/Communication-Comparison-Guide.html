The STAR Method, the Pyramid Principle, the narrative arc and the executive summary all help you get a message across, but each is built for a different situation: an interview answer, a quick update, a presentation that must persuade, a document that asks for a decision. This guide sets them side by side so you can pick the structure that fits.

In short

Choose by the situation. In an interview, use STAR for behavioral questions, the Pyramid Principle for case questions and the CIRCLES Method for product design questions. Outside interviews, the narrative arc wins over a room that must be persuaded, an executive summary gives leadership one page to decide from, and the pyramid covers everything else, including any case where you are unsure.

Four structures for four kinds of situation

Interviews, quick updates, persuasive presentations and decision documents

All four answer the same question: how do I get my message across? They differ by situation rather than by channel: STAR and the arc are both spoken, and the pyramid works spoken or written. STAR tells a story out loud. The Pyramid Principle leads with the answer. The narrative arc builds buy-in through tension. The executive summary puts a decision on one page.

S → T → A → R

STAR Method

“Here is a story that proves I can do this.”

STAR structures an answer to a behavioral interview question as a short story: the Situation, your Task, the Actions you took and the Result. Told in 90 seconds to 2 minutes, it ends with a Result you can measure.

Best for: behavioral interviews

Answer first

Pyramid Principle

“Here is my answer, and the three reasons behind it.”

The main point comes first, supported by a small group of ideas, each supported in turn by the ideas below it. The reader has the answer before any of the support and can stop as soon as they are convinced.

Best for: case interviews, email, Slack, and updates or readouts where the audience wants the answer

Tension, then release

Narrative Arc

“Here is why the way we work now cannot last, and what we do instead.”

Five beats: Status quo, Disruption, Struggle, Resolution and New reality. The arc builds tension before it offers the answer, so the audience feels the problem before it hears the solution.

Best for: presentations and pitches that must persuade

One page

Executive Summary

“Everything you need to decide, on one page.”

A short, decision-ready brief for leadership: Recommendation, Context, Key data, Options considered, Risks and mitigations, and the Ask and next steps. It is written last and read first.

Best for: documents leadership decides from

The situation decides first, then the audience

Start with the situation: a behavioral interview calls for STAR, a case interview for the Pyramid Principle and a product design question for the CIRCLES Method. Outside interviews, ask whether you are speaking or writing. Spoken to a room that must be persuaded, use the narrative arc; to one that wants the answer, use the pyramid. Written for leadership to decide from, use an executive summary; for any other writing, use the pyramid.

The Pyramid Principle is often summed up as top-down deduction, which is only half right. Its governing principle, set out in Barbara Minto’s book The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking, is to start with the answer and group the support beneath it. That support can be deductive (a chain of reasoning) or inductive (a group of ideas of the same kind), and Minto notes that inductive groups are usually easier for a reader to follow.

Each position is a bet about the audience

Answer first bets that the reader is busy, will accept the answer and wants the support only to check it. Holding the answer back bets that the listener cannot weigh it yet: an interviewer needs the context to believe the Result, and a skeptical room needs to feel the cost of the status quo before a proposal sounds like relief. Lose the bet and it costs you either way. A buried answer wastes a busy reader’s time, and an early answer to a skeptical room draws the objections before you have made your case.

Start from the situation, then the audience

Follow the path until it names a framework

  1. Are you answering an interview question?

    • BehavioralUse STAR. A “Tell me about a time…” question wants a story of 90 seconds to 2 minutes, with numbers in the Result.
    • CaseUse the Pyramid Principle. Give your conclusion first, then the grouped reasons.
    • Product designUse the CIRCLES Method. A question that asks you to design or improve a product needs its seven steps, worked through in order, not a story or an answer-first argument.
    • NoGo to question 2

  2. Will you be speaking to the audience, or will they read what you send?

    • SpeakingGo to question 3
    • WritingGo to question 4

  3. Must the audience be persuaded before it will accept your answer?

    • YesUse the narrative arc. Build the tension, then resolve it with your proposal. Send an executive summary after the meeting.
    • NoUse the Pyramid Principle. The audience already agrees or wants the answer, so give it first.

  4. Is it a formal document that leadership will use to make a decision?

    • YesUse an executive summary. One page that someone can decide from without you in the room.
    • NoUse the Pyramid Principle. An email, a Slack message or a quick update should open with the answer.

When unsure, default to the pyramid

If you cannot tell whether the audience needs persuading, lead with the answer: it costs them less time, and you can build the case if they push back. The “So what?” test is how you check. Would your recommendation alone make them act? If yes, lead with it. If they need to feel the problem first, use the arc.

Answer first still allows a little setup

When a listener needs context before the answer makes sense, give it a sentence or two of SCQA: the Situation they already know, the Complication that changed it, the Question it raises in their mind, then the Answer. Keep the first three brief, and give the Answer before any of the support.

If the path does not fit your case, look for a similar one in the scenarios, or answer one or two questions in the picker. The picker follows the same rules as this path.

Five situations and the structure for each

Find the one closest to yours

  • “Tell me about a time you led through conflict.”

    A behavioral interview question. You need a tight, memorable answer that proves the competency with evidence.

    STAR: a story of 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Set the Situation and your Task, spend about half the answer on the Actions you took, and end with a Result you can put a number on.

  • “We need to present a roadmap pivot to a leadership team that is skeptical of it.”

    They are attached to the current plan. They need to feel why the status quo is failing before you show them the new direction.

    Narrative arc: Status quo, Disruption, Struggle (the peak of tension), Resolution (the pivot), New reality. Put your strongest proof at the Resolution.

  • “Should we ship on Friday or slip a week? I need to post the answer in Slack.”

    A quick decision made asynchronously. People are scanning, and burying the answer wastes everyone’s time.

    Pyramid Principle: lead with the recommendation. “Slip a week, for three reasons: a gap in QA, an open P0 bug and the holiday code freeze.” Put the detail underneath for anyone who wants it.

  • “I need a one-page proposal for a new initiative, and it goes to the executive team.”

    Leadership will read it cold, decide on it, and never see your slides. The page has to stand on its own.

    Executive summary: the Recommendation first, then Context, Key data, Options considered, Risks and mitigations, and the Ask and next steps. Write it last, after the analysis; it is the part that gets read first.

  • “I’m giving a big all-hands pitch, and people need a document to take away from it.”

    You are presenting live, and you need something that keeps working once you have left the room.

    Narrative arc and an executive summary: present with the arc to win the room, then send the one-page summary so the decision holds after the meeting.

Most real work uses two of them

Four pairings that cover the common cases

Each framework covers one part of the job, so pairs are common: usually one structure for the conversation and one for what people read afterward.

A presentation and its follow-up

Present with the narrative arc in the meeting, then send an executive summary as the follow-up. The spoken story wins the room and the written page carries the decision forward.

Interview preparation

Prepare 6–8 STAR stories for the behavioral rounds. In case interviews, structure your analysis with the Pyramid Principle: the conclusion first, then the grouped reasons. Product design questions take the CIRCLES Method instead.

Inside an executive summary

An executive summary is the Pyramid Principle in document form, with Options and Risks added. Because the recommendation comes first, Context is a compressed Situation and Complication of one or two sentences: the Question is implied, and the Answer is already the Recommendation. Group the Key data under the reasons it supports.

Portfolio case studies

Write each project as a STAR story under an executive summary header. Recruiters scan the summary; interviewers hear the story.

In PM interviews, name the structure out loud

You rarely need all four at once. Saying which structure you are using shows that you chose it on purpose, and it tells the interviewer what to listen for: “I’ll answer this as a STAR story,” or “Let me give you the headline first, then the reasoning.”
