RICE gives every product idea a single score from four estimates: how many people it reaches, how much it changes things for each of them, how sure you are, and how much work it takes. Rank by the score and use the order to start the roadmap discussion.

In short

Multiply how many users a feature reaches by how much each one benefits and by how confident you are in those numbers, then divide by the total effort. The higher the score, the stronger the case for building it soon.

Four factors, one score

Three multiply the value of an idea and one divides it

RICE scores an idea as Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Reach is how many people the idea affects in a fixed period, Impact is how much it changes things for each of them, Confidence is how sure you are of the other estimates, and Effort is the total work it takes. Sean McBride introduced RICE in “RICE: Simple prioritization for product managers” on the Intercom blog, describing how the product team there ranked its ideas.

R

Reach

How many people will this touch?

An estimated count of the users who will meet the feature in a set period, usually a quarter. Base it on analytics rather than guessing, and use the same period for every feature you compare.

Users per quarter · multiplies the score

I

Impact

How much will each person benefit?

How strongly the feature changes things for each user who meets it. It is the most subjective factor, so everyone scores it on the same fixed scale.

0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 or 3 · multiplies the score

C

Confidence

How sure are we about these numbers?

The honesty check. A result from an A/B test earns 100%; a hunch earns 50%. It stops a well-argued guess from outranking an idea you have researched.

50%, 80% or 100% · multiplies the score

E

Effort

How much total work will this take?

Person-months across design, engineering, QA and documentation. It is the only divisor, so more effort lowers the score. Count every discipline, not only engineering.

Person-months · divides the score

A return-on-investment estimate

Reach × Impact × Confidence is the expected value: how many people, how much, how sure. Dividing by Effort turns that value into a return on the work you would put in.

Scoring each factor

Impact and Confidence use fixed values so a team scores the same way

Reach

Reach is an estimated count for a stated period, not a choice from a list. Use the number of users your analytics show for the period. If you have no data for an idea, the bands below give rough orders of magnitude; they are never required values.

  1. 50,000+EveryoneAll active users see it
  2. 10,000BroadMost active users
  3. 1,000SegmentOne user group
  4. 100NichePower users only

When an idea's Reach rests on a band rather than data, lower its Confidence to make up for it.

Impact

Pick one of five multipliers. Everyone on the team uses the same five.

  1. 3×MassiveChanges how people use the product
  2. 2×HighA major improvement
  3. 1×MediumA clear improvement
  4. 0.5×LowHelps a little
  5. 0.25×MinimalBarely noticed

The 20% rule

Most features are 1× or less. As a rule of thumb, if more than one in five of your features score 2× or 3×, the team is inflating Impact.

Confidence

Score how much evidence sits behind your Reach and Impact estimates. Be strict: this factor exists to discount optimism.

  1. 100%HighData behind every estimate, such as usage analytics or an A/B test result
  2. 80%MediumData behind some estimates, such as a survey or consistent customer feedback
  3. 50%LowGut feel: “I think users want this”

When you are unsure between two values, take the lower one. Overconfidence is a common way for RICE scores to go wrong.

Effort

Estimate the total person-months, adding up design, engineering, QA and documentation. These anchors are RICE's own; other scoring frameworks use different effort scales, and the scales do not convert.

  1. 6+LargeA full squad for two months or more
  2. 3MediumA small team for about six weeks
  3. 1SmallOne developer for a month
  4. 0.5TinyOne developer for two weeks

When you are unsure, round Effort up. Estimates often miss work outside engineering.

What the scores tell us

Smart Shuffle wins. It has wide reach, high impact, reasonable confidence and moderate effort: 40,000 × 2 × 80% ÷ 2 = 32,000, the best return of the three.

Social Playlists has the highest impact but ranks second. Its 50% confidence is a gut feel, and that halves the score. A survey that raised confidence to 80% would lift it to 12,000, still behind Smart Shuffle.

Lyrics Display reaches everyone but ranks last. Wide reach cannot make up for low impact and six person-months of work: 50,000 × 0.5 × 100% ÷ 6 is about 4,167.

Three experiments to try

  1. Set Confidence to 50%. The score drops from 2,000 to 1,250, more than a third: that is the cost of a guess.
  2. Double Effort from 2 to 4. The score halves.
  3. Set Impact to 3×. The score triples, which is why teams are tempted to inflate it.

    The scores give you an order to discuss, not a decision.

When RICE fits, and when it does not

It needs data and a long enough list to be worth the setup

Use it when

  • You have ten or more competing ideas to rank
  • Stakeholders disagree and you need a shared, explicit method
  • You have analytics data to estimate Reach
  • You are planning a quarterly roadmap across teams
  • You want to reduce decisions made on gut feel

Skip it when

  • The product is new and has no usage data to estimate Reach. Use ICE until it does.
  • You need an answer today. Use ICE or Value vs Effort instead.
  • The items are not comparable, such as infrastructure work against features
  • The team is small enough to decide in ten minutes
  • People are gaming the scores instead of debating them

The most rigorous way to rank, and one of the slower ones

Because Reach comes from usage data, RICE is the most rigorous of the prioritization frameworks for ranking. From fastest to slowest, they run ICE, Value vs Effort, RICE, then the Kano model, which gathers the most direct user evidence but classifies features rather than ranking them. A common sequence: ICE or Value vs Effort cuts 30 ideas to about 10, RICE ranks the shortlist, and Kano runs alongside when you need to know how a feature affects satisfaction. Choosing a prioritization framework sets the four side by side.

Six ways RICE goes wrong

The formula is simple; scoring honestly is the hard part

  1. Inflating Impact

    Everyone believes their own feature deserves 3×. If more than 20% of features score 2× or 3×, the scale has stopped meaning anything.

    Fix: agree on a distribution in advance: one or two features at 3×, most at 0.5× or 1×.

  2. Guessing Reach

    Reach is the one factor RICE expects you to base on data. Guessing it removes the method’s main advantage.

    Fix: pull Reach from analytics. If one idea has no data behind its Reach, set its Confidence to 50%; if most ideas have none, use a faster framework until data exists.

  3. Mixing time periods

    Reach per month for one feature and per year for another makes the comparison meaningless.

    Fix: use one period, usually a quarter, for every feature in the batch.

  4. Underestimating Effort

    A common mistake is to count only engineering time, while design, QA, documentation and migration can take months.

    Fix: ask each discipline for its own estimate, add them up and round up.

  5. Scoring alone

    One person’s scores carry one person’s biases. RICE works when a group calibrates together.

    Fix: score as a team. Where estimates are more than one step apart on a factor's scale, you have found the conversation worth having.

  6. Treating scores as final

    RICE cannot see strategy, dependencies or team morale. Sometimes a lower-scoring item is the right call.

    Fix: use the ranking to set the order of discussion, not to approve the roadmap automatically.

Running a 90-minute scoring session

Who to invite, how to spend the time and what to do with the ranking

Must attend
PM (facilitates), engineering lead, design lead
Useful to have
Data analyst, QA lead, a stakeholder who observes
Prepare
A shared spreadsheet with every candidate feature listed and the agreed period for Reach
  1. 0–10 min

    Set the stage

    Explain RICE, share the spreadsheet, and agree on the period and the goal.

  2. 10–20 min

    Score Reach

    The data analyst leads, feature by feature: how many users per quarter?

  3. 20–40 min

    Score Impact and Confidence

    Each person writes down a value from the factor's own scale (0.25 to 3 for Impact; 50%, 80% or 100% for Confidence), then everyone reveals. Discuss any factor where estimates are more than one step apart on that scale.

  4. 40–55 min

    Score Effort

    The engineering lead drives. The PM stays out of it and lets the people building it estimate.

  5. 55–70 min

    Calculate and react

    Sort live, give two minutes of silent reading, then ask what surprises people.

  6. 70–90 min

    Debate and decide

    Discuss the top five, apply strategic filters, record overrides and commit, following the steps below.

Three facilitation habits

  • Write estimates down first so the loudest voice does not anchor the room.
  • Timebox each debate to three minutes per feature, and park anything that stalls.
  • Capture the reasoning in a notes column; you will want it next quarter.

From a ranked list to a roadmap

Five steps sit between sorting the scores and committing to work.

  1. Sort and review

    Sort from highest to lowest and read the list before reacting. Does the order look roughly right?

    Ask: if the CEO saw the top five, would anything look wrong?

  2. Apply strategic filters

    Check the top ten against company goals, dependencies and risks to retention.

  3. Record every override

    A lower-scoring item can move up the queue, as long as you write down why.

    A rule of thumb: if you override more than 20% of the ranking, recalibrate your inputs.

  4. Check capacity

    Compare the total Effort against the person-months you actually have, and draw the cut line.

  5. Commit and communicate

    Commit to two to four items. Share the whole list, including what you said no to and why.

    Keep the spreadsheet. Next quarter, compare your estimates with what happened.

Check your understanding

Six questions on the formula and how to use it

  1. Where does Effort sit in the RICE formula?

    Effort is the only divisor: Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. The more work a feature takes, the lower its score, which is what makes the result a return on the work.

  2. Reach 10,000 × Impact 2 × Confidence 50% ÷ Effort 2 = ?

    10,000 × 2 × 50% ÷ 2 = 5,000. Leaving out Confidence or Effort gives 10,000; leaving out both gives 20,000. Low confidence halves the score.

  3. Halfway through a scoring session, seven of the ten features have an Impact of 2× or 3×. What is the most likely explanation?

    Most features are 1×, a clear improvement, or less. When most of a list sits at 2× or 3×, the scale has stopped separating ideas, so agree on a distribution or an anchor feature for each value and rescore. Effort is a separate estimate and does not explain a skewed Impact column.

  4. Your team has just sorted 15 features by RICE score. What comes next?

    The ranking starts the discussion. Check the top of the list against goals, dependencies and the person-months you have, record any overrides, then commit and share the whole list.

  5. A team launches a new app next month. It has no usage data and twelve ideas to rank this week. What fits best?

    Without data, every Reach is a guess, and RICE loses its advantage over faster methods. The bands are only rough orders of magnitude, and 100% Confidence needs strong evidence such as an A/B test.

  6. The Reach and Impact estimates for a feature come from a customer survey, not from a test. What Confidence should it get?

    A survey puts data behind the estimates without testing them, which is 80%. 100% needs data behind every estimate, such as usage analytics or an A/B test result, and 50% is for gut feel with no evidence behind it.
