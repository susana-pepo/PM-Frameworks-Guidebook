The North Star Framework pairs one metric, the North Star Metric, with 3–5 input metrics that teams can influence directly. The North Star Metric is the single metric that best captures the core value customers get from the product, so it gives every team the same number to aim at.

In short

Pick one metric that counts the value customers get from your product within a time window, such as Weekly Active Learners: learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week. Break it into 3–5 input metrics, each owned by a team. When the inputs improve, the North Star Metric rises, and revenue follows.

One number for the value customers get

A North Star Metric, plus the inputs that teams pull to move it

Most teams track dozens of metrics: DAU, MAU, revenue, NPS, conversion rate, session time, churn, activation rate. When every number is a priority, teams pull in different directions. Engineering improves page speed, marketing maximizes sign-ups, product chases engagement, and nobody is working toward the same result.

The North Star Metric (NSM) fixes this with one number that captures the value you deliver to customers. When it goes up, the business is healthy. When it goes down, you have a problem. Everyone knows it, and every team can say how its work moves it.

One fixed point to steer by

Sailors did not steer by fifty stars; they needed one fixed point to orient everything else. The NSM is the one point the whole team navigates by.

Growth advisor Sean Ellis popularized the term, including in Hacking Growth, the 2017 book he wrote with Morgan Brown. Amplitude later turned it into a working method in its North Star Playbook, written by John Cutler and colleagues, which adds the input metrics, the standard criteria for a good metric and the three “games” covered in the next section.

Weak NSM: revenue

Revenue is an output. Raising prices, cutting costs or making cancellation harder can lift revenue or profit in the short term without delivering any more value. Revenue alone does not tell you whether customers are getting what they came for.

Strong NSM: Weekly Active Learners

For a learning app, a learner who completes at least 3 lessons in a calendar week is learning and building a habit. If the number of those learners goes up, more of them renew, and revenue follows.

The inputs underneath

No team can move the NSM directly. Each team moves an input metric: a controllable, customer-facing metric that, when it improves, lifts the NSM. An input is always a metric, never a task or a project. A good tree has 3–5 inputs, and each team owns one or two of them. Amplitude’s playbook groups inputs into four kinds.

1

Breadth

How many customers reach the value?

New users who reach the value moment for the first time, or the share of accounts using a key feature.

2

Depth

How much value does each one get?

How far customers go once they arrive: more content used, more features adopted, more of the job done in your product.

3

Frequency

How often do they come back for it?

Days active per week, sessions per month, repeat purchases. Frequency is how a habit shows up in the data.

4

Efficiency

How quickly and smoothly do they get it?

Time to first value, load time, or the share of started tasks that are finished. Friction lowers every other input.

Leading for revenue, lagging for its inputs

The NSM is a leading indicator of revenue and long-term business results: it moves first, and they follow. Relative to its own inputs it is an output, so it moves after they do. Both statements are true. That is why teams set goals on the inputs and watch the NSM to check that the inputs were the right ones.

What makes a good North Star Metric

Seven criteria, three kinds of value and some commonly cited examples

Seven criteria from the playbook

Amplitude’s playbook lists seven properties a North Star Metric should have. These are the standard criteria. A candidate that fails one of them will mislead the team sooner or later.

Expresses customer value
It measures value delivered to customers, not value captured from them. “Lessons completed” is value delivered; “subscription revenue” is value captured.
Represents vision and strategy
It reflects the product you are trying to build and the customers you have chosen to serve, so improving it means moving toward the vision.
Leading indicator of business results
When it goes up, revenue and other business results eventually follow. A metric with no link to business results cannot tell you whether the business will follow, however good it looks.
Actionable
Teams can influence it through 3–5 input metrics they control. If you cannot break it into levers, nobody can move it.
Understandable
Anyone in the company can explain it in one sentence, including what counts and what does not.
Measurable
You can track it every week. “User happiness” is not measurable; “learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week” is.
Not a vanity metric
It cannot rise unless customers get more value, so it is hard to game. Total pageviews are easy to inflate; learners who complete at least 3 lessons a week are not.

Which game your product plays

Amplitude also sorts products by the kind of value customers get from them. Knowing which game you play narrows the candidates quickly.

Attention

How much time do customers choose to spend with you?

Value grows with time spent: listening, watching, reading, scrolling.

Media, streaming, social networks

Transaction

How many transactions do customers complete?

Value is delivered each time something is bought, booked or sold.

Marketplaces, e-commerce, travel

Productivity

How efficiently do customers get their work done?

Value is work completed with less effort, not time spent in the tool.

Collaboration and business software

Finding your North Star Metric in three steps

Start from the moment a customer gets value, then count it

  1. Define the core value

    Find the moment a customer gets what they came for. For Larkwell, the fictional piano-learning app in the worked example, it is playing a recognizable song. For a ride-hailing app, it is arriving at your destination. For a project-management tool, it is getting a project organized. This is the value moment.

    Ask: what moment makes a customer say “this is worth it”?

  2. Make it measurable

    Turn the value moment into something you can count, with a time window. Larkwell’s first draft is “songs completed per week”, not just “songs completed”: the window turns a running total into a trend you can track and react to each week. It is still a first draft, because a few heavy users could carry the number. The worked example sharpens it into a count of learners: Weekly Active Learners, learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week.

  3. Identify 3–5 input metrics

    List the levers teams can pull to move the NSM. These become each team’s focus. At Larkwell, the onboarding team owns median time to first note, the content team owns quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, and the growth team owns new activated learners per week.

Test the candidate with four questions

The four questions below are this guide’s shortcut for testing a candidate in the room, not part of the playbook. They compress the seven criteria; use the full list before you commit. If any answer is no, keep looking.

Value

If this metric doubles, are customers really getting more value?

Revenue

If this metric doubles, will revenue eventually follow?

Gaming

Is it hard to inflate without delivering value?

Total pageviews are easy to game. Learners who complete at least 3 lessons a week are hard to game.

Levers

Can you break it into 3–5 inputs that teams control directly?

The right metric changes as the product matures

The right metric depends on what the business most needs to prove. Expect to change it, and review it every 6–12 months.

  1. Before and just after product-market fitActivation as a provisional focus metric, such as new users who complete a first lesson
  2. Growth stageEngagement, such as Weekly Active Learners
  3. MatureValue metrics tied more closely to retention and monetization

Before product-market fit, the metric will move

Until you have product-market fit, the value moment itself is still changing, so the NSM is likely to change with it. A team can use activation as a provisional focus metric, labeled as provisional, watch retention as the sign of fit, and set the NSM once it knows what customers keep coming back for.

Choosing Larkwell’s North Star Metric and its inputs

One metric, five inputs, five owners

Worked example

A North Star Metric for a piano-learning app

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults, defines its NSM as Weekly Active Learners (WAL): learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week. Larkwell is a fictional company; all figures are illustrative. Today 40,000 learners meet that bar.

Completing at least 3 lessons a week means a learner is getting value (learning happens), building a habit and progressing, and it predicts renewal. Counting learners who cross that line, rather than total lessons, stops a few heavy users from carrying the number. It passes the four questions: value (learning happens), revenue (learners who keep practicing renew), gaming (it takes real lessons to move it) and levers (it breaks into the five inputs below).

Why D7 retention is not an input

Teams often list D7 retention, the share of a sign-up cohort that is active on day 7, as an input. Retention is an output metric: no team can change it directly, only by moving the inputs. In the tree it sits between the inputs and the NSM as an intermediate output, and no team owns it as an input. Track it beside the NSM, and put the behavior that drives it, practice sessions, in the tree.

Turning it into OKRs

The NSM is a long-lived metric; OKRs are set each quarter. The Objective stays qualitative. The NSM, or one of its inputs, appears as a key result, written from a starting value to a target. The NSM is never the Objective, and the projects that move it are never key results. Larkwell’s company OKR for Q3 shows the pattern.

Objective
Make Larkwell the app adult beginners keep playing with

The onboarding team’s changes in Sprint 14, such as skipping instrument setup for the first lesson. They are work that should move an input, not key results.

How the inputs add up

More activated learners (breadth) who reach a first note quickly and finish the lessons they start (efficiency), find a steady supply of quality beginner lessons (depth) and come back to practice (frequency) produce more Weekly Active Learners. Each team has one clear lever, and every team can see how its lever moves the shared number.

    When a North Star Metric helps, and when it does not

    It aligns teams over the long term; it does not diagnose problems or rank features

    Use it when

    • Cross-functional teams need to align on one goal
    • You are setting OKRs and quarterly goals
    • An interviewer asks how you would measure success
    • You are building a product analytics dashboard
    • Annual planning needs one number the whole company aims at

    Skip it when

    • You have not reached product-market fit, and the value moment is still changing. Use activation as a provisional focus metric, labeled as provisional, instead.
    • You need to find where growth is stuck. Use AARRR (pirate metrics) instead.
    • You are prioritizing features. Use RICE or ICE instead.
    • You run several products with different value propositions. Set one NSM per product instead of forcing a shared one.
    • You are making a short-term project decision. The NSM is a long-term measure.

    In a growth plan, the North Star Metric usually comes second: AARRR finds the leak, and the NSM gives every team the number that fixing it should move. Choosing a growth framework shows how the four growth frameworks fit together.

    Six ways a North Star Metric goes wrong

    Most of them count activity or outcomes that can rise without customers getting more value

    1. Revenue or MRR as the NSM

      Revenue measures value captured, not value delivered. You can raise prices without delivering more value, and the number goes up anyway.

      Fix: choose a metric of value delivered, such as lessons completed by active learners, and let revenue follow as the business result it predicts.

    2. A vanity count such as total sign-ups

      Total sign-ups keep rising while users leave. 1,000,000 sign-ups with 5% retention means 950,000 people who left.

      Fix: count a value event in a time window: what happens after sign-up, not the sign-up itself.

    3. A metric you cannot read weekly, such as NPS

      NPS is survey-based, lags behavior and is too noisy week to week to steer by. It is also easy to influence through when and whom you ask.

      Fix: keep it as a supporting metric and choose a behavioral metric you can track every week.

    4. Defaulting to DAU

      Daily active users is too vague for most products. It suits products where daily attention is the value, such as social networks and messaging, which is why it appears among the commonly cited examples. In a learning app, a user who opens a lesson and closes it counts as active without getting any value.

      Fix: count active users who reach the value moment, such as learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week.

    5. An NSM without owned inputs

      A single number without levers tells teams what to watch, not what to do. An input without an owner is no better: it is a number on a dashboard, not a lever, and if nobody is responsible for moving it, the NSM stalls.

      Fix: break the NSM into 3–5 input metrics and give every input a named team, with each team owning one or two inputs. Input and Output Metrics shows how to pair each input with a counter-metric.

    6. Making the NSM the Objective

      An OKR Objective with a number in it is really a key result. Writing “Grow Weekly Active Learners to 50,000” as the Objective leaves nothing qualitative to explain why the number matters.

      Fix: keep the Objective qualitative and use the NSM, or one of its inputs, as one of its key results.

    Check your understanding

    Eight questions on choosing and using a North Star Metric

    1. What is a North Star Metric?

      The NSM counts value delivered to customers, not revenue, sign-ups or whichever KPI was in focus last quarter. When it goes up, customers are getting more value and the business is healthy.

    2. Why is revenue a weak North Star Metric?

      Revenue measures value captured from customers. Raising prices or making cancellation harder can lift it while customers get nothing more. The NSM should measure value delivered, which leads revenue.

    3. What are input metrics?

      Input metrics are the levers: controllable, customer-facing metrics, never tasks or projects. Each team owns one or two, and when the inputs improve, the NSM moves.

    4. A growth team proposes D7 retention as one of the input metrics under its NSM, with itself as the owner. What is the problem?

      Retention cannot be changed directly by any team; it moves when inputs such as practice sessions or lesson completion improve. It sits between the inputs and the NSM as an intermediate output and is worth tracking, but no team owns it as an input. A longer window does not change that.

    5. Spotify’s commonly cited NSM is time spent listening. Why does it work?

      Listening is the value customers get from Spotify, and listeners who get more value stay subscribed. The metric expresses customer value and is a leading indicator of business results, two of the playbook’s criteria.

    6. A learning app’s candidate metric, app opens per week, doubles after a push-notification campaign, but learners complete no more lessons. Which criterion has it failed?

      The number rose without any more learning, so it fails the customer-value criterion, and the ease of inflating it makes it a vanity metric too. A team moved it with one campaign, and it has a weekly window, so actionable and measurable do not explain the problem.

    7. How does a North Star Metric connect to OKRs?

      An Objective describes the goal in words, such as “make Larkwell the app adult beginners keep playing with”. The NSM, or one of its inputs, gives a measurable key result, such as Weekly Active Learners (WAL) from 40,000 to 50,000. Projects, like the onboarding changes that cut time to first note, are initiatives, not key results.

    8. Airbnb’s commonly cited NSM is nights booked. Which game does that put it in?

      Airbnb delivers value each time a stay is booked, so it plays the transaction game. Attention products count time spent; productivity products count work done.
