The Value vs Effort matrix sorts a backlog by two judgments: how much each idea matters and how hard it is to build. On a 2×2 grid the ideas fall into four groups, and each group comes with a default next move.

In short

Rate each idea from 1 to 5 for value and for effort, plot it on the 2×2 and read the quadrant. Quick wins usually go first, Big bets get planned in phases, Fill-ins wait for spare capacity and Avoid items get a no. It is a fast first pass, not a precise ranking.

Four quadrants, four default moves

Where an idea lands suggests what to do with it

Every idea gets two scores. Value is how much it matters to users and the business. Effort is how much work it takes to ship. Crossing the two splits the backlog into four quadrants, numbered here in the order you would normally work through them.

1

Quick wins

High value, low effort

A large benefit for little work. Doing these first builds momentum and shows stakeholders results early.

Default move: build now, in the next sprint

2

Big bets

High value, high effort

Worth doing but expensive. Break them into phases, test the riskiest assumptions first and staff them properly.

Default move: plan and phase; put it on the roadmap without rushing

3

Fill-ins

Low value, low effort

Easy nice-to-haves that will not shift your key metrics. Do them when there is spare capacity, for example between larger projects, in a hackathon or as a starter task for a new teammate. Do not plan around them.

Default move: do it only when there is spare capacity

4

Avoid

Low value, high effort

The trap. Complexity can make these look important, but they consume a lot of capacity and return little. Some teams call them money pits or time sinks.

Default move: say no early, or revisit the assumptions behind the scores

Read the grid as a default order

Quick wins usually come first, then Big bets, then Fill-ins, with Avoid last. The quadrant suggests a next move, not a verdict: strategy, dependencies and deadlines can override it.

Scoring value and effort from 1 to 5

Two short scales, agreed before anyone starts scoring

Value: how much does this matter?

Fold user impact, business impact and strategic fit into one judgment. Before anyone scores, agree what value means for this round, such as retention, revenue or activation, so everyone scores against the same thing.

  1. 5CriticalUnblocks a key goal or changes a core metric
  2. 4HighMoves a key metric
  3. 3MediumA clear improvement
  4. 2LowSome users care
  5. 1MinimalNice to have, but nobody asked for it

The do-nothing test

Ask what happens if you do not build it. If the honest answer is “nothing much,” the idea is a 1 or a 2.

Effort: how hard is this to build?

Count design, engineering, QA and any dependencies on other teams, not only engineering time.

  1. 5MassiveMonths, across several teams
  2. 4LargeSeveral weeks to a month
  3. 3Medium1–2 weeks
  4. 2SmallA few days
  5. 1TinyA few hours

The effort scale is deliberately non-linear, while the value scale is linear: each effort step is several times the size of the one before. That suits a quick sort: people can reliably tell a day from a week, but not a seven-week job from an eight-week one. If your typical item is larger, shift the anchors, and agree them before scoring. They are for sorting, not costing, so they do not convert into the person-months that RICE uses.

The two scales point opposite ways. On value a high score is good; on effort a low score is. That is why Quick wins sit top-left.

Where the midline falls

A 4 or 5 counts as high and a 1 or 2 counts as low. A 3 sits on the midline, so the grid cannot place it for you: treat it as a border item and settle it in discussion. This also means a Quick win has to earn its place, with a Value of 4 or 5 and an Effort of 1 or 2.

Relative scores, not measurements

RICE asks for estimates in real units where it can, such as a Reach of 10,000 users per quarter or an Effort of 2 person-months. Value vs Effort uses relative 1–5 scores for both axes. That makes it faster and less rigorous: well suited to aligning a team quickly, not to detailed quarterly planning.

    When Value vs Effort fits, and when it does not

    A quick 2×2 is fast and visual, but too coarse for some decisions

    Use it when

    • You need to align a team in under an hour
    • You are grooming a backlog and want a quick sort of up to about 30 items
    • Stakeholders want a visual they can read at a glance
    • The product is early and has little data
    • You want a first pass before deeper analysis, such as RICE

    Skip it when

    • You need precise, data-backed rankings. Use RICE instead.
    • You are comparing many more than 30 items: the grid gets crowded, so cut the list with ICE first
    • You must show the evidence behind each rank, for example to justify budget: a 1–5 score records a judgment, not the data behind it
    • Effort estimates vary widely between team members; settle those first
    • The items are fundamentally different kinds of work, such as infrastructure against features

    A first pass before RICE

    Use Value vs Effort, or ICE, for the first pass: cut 30 ideas to about 10 strong candidates. Then rank that shortlist with RICE and take the top 3. The matrix does the quick cut; RICE does the careful ordering.

    Run the Kano model alongside when you need to know how a feature affects satisfaction; it is not a step between the first pass and RICE. At the end, the same grid is a clear way to present the result. Choosing a prioritization framework sets the four side by side.

    Six ways the grid misleads

    A simple method with subtle traps

    1. Everything lands in Quick wins

      If most of the backlog scores high value and low effort, the scoring is not honest. Value is being inflated, effort underestimated, or both.

      Fix: calibrate. Agree on one anchor item for value 1, 3 and 5 and for effort 1, 3 and 5, and score against them. Check effort with the people who will build it.

    2. Confusing value with novelty

      An idea can be exciting and new and still deliver little to users or the business. Being interesting to build is not the same as being valuable.

      Fix: define value before scoring, as user impact, revenue impact and strategic fit, and score against that definition rather than against excitement.

    3. Counting only engineering effort

      Design, QA, documentation, data migration, training and support are all effort. Leaving them out makes every idea look cheaper than it is.

      Fix: ask who has to do work for this to ship, and count all of their time.

    4. Anchoring on the HiPPO

      The highest-paid person’s opinion goes first and everyone else agrees. That is anchoring bias, and it turns a team exercise into one person’s ranking.

      Fix: score silently first. Everyone writes their value and effort scores on a sticky note, then all reveal at the same time.

    5. Using it for detailed planning

      The matrix sorts; it does not plan. It tells you what to focus on, not how to build it or exactly when.

      Fix: use it to decide what, then switch to RICE to rank the shortlist or to a story map to plan how and when it ships.

    6. Treating the quadrant as a verdict

      The grid sees only two scores. A Big bet that a key customer depends on this quarter, or a Fill-in that unblocks another team, can matter more than its position suggests.

      Fix: read the quadrant as the default move, then check strategy, dependencies and deadlines before you commit, and write down why when you override it.

    Running a 45-minute sorting session

    Who to invite, what to prepare and how to spend the time

    Must attend
    Product manager (facilitates), engineering lead, design lead
    Useful to have
    A stakeholder, a data analyst, anyone who will build the work
    Prepare
    A whiteboard or a digital board such as Miro or FigJam with the 2×2 drawn, sticky notes, and a list of 8–15 candidate items written up in advance, one per sticky. Bring no more than 20, or the session drags.
    1. 0–5 min

      Set the context

      Explain the matrix. Define what value means for this session, such as user impact, revenue or retention, agree what effort covers and name the anchor items for 1, 3 and 5 on each scale.

    2. 5–15 min

      Score silently

      Everyone scores each item on their own sticky notes, value 1–5 and effort 1–5. No discussion yet.

    3. 15–25 min

      Reveal and plot

      Go item by item. Everyone reveals their scores; average them or discuss the outliers, then place the sticky on the grid.

    4. 25–35 min

      Debate the edges

      Focus on items on or near the midline, where placement changes the decision. Timebox each one to 2 minutes.

    5. 35–45 min

      Decide and commit

      Quick wins go into the next sprint, Big bets get a roadmap slot, Fill-ins go to the backlog and Avoid items are archived or dropped. Photograph the board.

    Three facilitation habits

    • Define value up front. If you do not, everyone scores against different criteria and the grid means nothing.
    • Spend the debate on the edges. Items clearly inside a quadrant need no discussion; items on the border do.
    • Photograph the result. Keep a picture of the board or an export of the digital one; you will refer to it for weeks.

    Check your understanding

    Six questions on the quadrants, the scores and the session

    1. A feature with high value and low effort goes in which quadrant?

      Quick wins pair high value with low effort, the best return on the work, so they go first. Big bets are high value but high effort; Fill-ins are low on both.

    2. Your team scored 12 of 15 features as Quick wins. What should you do before acting on the grid?

      When most items land in Quick wins, value is being inflated or effort underestimated. Anchor items for 1, 3 and 5 give every score the same meaning, and checking effort with the people who will build it catches optimistic estimates. Moving the midline hides the inflation instead of fixing it, and building all 12 trusts scores that are probably wrong.

    3. Personalized recommendations scores Value 5 and Effort 5. What should the team do with it?

      Value 5 and Effort 5 is a Big bet: worth doing but expensive. Break it into phases, validate the riskiest assumptions with the first slice and staff it well. Building it in one release puts months of work at risk before anything is learned; dropping it gives up an idea with the highest possible value score.

    4. What is the best way to prevent HiPPO bias in a scoring session?

      Silent scoring prevents anchoring. Everyone commits to their value and effort scores independently, then all reveal together. Discussing first lets the loudest or most senior voice set everyone else’s scores, and a senior review at the end lets one opinion override the team’s.

    5. An idea scores Value 3 and Effort 2. Where does it go?

      A 3 sits on the midline, between Quick wins and Fill-ins here. A Quick win needs a Value of 4 or 5, so this is a border item to settle in discussion.

    6. Value vs Effort is best used as…

      Its job is the first pass: cut about 30 ideas to about 10, then rank that shortlist with RICE. Relative 1–5 scores are too coarse to replace RICE on a large backlog or to plan a quarter in detail.
