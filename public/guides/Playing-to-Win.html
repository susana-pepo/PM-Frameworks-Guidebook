Playing to Win describes a strategy as five linked choices, from what winning means down to the systems that keep the organization on course. Use it to turn goals and wish lists into a strategy a team can act on.

In short

Where to play and how to win are the core: the aspiration frames them, and capabilities and systems exist to make them work. A target like “grow 20%” isn’t one of the five; it goes in the management systems as a measure. A finished cascade passes one test: a competitor could not adopt it unchanged.

What each of the five choices decides

Most documents called a strategy are really goals (“grow 20%”) or activities (“launch a mobile app”). In Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works (2013), A.G. Lafley, former CEO of Procter & Gamble, and Roger Martin, former dean of the Rotman School of Management and a long-time adviser to P&G, hold strategy to a stricter standard: an integrated set of choices about where and how to compete, which reinforce one another and position you to win.

The important word is choice. A strategy decides what you will not do as firmly as what you will. If you are trying to serve everyone, you haven’t made a choice yet.

1

Winning aspiration

What winning means for you, stated as a purpose rather than a revenue target. “Help every adult who has wanted to play the piano play the songs they love” is an aspiration.

Sets the boundary for every choice below it

2

Where to play

The specific playing field: geography, customer segment, product category, channel and value chain stage. The more specific, the better.

With how to win, the heart of the strategy

3

How to win

Your advantage on the field you chose. There are two basic routes: cost leadership, which delivers acceptable value at the lowest cost (not necessarily the lowest price), or differentiation, which delivers unique value that justifies a premium.

Decides the capabilities you need

4

Capabilities

What you must be excellent at to win this way: the few capabilities that make your where-to-play and how-to-win choices work, and that reinforce each other. Everything else only has to be good enough. The book sets no number, but a long list means you have no priorities.

Follows from how you win

5

Management systems

The processes, metrics and structures that make the choices stick: OKRs, hiring profiles, team structure and review cadences. Together they are the operating system of the strategy.

Keeps the other four in place

The five choices only work as a set

A common mistake is to make each choice on its own: “Our aspiration is X, we play everywhere, and we win by being better at everything.” That is a wish list. In a strategy, every choice constrains the ones beneath it.

Where to play rules out most of the market, and how to win rules out most ways of competing in what’s left. Capabilities and systems then follow from those two. Nobody fills in the cascade once from the top, though. Teams draft the five choices together and keep revising them as they learn what they can actually build.

The competitor test

Read your strategy statement aloud and imagine your closest rival saying it. If it would fit them unchanged, it isn’t specific enough. A real cascade only works for the organization that wrote it.

Cascades nest inside each other

The same five choices repeat at every level of an organization. Each level’s cascade sits inside the one above it: the level above sets the boundary, and the level below makes its own choices within it.

Company
Which businesses to be in, and what winning means across the portfolio
Category or business unit
Where to play and how to win within one market, inside the company’s choices
Brand or product
The same five choices for a single offer, inside the category’s choices. A product team’s cascade lives here, and the company’s where to play is its outer edge.

Where to play and how to win are the core

Lafley and Martin call these two choices the heart of strategy. The aspiration frames them, and capabilities and management systems make them possible. If either is vague, the rest of the cascade has nothing to support.

Two ways to win

Playing to Win takes its two routes from Michael Porter’s generic strategies. Cost leadership means the lowest cost in the industry, which isn’t the same as the lowest price. Differentiation means a valued difference that customers will pay more for.

Cost leadership and differentiation compared
DimensionCost leadershipDifferentiation
What you deliverAcceptable value at the lowest costUnique value that justifies a premium price
How you winEfficiency, scale and simplicityInnovation, brand or experience
ExamplesIKEA, Ryanair, WalmartOlay, Apple, Dyson
The riskA race to the bottom on priceA niche too small to sustain the premium

The book’s own differentiation case is Olay: Procter & Gamble repositioned it as a premium “masstige” skin-care brand, priced between mass-market and prestige brands but sold in mass retail. The other names in the table are commonly cited textbook examples, not statements from the companies. Pick one route and let it shape the capabilities. Trying for both is the trap Porter called stuck in the middle.

Ask what would have to be true

Teams stall on these two choices because each side argues that its option is right. Roger Martin’s method, set out in the book and in his later writing, changes the question. For each option, ask: what would have to be true for this to be a great choice? The argument moves from opinions to conditions the team can test.

  1. Turn the issue into a choice

    Frame at least two mutually exclusive options that would each resolve the problem in front of you.

    With only one option on the table, there is nothing to choose.

  2. Generate possibilities

    Widen the set. Each possibility is a complete where-to-play and how-to-win story, including some that look unlikely at first.

  3. Specify the conditions

    For each possibility, list what would have to be true about customers, the industry, your capabilities, your costs and competitors’ reactions for it to be a great choice.

    Do not argue yet about whether the conditions hold.

  4. Identify the barriers

    Mark the conditions you are least confident are true. These are what stands between you and a choice.

  5. Design the tests

    For each barrier, agree on a test whose result the whole team would accept. Let the most skeptical person design it.

  6. Run the tests

    Start with the condition most likely to rule an option out, so you spend the least effort on options that will fail.

  7. Choose

    Pick the possibility whose conditions held up best. The team agreed in advance what evidence would settle it, so the decision sticks.

Worked example

Teaching adult beginners to play the piano

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app, teaches adults to play through real-time feedback on each note, heard through the phone’s microphone or a MIDI keyboard.

The two core choices decide the capabilities and the metric

Piano and keyboard only (where to play) makes accurate note detection (a capability) achievable, because the team tunes detection for one instrument’s sound instead of many.

Learning outcomes (how to win) is why Weekly Active Learners, which counts learners completing lessons, is a headline metric (management systems), rather than app opens or daily active users alone.

Change one choice and the others have to change with it. Adding guitar would split the audio work, dilute the teaching expertise and blur the metric.

    Saying it in an interview

    For this product, I would define the strategy choice cascade.
Our aspiration is [what winning looks like for customers].
We would play in [segment], via [channel], and not in [what we exclude].
We would win through [cost leadership or a specific differentiation].
That requires capabilities in [Y] and [Z].
We would keep it on track with [the metric and process that reinforce it].

    Reach for Playing to Win when you are choosing a direction

    Use it when

    • You are defining or refining a product strategy
    • The team cannot agree on priorities, because the cascade forces the choices into the open
    • You are running annual or quarterly planning
    • A PM interview asks how you would approach strategy for a product
    • You are launching a new product and need to define the playing field

    Skip it when

    • You need to prioritize features. Use RICE or ICE instead.
    • You need user insight. Use Jobs to Be Done or user research.
    • You need a competitive diagnosis. Start with Porter’s Five Forces.
    • You need design solutions. Use design thinking.
    • You are in early discovery and do not yet have the evidence a strategy needs

    In a planning cycle, Playing to Win is usually where options become choices: after the market has been diagnosed and before the plan turns into quarterly work. Compare the strategy frameworks to see how they hand off and when a step can be skipped.

    Six ways a cascade breaks

    1. Choices that do not fit together

      When each choice is drafted by a different person or in a different workshop, the five answers read well alone and contradict each other as a set, or add up to something generic.

      Fix: read the cascade from top to bottom and ask what would have to be true for each choice to work given the others. When you change one, revisit the rest, and check that a competitor could not adopt the whole cascade unchanged.

    2. “We play everywhere”

      If you have not said no to any market, you have not made a where-to-play choice.

      Fix: write the exclusions down. Name the segments, geographies, channels or products you will not serve, and say why.

    3. “We win by being the best”

      Best at what, and for whom? “Best” is a hope, and every competitor can claim it. It says nothing about how you win.

      Fix: state whether you win on lower cost or on a difference customers value, and for which customers. Then complete the sentence “we win through [specific advantage] that [specific competitor] cannot match because [reason].”

    4. “We need to be great at everything”

      If you list ten capabilities, you have no priorities, and investment spreads too thin to build any of them.

      Fix: for each capability, ask which where-to-play or how-to-win choice fails without it. If none does, it only has to be good enough.

    5. A target in place of a strategy

      “Our strategy is to grow 20%” is a goal, not a choice. It says nothing about how you will reach it. The same mistake shows up when a revenue or market-share figure fills the aspiration box: it tells the team nothing about which customers to serve or why they will choose you.

      Fix: put targets in the management systems, for example as OKRs or other measures, and keep the aspiration about who you serve and what winning looks like for them. Then answer where you will play and how you will win to reach the target.

    6. Stuck in the middle

      A business that tries to be both low-cost and differentiated usually ends up as neither: middling quality at middling prices. The term is Michael Porter’s, from Competitive Strategy (1980). Blue Ocean Strategy argues that value innovation can break the trade-off, but treat that as an exception you have to prove.

      Fix: write down which route you are on, then check every capability and metric against it. One that only serves the other route is a sign you are drifting into the middle.

    Test yourself on Playing to Win

    1. According to Playing to Win, what is a strategy?

      Lafley and Martin judge a strategy by its choices and by how tightly those choices reinforce one another. A revenue plan is the tempting pick, but targets measure results; they never say where or how you will compete.

    2. What are the five choices in the cascade?

      The cascade opens with what winning means and closes with the systems that hold the other four in place. Vision, mission and goals look like a close match, yet they state ambitions and measures without picking a market or a way to win. The other two lists come from the marketing mix and the Lean Canvas, tools for launching a product or sketching a business model.

    3. Which two choices do Lafley and Martin call the heart of strategy?

      These two set the playing field and the advantage on it, which is why the book puts them at the center. Pairing the aspiration with how to win is tempting, but the aspiration only draws the boundary that where to play then fills in.

    4. A leadership deck opens with “Our strategy is to grow 20% this year.” What is the problem with it?

      Twenty percent growth is an outcome to track: it picks no customer and no advantage, so the team still has nothing to act on. Being measurable doesn’t rescue it, because measures belong in the management systems, under choices that explain how the number gets hit.

    5. What does “stuck in the middle” mean?

      Chasing a low-cost model and a premium difference at the same time splits investment between two incompatible businesses, and the company ends up with neither advantage. Serving mid-market buyers sounds like the same thing, but it is a where-to-play choice and can be a perfectly sound one.

    6. How can you tell whether a strategy statement is specific enough?

      Specific means the statement depends on your own customers, assets and edge, so a rival couldn’t borrow it. A tidy one-pager signed off by leadership can still be generic; format and approval say nothing about whose strategy it is.

    7. Your team is split between two where-to-play options. What does “what would have to be true?” ask you to do?

      The question replaces a debate between camps with a list of conditions, and the doubtful ones become tests both sides accept before anyone decides. A vote or a merged plan ends the argument without any evidence. Going with the bigger market feels objective, but market size is only one condition among several.

    8. Larkwell plays in piano and keyboard only. Leadership now proposes adding guitar lessons next quarter. What does the cascade tell you?

      Larkwell’s aspiration names the piano, and its note detection, lesson reviews and headline metric were all built around one instrument, so guitar reaches every level. Calling it a where-to-play change alone misses how far that one choice runs down the cascade.
