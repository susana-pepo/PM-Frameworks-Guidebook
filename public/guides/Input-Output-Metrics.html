The model sorts a company’s numbers into two kinds. Output metrics are the results the business wants, such as revenue or retention. Input metrics are the controllable, customer-facing measures a team’s own work moves directly, and that push the outputs up when they rise.

In short

Give every input one team that owns it, usually three to five inputs per output, and check that each is controllable, predictive and fast enough to move within a week. Pair any input that can be gamed with a counter-metric, a number that gets worse when the input is pushed too far. Review the inputs weekly and judge progress by the output.

Inputs you control, outputs you measure

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), has a company key result that reads “D30 retention of new accounts from 9% to 11%.” It’s a clear goal, yet it doesn’t tell any team what to work on this week. Retention is an output: no team can change it directly, only the things that influence it.

Splitting leading measures from lagging ones is an old management idea. Andy Grove wrote about leading indicators, and about pairing each one with a guard against its side effects, in High Output Management (1983). The version product teams usually cite comes from Amazon, whose leaders review a set of controllable input metrics every week in the Weekly Business Review (WBR), next to the financial results they lead to. Colin Bryar and Bill Carr describe the practice in Working Backwards (2021).

Input metrics: they move first

Controllable, customer-facing measures the team moves directly through its own work, such as median time to first note, lesson completion rate or the share of lapsed learners who return after a day-3 reminder. They respond within days, so they give an early signal.

Output metrics: they follow the inputs

The business results you want to achieve: retention, revenue, NPS, growth rate. Many inputs and outside factors shape them, so they move weeks or months after the inputs that drive them.

No one can “do retention.” A team can only do the things that cause it, and its input metric is how it checks that the work is landing.

Activities are not input metrics

Redesigning onboarding is something a team does. Median time to first note is the number that work should move. In the same way, publishing 8 new beginner song lessons is an activity; quality-reviewed beginner lessons available is the input metric it moves. Track the number, and treat each project as one way of moving it.

Leading and lagging depend on the level

Weekly Active Learners is an output: the teams below it move it through their inputs. It is also a leading indicator of revenue, because it moves before revenue does. A North Star Metric leads revenue and lags the inputs that drive it. Name the level before you argue about whether a metric leads or lags.

Three layers, from team work to business results

Retention, where a company tracks it, sits between the first two layers as an intermediate output. The inputs move it and it moves the North Star Metric, but it stays an output: no team owns it as its input.

Three tests for an input metric

1

Controllable

Can the team move it through its own work?

“Median time to first note” passes. “App Store ranking” does not: too many outside factors move it.

2

Predictive

When it rises, does the output follow?

Expect some lag, and check the link with data before you rely on it: more push notifications don’t always mean more retention.

3

Timely

Does it move faster than the output?

An input should give a weekly signal, ideally a daily one. If you have to wait six months to learn whether it worked, it is too slow.

Inputs get sharper over time

In Bryar and Carr’s account, Amazon’s retail team first tracked how many new product pages it created, then how often those pages were viewed, and finally the share of views where the item was in stock and ready to ship. Each change moved the input closer to what customers experienced. Expect to revise your own inputs once you see how well they predict the output.

Guard each input, then trace it to revenue

Counter-metrics catch over-optimization

Many inputs can be gamed or pushed too far. If the input is the day-3 reminder return rate, a team can lift it by sending reminders every few hours: good for the input, bad for the people receiving them. The counter-metric is the number that gets worse when that happens, here the notification opt-out rate. Any input that can be gamed needs one, tracked next to it so the two are always read together.

A counter-metric has to pull against the metric it guards. Subscriber churn fails that test for Weekly Active Learners. Churn worsens when active learners fall and improves when they rise, so it reports the same health from the other side and can’t warn you that the North Star Metric is being pushed up in a harmful way.

Ask what breaks at twice the volume

For each input, ask what would go wrong if a team doubled it. The answer usually names the counter-metric. An input that cannot be pushed in a harmful way may not need one.

A metric tree breaks an output into its levers

Each branch of the tree is a different lever, and often a different team’s focus. For a subscription product, monthly recurring revenue splits into how many people pay and how much each of them pays. At Larkwell that is 60,000 paying subscribers × $9.00 ARPPU = $540,000 of MRR.

Decompose with arithmetic

Writing the tree as a formula checks that the branches really multiply back to the output, and it shows which factor a single team could take on.

MAU ×Paid share ×ARPPU =MRR

At Larkwell: 400,000 monthly active users × 15% paying × $9.00 = $540,000. Paid share in turn depends on trial-to-paid conversion, so this is how “increase revenue” (vague) becomes “raise trial-to-paid conversion from 30% to 33%” (a number one team can work on). With 20,000 trial starts a month, that is 600 more new subscribers each month.

Use revenue per paying user, not per user

ARPU averages revenue across every user, paying or not, so it already includes the conversion rate: Larkwell’s ARPU is 15% × $9.00 = $1.35 a month. Multiplying it by conversion counts conversion twice. Write either MAU × paid share × ARPPU or MAU × ARPU (400,000 × $1.35 = $540,000), never MAU × paid share × ARPU.

Worked example

One owner and one counter-metric for every metric

Larkwell has 400,000 monthly active users and 60,000 paying subscribers. Its North Star Metric is Weekly Active Learners, learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week. Four inputs sit below it, each with an owner.

Every input has an owner and a line to MRR

  1. The growth team brings in new learners and gets them to complete a first song lesson within 24 hours (about 8,300 a week).
  2. The onboarding team cuts the time to first note, so the first session feels like progress.
  3. The content team grows the library of quality-reviewed beginner lessons waiting for them (120 today).
  4. The growth team’s lifecycle work brings back learners who lapse, measured by the day-3 reminder return rate.
  5. Together, these lift retention, an intermediate output no team owns, and Weekly Active Learners, which drives MRR.

Each team’s number traces up to MRR through the North Star, and the two outputs have counters of their own. Weekly Active Learners is read against the average lesson rating, which would fall if the team split lessons into shorter, thinner ones to reach three a week. MRR is read against the refund rate.

Lessons available rose to 128 while completion of the new ones fell

This quarter the content team publishes 8 new beginner song lessons, taking quality-reviewed beginner lessons available from 120 to 128. The completion rate of new lessons, its counter-metric, drops at the same time. The likely cause is that the new lessons are too hard. Read alone, the input would have looked like a win while it was hurting the output.

From the stack to this quarter’s OKRs

The stack translates into the company OKR and each team’s OKR without changing any metric. The objective stays qualitative. The North Star Metric, or one of its inputs, appears as a key result; it is never the objective, and the projects that move it are initiatives, not key results.

It lists “Publish 8 new beginner song lessons” as a committed key result: the team promises the deliverable, and its other key results measure whether it worked. In the stack the lessons are still the activity; the input metric they move is quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, from 120 to 128.

    Saying it in an interview

    I would structure the metrics as a stack. The output we are optimizing for is [X], and the team inputs that drive it are [A], [B] and [C]. I would track counter-metrics such as [Y] to make sure we are not over-optimizing one lever at the expense of the user experience.

    The model turns a goal that is already set into levers for each team

    Use it when

    • You are setting team-level OKRs and goals
    • You need to turn a North Star Metric into work for each team
    • An interviewer asks “how would you measure success?”
    • You are building a product analytics dashboard
    • Functions need to agree who owns which metric
    • You are planning a sprint and need to name the inputs it will move

    Skip it when

    • You have not chosen your North Star Metric yet. Start with the North Star Framework.
    • You are before product-market fit, when it is too early to optimize
    • You are diagnosing where a funnel leaks. Use AARRR (pirate metrics) instead.
    • You are setting high-level strategic direction. Use Playing to Win instead.
    • You are prioritizing individual features. Use RICE instead.

    In a growth plan, input and output metrics usually come after AARRR, which finds the stage that leaks, and the North Star Framework, which picks the output this model breaks down. They come before Growth Loops, which feeds a loop’s output back in as input so growth compounds. Choosing a growth framework compares all four, from diagnosing the funnel to designing loops.

    Six ways a metric stack goes wrong

    1. Calling an activity an input

      “Publish 8 new lessons,” “onboarding redesign” and “push notification timing” are deliverables, projects and settings, not metrics the team moves week to week. Once the project ships, there is nothing left to track.

      Fix: name the customer-facing metric the work should move, such as quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, median time to first note or day-3 reminder return rate, and keep tracking it after the project ends.

    2. Mistaking a correlation for a lever

      An input that rises with the output may not cause it. “We sent more emails” does not always mean “retention improved.” A team can push an input for a quarter and leave the output where it was.

      Fix: test the link with an experiment before making the input a team’s goal: if we double this input, does the output move? If not, find a better lever.

    3. Choosing inputs the team cannot move

      An App Store ranking or a market-wide trend responds to too many outside factors. The team cannot tell whether its work made the difference.

      Fix: run each candidate through the three tests, and drop any the team can’t move with its own work.

    4. Reading an input without its counter

      A team can push an input up in ways that hurt users, such as sending more reminders, and an input on its own always looks like progress when it rises. At Larkwell, 8 new lessons lifted the content team’s input while completion of those lessons fell, and only the counter showed it.

      Fix: pair each input that can be gamed with a counter-metric, put the two side by side on the same dashboard and review them together.

    5. A counter-metric that moves with what it guards

      A metric that tracks the same health as the one it guards can’t warn you. Subscriber churn gets worse whenever Weekly Active Learners falls, so it says nothing about how the number was reached.

      Fix: pick a metric that gets worse when the input is pushed too far, such as the average lesson rating for Weekly Active Learners.

    6. Reviewing only outputs

      Weekly reviews of revenue and retention show results too late to act on, and they do not say which lever to pull.

      Fix: review controllable inputs every week, as Amazon’s Weekly Business Review does (Bryar and Carr, 2021), with the outputs alongside as the results the inputs should explain.

    Test yourself on input and output metrics

    1. What separates an input metric from an output metric?

      The dividing line is control. A team’s own work changes an input such as time to first note, while retention or revenue moves through its inputs and outside factors, never directly. Either kind can be a count or a rate, and executives and teams look at both.

    2. A team’s only goal is “D30 retention of new accounts from 9% to 11%.” What is missing?

      The goal names a result and gives the team nothing to work on this week, and it shows progress only weeks later. A stretch target of 15% leaves the same gap, because a bigger output is still an output.

    3. What does a counter-metric do?

      It is the number that worsens when an input is pushed too hard, like opt-outs climbing as reminders multiply. A mirror of the North Star only restates the target, so it can’t flag harm in how the target was reached.

    4. Which makes the best input metric?

      Those three properties make an input worth handing to a team: the team can change it, the output follows, and the signal arrives within days. A metric that is easy to report can still be one no team controls or one the output ignores.

    5. Larkwell’s onboarding team drafts an OKR. Objective: “Median time to first note under 90 seconds.” Key result: “Ship the Sprint 14 onboarding changes.” How should it be rewritten?

      The objective says in words what should change, the input metric measures it, and the Sprint 14 work is how the team gets there, so it belongs among the initiatives. Putting Weekly Active Learners in the objective swaps one number for another, and the North Star belongs in the company’s key results anyway.

    6. Larkwell’s content team publishes 8 new beginner song lessons, so quality-reviewed beginner lessons available rises from 120 to 128, but the completion rate of new lessons drops. What is the most likely reading?

      When an input rises and its counter falls, the counter is pointing at the work itself: the new lessons are probably harder or longer than learners can finish. Nothing points to bad data, and a bigger library doesn’t push completion down on its own.

    7. A product manager proposes subscriber churn as the counter-metric for Weekly Active Learners. What is the problem?

      Churn and Weekly Active Learners report the same health from two sides, so if the team inflated active learners with thinner lessons, churn would look fine too, while the average lesson rating would drop. Calling churn an input is the tempting slip: it’s an output, and outputs can have counter-metrics as well. Who can act on churn doesn’t decide whether it’s a good counter.
