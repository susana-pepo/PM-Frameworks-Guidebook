Input and Output Metrics separate the numbers a team can move directly from the business results it is trying to achieve. Use the split to give every team a lever it controls and a visible line from that lever to the result.

In short

Output metrics are the results you want, such as revenue or retention. Input metrics are the controllable, customer-facing measures a team can move directly and that, when they rise, push the outputs up. Give each team an input to work on, pair any input that can be gamed with a counter-metric, and judge progress by the output.

Inputs you control, outputs you measure

Retention is a result, so no team can work on it directly

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app, has a company key result that reads “D30 retention of new accounts from 9% to 11%.” It is a clear goal, but it does not tell any team what to work on this week. Retention is an output: no team can change it directly, only the things that influence it.

The model separates what a team controls (inputs) from what it is trying to achieve (outputs). When the link between the two is written down, each team knows which number its daily work should move. Separating leading measures from lagging ones is an old management idea: Andy Grove wrote about leading indicators, and about pairing each one with a guard against its side effects, in High Output Management (1983). The version product teams usually cite comes from Amazon, whose leaders review a set of controllable input metrics every week in the Weekly Business Review (WBR), next to the financial results they lead to. Colin Bryar and Bill Carr describe the practice in their book Working Backwards (2021).

Input metrics: they move first

Controllable, customer-facing measures the team moves directly through its own work, such as median time to first note, lesson completion rate or the share of lapsed learners who return after a day-3 reminder. They respond within days, so they give an early signal.

Output metrics: they follow the inputs

The business results you want to achieve: retention, revenue, NPS, growth rate. Many inputs and outside factors shape them, so no team can change them directly. They lag the inputs that drive them.

No one can “do retention.” A team can only do things that cause it, and the input metric is how it checks that the work is landing. Retention stays an output even inside a metric tree: it can sit between the inputs and the North Star Metric as an intermediate output, but no team owns it as an input.

Activities are not input metrics

Redesigning onboarding is something a team does. Median time to first note is the number that work should move. In the same way, publishing 8 new beginner song lessons is an activity; quality-reviewed beginner lessons available is the input metric it moves. Track the number, and treat each project as one way of moving it.

Leading and lagging depend on the level

Weekly Active Learners is an output: the teams below it move it through their inputs. It is also a leading indicator of revenue, because it moves before revenue does. A North Star Metric leads revenue and lags the inputs that drive it. Name the level before you argue about whether a metric leads or lags.

Three layers, from team work to business results

Each layer drives the next one, and each has its own owner

Retention, where a team tracks it, sits between the first two layers as an intermediate output: the inputs move it, and it moves the North Star Metric.

Three tests for an input metric

1

Controllable

Can the team move it through its own work?

“Median time to first note” passes. “App Store ranking” does not: too many outside factors move it.

2

Predictive

When it rises, does the output follow?

The output should follow, with some lag. Validate the link with data: more push notifications do not always mean more retention.

3

Timely

Does it move faster than the output?

An input should give a weekly signal, ideally a daily one. If you have to wait six months to learn whether it worked, it is too slow.

Inputs get sharper over time

In Bryar and Carr’s account, Amazon’s retail team first tracked how many new product pages it created, then how often those pages were viewed, and finally the share of views where the item was in stock and ready to ship. Each change moved the input closer to what customers experienced. Larkwell’s content team makes the same move: rather than count the lessons it publishes, it tracks the quality-reviewed beginner lessons available to learners. Expect to revise your inputs once you see how well they predict the output.

Guard each input, then trace it to revenue

A counter-metric catches over-optimization; a metric tree shows which lever moves which result

Counter-metrics catch over-optimization

Many inputs can be gamed or pushed too far. If the input is the day-3 reminder return rate, a team can lift it by sending reminders every few hours: good for the input, bad for the people receiving them. A counter-metric is a metric that gets worse when that happens. Pair any input that can be gamed with one, track it next to the input, and read the two together.

A counter-metric must pull against the metric it guards, not move with it. Subscriber churn is not a counter-metric for Weekly Active Learners: it tends to rise when the North Star Metric falls, so it moves with it and cannot warn that the North Star Metric is being pushed in a harmful way.

Ask what breaks at twice the volume

For each input, ask what would go wrong if a team doubled it. The answer usually names the counter-metric. An input that cannot be pushed in a harmful way may not need one.

A metric tree breaks an output into its levers

A metric tree decomposes an output into the inputs that produce it. Each branch is a different lever, and often a different team’s focus. Monthly recurring revenue, for a subscription product, splits into how many people pay and how much each of them pays. At Larkwell that is 60,000 paying subscribers × $9.00 ARPPU = $540,000 of MRR.

Decompose with arithmetic

Any output can be broken into factors with a formula. Each factor is a branch that a team can work on through its own input metrics.

MAU ×Paid share ×ARPPU =MRR

At Larkwell: 400,000 monthly active users × 15% paying × $9.00 = $540,000. Paid share in turn depends on trial-to-paid conversion, so this is how “increase revenue” (vague) becomes “raise trial-to-paid conversion from 30% to 33%” (a number one team can work on). With 20,000 trial starts a month, that is 600 more new subscribers each month.

Use revenue per paying user, not per user

ARPU averages revenue across every user, paying or not, so it already includes the conversion rate: Larkwell’s ARPU is 15% × $9.00 = $1.35 a month. Multiplying it by conversion counts conversion twice. Write either MAU × paid share × ARPPU or MAU × ARPU (400,000 × $1.35 = $540,000), never MAU × paid share × ARPU.

Building Larkwell’s metric stack, from MRR to each team’s lever

Worked example

One owner and one counter-metric for every metric

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults, has 400,000 monthly active users and 60,000 paying subscribers; all its figures are illustrative. Its North Star Metric is Weekly Active Learners, learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week. Four inputs sit below it, each with an owner.

How the stack connects

  1. The growth team brings in new learners and gets them to complete a first song lesson within 24 hours (about 8,300 a week).
  2. The onboarding team cuts the time to first note, so the first session feels like progress.
  3. The content team grows the library of quality-reviewed beginner lessons waiting for them (120 today).
  4. The growth team’s lifecycle work brings back learners who lapse, measured by the day-3 reminder return rate.
  5. Together, these lift retention, an intermediate output no team owns, and Weekly Active Learners, which drives MRR.

Each team has one number it can move this week and a clear line from that number to the business result. The counters pull against what they guard: Weekly Active Learners is read against the average lesson rating, which falls if the team splits lessons into shorter, thinner ones to reach three a week, and MRR against the refund rate.

What the counter-metric caught

This quarter the content team publishes 8 new beginner song lessons, taking quality-reviewed beginner lessons available from 120 to 128, so its input rises. The completion rate of new lessons, its counter-metric, drops. The likely cause is that the new lessons are too hard. Without the counter, the team would celebrate the input and miss that it was hurting the output.

From the stack to this quarter’s OKRs

The stack translates into the company OKR and each team’s OKR without changing any metric. The objective stays qualitative. The North Star Metric, or one of its inputs, appears as a key result; it is never the objective, and the projects that move it are initiatives, not key results.

In the stack the lessons are still the activity; the input metric they move is quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, from 120 to 128.

    Saying it in an interview

    I would structure the metrics as a stack. The output we are optimizing for is [X], and the team inputs that drive it are [A], [B] and [C]. I would track counter-metrics such as [Y] to make sure we are not over-optimizing one lever at the expense of the user experience.

    When the model fits, and when it does not

    It turns a chosen goal into team levers, so it comes after the goal is set

    Use it when

    • You are setting team-level OKRs and goals
    • You need to turn a North Star Metric into work for each team
    • An interviewer asks “how would you measure success?”
    • You are building a product analytics dashboard
    • Functions need to agree who owns which metric
    • You are planning a sprint and need to name the inputs it will move

    Skip it when

    • You have not chosen your North Star Metric yet. Start with the North Star Framework.
    • You are before product-market fit, when it is too early to optimize
    • You are diagnosing where a funnel leaks. Use AARRR (pirate metrics) instead.
    • You are setting high-level strategic direction. Use Playing to Win instead.
    • You are prioritizing individual features. Use RICE instead.

    In a growth plan, inputs and outputs usually come third, once the North Star Metric is chosen. Choosing a growth framework shows how the four growth frameworks fit together, from diagnosing the funnel to designing loops.

    Six ways a metric stack goes wrong

    Most failures come from a weak link between an input, its counter and the output

    1. Calling an activity an input

      “Publish 8 new lessons,” “onboarding redesign” and “push notification timing” are deliverables, projects and settings, not metrics the team moves week to week. Once the project ships, there is nothing left to track.

      Fix: name the customer-facing metric the work should move, such as quality-reviewed beginner lessons available, median time to first note or day-3 reminder return rate, and keep tracking it after the project ends.

    2. Mistaking a correlation for a lever

      An input that rises with the output may not cause it. “We sent more emails” does not always mean “retention improved.” A team can push an input for a quarter and leave the output where it was.

      Fix: test the link with an experiment before making the input a team’s goal: if we double this input, does the output move? If not, find a better lever.

    3. Choosing inputs the team cannot move

      An App Store ranking or a market-wide trend responds to too many outside factors. The team cannot tell whether its work made the difference.

      Fix: apply the three tests. Keep inputs the team controls, that predict the output and that give a weekly signal.

    4. Reading an input without its counter

      A team can push an input up in ways that hurt users, such as sending more reminders, and an input on its own always looks like progress when it rises. Larkwell’s content team would have celebrated 8 new lessons while the completion rate of new lessons fell.

      Fix: pair each input that can be gamed with a counter-metric, put the two side by side on the same dashboard and review them together.

    5. A counter-metric that moves with the input

      A metric that rises and falls with the one it guards cannot warn you. Subscriber churn tends to rise when Weekly Active Learners falls, so it says nothing about how the number was reached.

      Fix: pick a metric that gets worse when the input is pushed too far, such as the average lesson rating for Weekly Active Learners.

    6. Reviewing only outputs

      Weekly reviews of revenue and retention show results too late to act on, and they do not say which lever to pull.

      Fix: review controllable inputs every week, as Amazon’s Weekly Business Review does (Bryar and Carr, 2021), and read the outputs next to them as results to explain, not as levers to pull.

    Check your understanding

    Seven questions on inputs, outputs, counter-metrics and OKRs

    1. What separates an input metric from an output metric?

      An input is a controllable, customer-facing measure the team’s own work moves, such as median time to first note. An output, such as retention or revenue, results from many inputs and cannot be changed directly. Both can be counts or rates, and both go to teams and executives.

    2. A team’s only goal is “D30 retention of new accounts from 9% to 11%.” What is missing?

      Retention is an output: it lags the inputs that drive it. The goal says what should happen but not what the team should work on. Inputs give the team levers and an earlier signal of whether its work is landing. A higher target or a second output would not change that.

    3. What does a counter-metric do?

      A counter-metric is a guardrail that gets worse when an input is pushed too far. A day-3 reminder return rate read without the notification opt-out rate can hide a team sending so many reminders that learners switch notifications off.

    4. Which makes the best input metric?

      A good input passes three tests: the team can move it (controllable), the output follows when it rises (predictive), and it gives a weekly or daily signal (timely). Ease of reporting, raw growth and a link to the revenue target say nothing about whether the team can move it.

    5. Larkwell’s onboarding team drafts an OKR. Objective: “Median time to first note under 90 seconds.” Key result: “Ship the Sprint 14 onboarding changes.” How should it be rewritten?

      An objective is qualitative, such as “make a new learner’s first week feel like progress.” The input metric becomes the key result, median time to first note from 4 minutes to under 90 seconds, with week-1 setup errors as its guardrail. The Sprint 14 changes are the initiative that should move it: shipping them measures the work, not its effect. The North Star Metric is never the objective either; Weekly Active Learners from 40,000 to 50,000 is a company key result.

    6. Larkwell’s content team publishes 8 new beginner song lessons, so quality-reviewed beginner lessons available rises from 120 to 128, but the completion rate of new lessons drops. What is the most likely reading?

      This is the case counter-metrics exist for. The input looks healthy, but the counter shows the new content is hurting the learning experience. Nothing in the numbers points to bad data, and a larger library does not lower completion by itself. Without the counter, the team would celebrate while learners struggled.

    7. A product manager proposes subscriber churn as the counter-metric for Weekly Active Learners. What is the problem?

      A counter-metric must pull against the metric it guards. Churn rises when active learners fall, so it tells the team what the North Star Metric already shows. If the team reached three lessons a week by splitting lessons into thinner ones, churn would not catch it; the average lesson rating would. Churn is an output, and outputs such as the North Star Metric can have counters too. Who can act on churn is beside the point; what matters is whether the metric pulls against the one it guards.
