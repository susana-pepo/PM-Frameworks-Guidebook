A Wardley map places the components that serve a user need on two axes: how visible each component is to the user (the value chain, top to bottom) and how evolved it is (left to right, from genesis to commodity). Because components evolve, the map shows where to build, where to buy and where to outsource.

In short

Put the user’s need at the top, list the components that serve it in order of how visible they are and place each one by how evolved it is, from genesis to commodity. Explore what is in genesis, build where you differentiate, buy or adopt products and outsource commodities. Then watch what is moving: the direction of evolution is predictable, its timing is not.

A map of what you need and how mature it is

Two axes turn a list of dependencies into a picture a team can argue about

Teams often plan strategy knowing their capabilities and goals but without situational awareness: they cannot see the landscape or how it is changing.

Simon Wardley developed Wardley Mapping around 2005 to give strategy the equivalent of a geographical map, and has published it online as a book, Wardley Maps, since 2016. A map shows what you need to serve a user, how visible each part is to that user, how evolved each part is and where each part is heading.

Situational awareness

See the whole landscape: your value chain, what depends on what and how evolved each component is.

Everything evolves

Every component moves from genesis to custom-built to product to commodity as competition spreads it. The direction of this movement is predictable; its timing is not.

Build, buy or outsource

A component’s position tells you how to source it: explore what is new, build where you differentiate, buy products and outsource commodities.

Height is visibility, width is evolution

Vertical axis: the value chain, by visibility

Top: the anchor (the user and their need), then what the user sees and cares about directly, such as the app and its lessons.

Bottom: infrastructure the user never sees, such as cloud hosting, databases and SDKs.

Height on the map is visibility, not importance. A hidden component can matter a great deal.

Horizontal axis: evolution

Left: genesis, new and uncertain. It needs exploring.

Right: commodity, standard and well understood. Outsource it.

Position on this axis is a stage, not a date.

Dirt paths and highways

Read the horizontal position as the state of a road. On the left is a dirt path you have to scout; on the right is a highway everyone uses without thinking. A component on a dirt path needs different treatment from one on a highway.

The map is one step in a strategy cycle

The axes and the build, buy or outsource rule are only the start. Wardley places the map inside a strategy cycle, adapted from Sun Tzu: the map is the landscape, and the other steps turn that picture into decisions.

  1. PurposeWhy you are doing this and for whom
  2. LandscapeThe map of your value chain
  3. ClimatePatterns that change the map over time
  4. DoctrineHabits that make any organization better
  5. LeadershipThe moves you choose to make

The moves you make change the landscape, so the cycle starts again from purpose with a new map. Climate and the moves it allows are covered under evolution; doctrine applies to every map.

Doctrine: habits that hold on any map

  • Focus on user needs. Every map starts from the anchor.
  • Use a common language. A shared map lets engineering, design and the business argue about the same picture.
  • Challenge assumptions. Positions on the map are claims; ask for the evidence.
  • Use appropriate methods. Explore genesis with small agile teams, buy products and run commodities for efficiency.

Building a map in five steps

Start from the user and work down the value chain

  1. Start with the user need

    What does the user ultimately want? The user and that need are the anchor, and they go at the top of the map.

  2. List the components that serve the need

    What do you need to deliver it? The app, content, technology, infrastructure, data and people.

  3. Draw the dependencies

    Connect each component to the ones it relies on. The user need depends on the app, the app depends on content and technology, and so on down. Each component sits below whatever depends on it.

  4. Place each component on the evolution axis

    Is it in genesis (new and poorly understood), custom-built (understood well enough to build, but made to order), a product (sold by several providers) or a commodity (standard, often bought as a utility)?

    Judge it by the characteristics in the next section, such as how many providers exist and how well it is understood.

  5. Add movement

    Everything moves right over time. Mark the components that are actively evolving, because that is where the strategic decisions are.

Draw it together, and roughly

Do not aim for a perfect first map. A rough one that reveals a single insight is better than no map, and you can refine it later. Draw it with engineering, design and the business: a map drawn by one person carries one person’s assumptions, and where people disagree about a position, you have found the conversation worth having.

Anchor

Movement
An arrow pointing right, from where a component is to where it is heading

Every component moves from genesis to commodity

Each stage calls for a different way to source and run a component, and the movement shapes the moves you can make

The central idea of Wardley Mapping is that every component evolves in the same direction, from new and uncertain to standard and commodity, driven by supply and demand competition. Where a component sits tells you how to treat it.

1

Genesis

  • Brand new, never done before
  • Uncertain and experimental
  • High failure rate
  • Needs an agile, exploratory approach

Explore: build in-house and tolerate failure

2

Custom-built

  • Proven concept, bespoke implementation
  • Few providers, and expensive
  • A competitive advantage if done well
  • Needs skilled engineers

Build where it differentiates

3

Product (including rental)

  • Several providers, with features converging
  • Off-the-shelf options cover most needs
  • Shrinking competitive advantage
  • The point where you decide to buy or build

Buy or adopt, unless you have a clear reason to differentiate

4

Commodity (including utility)

  • Standardized and interchangeable
  • Providers compete mainly on price
  • No competitive advantage left
  • Users expect it to just work

Outsource or use a utility; do not invest in building it

Direction is predictable, timing is not

You can say which way a component will move, because competition keeps pushing it right, but not when it will get there. The horizontal axis is not a timeline, so treat any forecast of the date a component becomes a commodity as a guess.

What movement means for you

When a component moves right

Switch from building it to buying it. What was your advantage becomes table stakes, because competitors can now buy the same thing.

When a commodity makes something new possible

Commodities become the building blocks of new, higher-order systems. When electricity became a utility, it made radio and television possible. When computing became a utility, for example with Amazon EC2 in 2006, a wave of new online services could be built without owning servers.

Climate: what happens whatever you do

  • Competition drives evolution. Wherever supply and demand competition exists, components move right.
  • Characteristics change as components evolve. The same component needs different methods, skills and contracts at each stage.
  • Practices evolve with the components they serve. As computing became a utility, new ways of running it, such as DevOps, grew up alongside.
  • Success breeds inertia. The more a company earns from the current way of doing things, the harder it resists the next one.

Inertia: why organizations hold on

Inertia is resistance to a change the map says is coming. It comes from past investment, existing contracts, skills built for the old way and revenue that depends on it. A company that earns its money from managed servers, for example, has every reason to resist utility computing. Mark inertia on the map so the team plans for it rather than being surprised by it.

Gameplay: moves the map makes visible

  • Open approaches. Open-sourcing a component speeds its move to commodity, which can undercut a rival who sells it.
  • Sensing engines. Offer a component as a utility and let others innovate on top of it. Usage of your utility shows which of their ideas are spreading, and you commoditize the most successful into new utilities. Wardley calls this pattern ILC: others innovate on your utility, you spot what spreads from its usage and you commoditize it.
  • Exploiting inertia. Industrializing a component that a rival still custom-builds leaves them paying more for the same component than you do, while their inertia slows the switch.

Pioneers, settlers and town planners

Different stages need different kinds of people. Wardley suggests organizing around three groups, each taking work from the one before.

P

Pioneers

Explore the uncharted. They work in genesis, try many things and expect most of them to fail.

S

Settlers

Turn the pioneers’ prototypes into products people understand, moving components from custom-built to product.

T

Town planners

Industrialize products into commodities and utilities that run at scale and low cost.

Worked example

An app that teaches adults to play the piano

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app, listens through the phone’s microphone or a MIDI keyboard and gives feedback on each note. Its anchor is an adult beginner who wants to play songs they love. Build the lesson experience, adopt the rest, watch note detection

Two components high on the map are still early in evolution, and that is where Larkwell can stand apart. The lesson experience is custom-built, so Larkwell builds it in-house and differentiates there. Practice plans are in genesis: nobody yet knows what works, so the team explores with small bets such as Practice Mode rather than a large build.

Everything low on the map is a product or a commodity. Larkwell adopts a cross-platform mobile framework, bills through the app stores or a payment provider and rents cloud hosting. In the middle, song licensing and gamification are products, so standard deals and established patterns are enough, while the lesson library is custom-built and worth curating for quality rather than volume. If engineers are custom-building gamification or payment flows, that effort will not set Larkwell apart.

The movement to watch is real-time note detection. Note-detection libraries are already available off the shelf (for example Spotify’s open-source Basic Pitch, 2022), so this component is moving toward product. Larkwell keeps investing while its own detection differentiates, watches the libraries and adopts one when it matches Larkwell’s accuracy. The map says which way note detection is heading, not when it will get there.

      When a Wardley map fits, and when it does not

      It answers questions about sourcing and change, not about features or users

      Use it when

      • You face a build or buy decision: should you build this or use a service?
      • You are planning a tech stack and deciding where to invest engineering effort
      • You need a competitive strategy and want to see where advantage can come from
      • You want to anticipate disruption: what will commoditize next?
      • You need to show leadership a strategy visually

      Skip it when

      • You need to prioritize features. Use RICE or ICE instead.
      • You need user insights. Use Jobs to Be Done or user research instead.
      • You are in a 30-minute PM interview. Mention the map and what it would show; do not draw a full one.
      • The product is simple and has few components
      • You have not done a basic competitive analysis yet. Start with Porter’s Five Forces.

      Saying it in an interview

      I won’t draw a full map here, but I’d map the value chain to separate the commodities we should outsource from the components still in genesis or custom-built. The components users can see that are still early in evolution are the first place I’d look for a differentiator, and I’d check whether a rival’s inertia gives us an opening too.

      Wardley Mapping usually comes after the strategic choices are made, to decide which capabilities to build, buy or outsource. The guide to choosing a strategy framework shows how the strategy frameworks fit together and when to skip a step.

      Six ways teams misread or misuse a map

      Three are about drawing and reading the map, three about acting on it

      1. Mapping without a user need

        Without an anchor, the map becomes an architecture diagram: a list of technology with no way to judge what is visible or valuable.

        Fix: write down the user and their need first, and place every component below whatever depends on it.

      2. Reading the axes as a 2×2

        Treating the vertical axis as importance, or a corner of the map as the place where advantage lives, turns a map into a quadrant chart and misreads it. Visible, early-stage components are a place to start looking, not a rule.

        Fix: read vertical position as visibility to the user through the value chain and horizontal position as evolution. Judge each component by both, and by what it depends on.

      3. Reading evolution as a timeline

        The horizontal axis shows how evolved a component is, not when it will change. Claiming a component will be a commodity next year turns a sound direction into a false forecast.

        Fix: commit to the direction, keep timing assumptions explicit and watch for signals, such as new providers, falling prices and standard interfaces.

      4. Custom-building commodities

        Building your own authentication, payment processing or hosting spends engineering time on solved problems. The map makes this easy to see: these components sit on the right.

        Fix: outsource or rent anything in the commodity stage and move the people to components that differentiate.

      5. Defending yesterday’s advantage

        When a differentiator evolves from custom-built to product, competitors can buy it off the shelf and the advantage erodes. Past success makes teams hold on anyway, which is inertia at work in your own organization.

        Fix: for each differentiator, ask what happens when it becomes a product. Mark where your team or your rivals will resist the move, and start exploring the next genesis component before then.

      6. Using one method for everything

        Running genesis work to fixed plans and targets smothers it; running commodities as experiments wastes money.

        Fix: match the method to the stage, as doctrine says. Give genesis work room to fail, and hold commodities to cost and reliability targets.

      Check your understanding

      Nine questions on the axes, the stages and how maps change

      1. What does the horizontal axis of a Wardley map show?

        The horizontal axis is evolution: genesis on the left, commodity on the right. Components move left to right as competition spreads them, but the axis shows a stage, not a date.

      2. What does the vertical axis show?

        The top holds the user’s need and what the user sees directly; the bottom holds infrastructure they never see. Each component sits below what depends on it. Height is not importance: a hidden component such as hosting can matter a great deal.

      3. What should you do with a component at the commodity stage?

        A payment provider, a cloud host or an authentication service does the job for less than building your own. You cannot differentiate on a commodity, but you still depend on it, so it stays on the map.

      4. Larkwell’s song licensing sits at the product stage: several publishers offer standard deals. What does the map suggest?

        At the product stage, buying or adopting a standard offer is usually right unless you have a clear reason to differentiate. Publishers already offer licensing deals, so the effort goes into negotiating them well. A utility is the commodity-stage answer, one stage further right.

      5. A team is building its own payment processing and login system. What does the map suggest?

        Payments and authentication are commodities, so building them in-house spends effort on solved problems. The same goes for hosting and build pipelines: anything on the right of the map is better bought or rented, which frees engineers for work users notice.

      6. Larkwell’s real-time note detection is custom-built, and open-source note-detection libraries keep improving. What should the team plan for?

        As a component moves from custom-built to product, rivals can buy an equivalent off the shelf, so the advantage erodes. The map says which way note detection is heading, not when, so the team keeps investing while it differentiates and watches the libraries.

      7. Which statement about evolution is accurate?

        Competition pushes every component toward commodity, so the direction is reliable. When it gets there is not, which is why the horizontal axis is not a timeline. Evolution applies to practices, data and knowledge as well as technology.

      8. In Wardley Mapping, what is inertia?

        Inertia comes from past investment, contracts, skills and revenue tied to the old way. The more successful the current model, the stronger the resistance, so it is worth marking on the map.

      9. Which group turns a product into a commodity or utility?

        Pioneers explore genesis, settlers turn prototypes into products and town planners industrialize products into commodities and utilities that run at scale. Work passes from one group to the next rather than staying with the team that started it.
