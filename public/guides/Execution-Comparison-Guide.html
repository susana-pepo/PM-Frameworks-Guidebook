OKRs, dual-track agile, Scrum, Kanban and Shape Up work at different levels. OKRs set the quarter’s goals, dual-track agile tests which ideas are worth building while the team keeps shipping, and Scrum, Kanban or Shape Up organizes how the team delivers. Only those last three compete: Scrum runs short Sprints from one ordered backlog, Kanban pulls work continuously under a limit on how much is in progress, and Shape Up bets six weeks at a time on shaped pitches.

In short

Pick one framework per layer and one delivery method per team; two teams in the same company can still deliver differently. The numbers to remember: 3–5 objectives with 2–5 key results each, graded from 0.0 to 1.0: committed key results aim for 1.0, and an average of about 0.7 counts as success for aspirational ones. Scrum Sprints last one month or less, often two weeks, for teams of typically 10 or fewer. A Shape Up cycle is six weeks plus a two-week cooldown, with one designer and one or two programmers per bet. Kanban has no iterations; its numbers are the team’s own, tied by Little’s Law: average cycle time equals average WIP divided by average throughput.

One sets goals, one chooses the work and three organize delivery

OKRs
Why. A few measurable goals for a fixed period, usually a quarter, with every key result graded at the end.
Dual-Track Agile
What. Discovery and delivery run in parallel every week in the same team: discovery tests ideas cheaply with users, prototypes and experiments, and delivery builds what discovery has de-risked.
Agile and Scrum
How. Agile is the set of values and principles in the 2001 Manifesto for Agile Software Development; Scrum is one framework that applies them, in Sprints of one month or less with a Sprint Goal each.
Kanban
How. Kanban manages work as a continuous flow: the team shows each step of its workflow on a board, caps the items in progress and starts new work only when a finished item frees a slot.
Shape Up
How. Shape Up fixes the time and lets the scope vary: senior people shape a problem into a pitch with an appetite and choose at the betting table which pitches get the next six-week cycle, and a small team cuts scope to ship on time.

Scrum, Kanban, Shape Up and dual-track agile all shape what a team does each week, so they compare on the same dimensions. Dual-track agile adds discovery to whichever delivery method the team runs. OKRs set goals rather than run the work, so their facts follow the table.

OKRs answer a different question

OKRs sit above all four. They do not tell a team how to run its delivery; they set the outcomes the delivery serves.

The rules behind the picker

Answer the questions in order. A yes at question 1 or 2 names a missing layer, but every team still needs a way to deliver, so carry on until the path names Scrum, Kanban or Shape Up.

  1. Are teams unclear about this quarter’s goals, or pulling in different directions?

    • YesUse OKRs. Set a few objectives with measurable key results, then go on to question 2.
    • NoGo to question 2

  2. Does the team ship features that do not move the metrics?

    • YesUse dual-track agile. Add continuous discovery so ideas are tested before engineering builds them, then answer questions 3 to 5 to choose a delivery method.
    • NoGo to question 3

  3. Does most of the work arrive unpredictably, such as support requests, incidents or maintenance, and need attention before the next planning cycle?

    • YesUse Kanban. Show the workflow on a board, limit the work in progress and pull the next item whenever a slot frees up. Planned feature work can still run in Scrum or Shape Up beside it.
    • NoGo to question 4

  4. Do stakeholders or other teams depend on a steady, predictable delivery rhythm?

    • YesUse Scrum. Short Sprints with a clear goal make delivery predictable and give regular points to inspect and adapt. With more than 10 people, split into several teams.
    • NoGo to question 5

  5. Is the work a meaningful chunk that senior people can shape in advance and a small team can finish alone in six weeks?

    • YesUse Shape Up, with one designer and one or two programmers per bet. In a larger organization, make sure senior people have time to shape pitches, and pilot it with one team before switching everyone.
    • NoUse Scrum as the default.

The symptom tells you which layer to fix

  • “Our team ships features every Sprint, but the metrics don’t move.”

    Dual-track agile and OKRs: the team is busy but not effective, which usually means it is building the wrong things. Add discovery so ideas are tested before they are built, and set outcome-based OKRs so success is measured by results, not by features shipped.

  • “Our teams are working on different priorities and pulling against each other.”

    OKRs: the company needs shared goals. Set a few company objectives, and have each team write OKRs that contribute to them.

  • “Scrum events feel like overhead, and the team is burning out.”

    Fix the Scrum events first: check that each one has a clear purpose and keeps to its timebox. If the team still lacks long stretches of focused building time, try Shape Up, which has fewer scheduled meetings, gives the team more autonomy and leaves a cooldown of unscheduled time after every cycle.

  • “We’re setting up a new product team from scratch.”

    One framework per layer: OKRs for direction, dual-track agile for discovery, and Scrum, Kanban or Shape Up for delivery, chosen by the kind of work. Using them together follows one company that runs all five.

  • “We have 14 engineers on one product, the Daily Scrum runs past half an hour, and nobody can follow what everyone else is doing.”

    Scrum, split into smaller teams: the Scrum Guide describes a Scrum Team as typically 10 or fewer people. Form two teams that share one Product Owner, one Product Goal and one Product Backlog. Scaling frameworks such as LeSS and SAFe exist for coordinating many teams.

  • “Most of our week goes on incoming requests and incidents, so no plan survives.”

    Kanban: with no fixed iteration to protect, the team pulls the next request whenever a slot frees up, and a WIP limit keeps it from starting more than it can finish. Planned feature work can still run in Scrum or Shape Up alongside it.

  • “Everyone asks when their request will be done, and our estimates are always wrong.”

    Kanban, measured: record when each item starts and finishes, and answer with a service level expectation drawn from past cycle times, such as 85% of items within eight days. A time with a probability holds up better than a single estimated date.

  • “Our Scrum team starts every item on the first day of the Sprint and finishes most of them on the last.”

    Keep Scrum and add Kanban’s flow practices: limit the items in progress inside the Sprint and look at the oldest ones in the Daily Scrum, so work finishes throughout the Sprint instead of piling up at the end.

Goals choose what discovery tests, and discovery chooses what delivery builds

Worked example

How Larkwell’s goals, discovery and delivery connect

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), wants more new learners to keep playing after their first week. D7 and D30 retention are the shares of new accounts still active 7 and 30 days after sign-up.

  1. OKRs set the direction

    The company objective, “Make Larkwell the app adult beginners keep playing with”, includes an aspirational key result: D30 retention of new accounts from 9% to 11%. The Onboarding team supports it with its own objective, “Make a new learner’s first week feel like progress”. Two of its key results are D7 retention from 19% to 24% (aspirational) and median time to first note from 4 minutes to under 90 seconds (committed).

    Set the key results before choosing any solution.

  2. Dual-track agile tests the next idea

    To move the team’s D7 key result, its product trio asked whether early lessons should let learners play along. In Sprints 15 and 16 the Onboarding team kept delivering (the onboarding redesign from 7 steps to 3 and the item carried over from Sprint 14) while the trio ran the tests. In 8 interviews, 6 learners wanted to play “with someone”, which meant either live with a friend or along with a recording. The tech lead’s feasibility spike put live two-person sync at 6 months or more and a backing-track version at 3–4 weeks. A clickable prototype of Play Along, the backing-track version, tested with 5 learners, was completed by 4 of them. Play Along joined the Product Backlog for a later Sprint, and live sync was dropped.

    An idea moves on only when the evidence supports it.

  3. Scrum, Shape Up and Kanban deliver

    The Onboarding team, seven people in two-week Sprints, uses Scrum. Its Sprint 14 goal, the Sprint before that discovery work, was to cut the median time to first note from 4 minutes to under 90 seconds, with 28 story points planned against an average velocity of 27 (story points are a practice many Scrum teams use, not part of the Scrum Guide). At the Sprint Review the median had fallen to 1 minute 15 seconds. Five of the six items, 20 points, met the Definition of Done; the unfinished inline microphone and keyboard check earned no points and went back to the Product Backlog.

    The Practice team uses Shape Up. It bet a six-week cycle on the Practice Mode pitch, with one designer and two programmers, aimed at the company’s D30 key result and measured by an A/B test from day one.

    The content team, 8 music educators and 2 producers, uses Kanban, because lessons arrive continuously and each one passes through writing, recording and review. Lessons piled up in front of the producers, so the team cut its WIP from 12 lessons to 8. Throughput held at 4 a week, the average cycle time fell from 15 working days to 10, and the team now says 85% of lessons are published within 13 working days.

    Ship it, then check whether the key result moved.

Two Sprints of tests ruled out a build of six months or more

The interviews, the spike and the prototype settled live sync against the backing track within two Sprints, and only Play Along reached the Product Backlog. The three teams then deliver differently because their work differs. The Onboarding team ships small changes on a steady rhythm, Practice Mode needed one bounded six-week bet, and the content team’s lessons flow continuously through a board.

When an interviewer asks how you run your team’s process, walk through the same layers in the same order, with the North Star Metric or one of its inputs as a key result.

Saying it in an interview

I set quarterly OKRs, with our North Star Metric or one of its inputs as a key result.
The same team runs discovery every week to test which ideas actually move those key results.
Ideas join our one backlog when the evidence supports them, and we build them in two-week Sprints with a clear Sprint Goal.

One delivery rhythm per team

Layering works because each framework answers a different question. Running Scrum and Shape Up on the same team gives two answers to the same question, two calendars and two sets of meetings. Each team chooses one and switches only if its work changes, though different teams in one company can choose differently, as Larkwell’s do. Kanban’s practices are the exception: The Kanban Guide for Scrum Teams keeps every Sprint and event and adds WIP limits and flow measures to them.
