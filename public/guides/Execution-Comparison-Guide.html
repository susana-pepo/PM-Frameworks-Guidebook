OKRs, dual-track agile, Agile and Scrum, and Shape Up are often compared as rivals, but they mostly work at different levels: OKRs set the goal, dual-track agile decides what to build, and Scrum or Shape Up organizes delivery. This guide shows where each one fits, where two of them compete, and how to combine them into one way of working.

In short

Use one framework per layer; only delivery offers a choice. OKRs say why the work matters this quarter, dual-track discovery decides which ideas are worth building, and Scrum or Shape Up delivers them: Scrum for a steady rhythm of short Sprints, Shape Up for six-week bets by small, autonomous teams.

Three levels, four frameworks

One sets goals, one chooses the work and two organize delivery

Why

OKRs

Goals for a fixed period, usually a quarter. Each team sets no more than 3–5 objectives, each with 2–5 measurable key results, and grades every key result from 0.0 to 1.0 at the end. Committed OKRs are meant to be met in full (1.0); for aspirational ones, an average of about 0.7 counts as success.

Level: quarterly planning

What

Dual-Track Agile

Discovery and delivery run in parallel, every week, in the same team. Discovery tests ideas cheaply with users, prototypes and experiments; delivery builds and ships what discovery has de-risked. A product trio of product manager, designer and tech lead leads discovery.

Level: discovery, alongside delivery

How

Agile and Scrum

Agile is the set of values and principles in the 2001 Manifesto for Agile Software Development; Scrum is one framework that applies them. Work happens in Sprints of one month or less, often two weeks. Each Sprint has a Sprint Goal, and the team holds Sprint Planning, a Daily Scrum every working day, a Sprint Review and a Sprint Retrospective inside it.

Level: delivery in Sprints

How

Shape Up

Shape Up fixes the time and lets the scope vary. Senior people shape a problem into a pitch with an appetite. At the betting table they choose which pitches get the next six-week cycle, and a small team builds each one with full autonomy, cutting scope to ship on time. A two-week cooldown follows every cycle.

Level: delivery in cycles

Each framework speaks at its own level

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app, uses all four frameworks this quarter. Here is one sentence from each:

  • OKRs: “This quarter we raise D7 retention from 19% to 24%.”
  • Dual-track: “We tested a Play Along prototype with 5 learners before building it.”
  • Scrum: “Sprint 14: time to first note under 90 seconds; 28 points planned.”
  • Shape Up: “We bet six weeks on the Practice Mode pitch.”

OKRs answer a different question

Where flow-based work fits

For work that arrives unpredictably, such as support, operations or maintenance, the usual alternative to Scrum and Shape Up is a flow-based method such as Kanban (not covered in this guide). It has no fixed iterations: work flows through a board, and limits on work in progress keep the team from starting more than it can finish.

Start from the problem you are solving

Follow the path until it names a framework

  1. Are teams unclear about this quarter’s goals, or pulling in different directions?

    • YesUse OKRs. Set a few objectives with measurable key results, then come back to question 2 to choose how the work runs.
    • NoGo to question 2

  2. Does the team ship features that do not move the metrics?

    • YesUse dual-track agile. Add continuous discovery so ideas are tested before engineering builds them, then choose a delivery method below.
    • NoGo to question 3

  3. Do stakeholders or other teams depend on a steady, predictable delivery rhythm?

    • YesUse Scrum. Short Sprints with a clear goal make delivery predictable and give regular points to inspect and adapt. With more than 10 people, split into several teams.
    • NoGo to question 4

  4. Is the work a meaningful chunk that senior people can shape in advance and a small team can finish alone in six weeks?

    • YesUse Shape Up, with one designer and one or two programmers per bet. In a larger organization, make sure senior people have time to shape pitches, and pilot it with one team before switching everyone.
    • NoUse Scrum as the default. If most of the work is unplanned requests, a flow-based method suits it better.

The path finds the first gap, not the only one

A yes at question 1 or 2 names the layer that is missing, but every team still needs a way to deliver. Keep going until you reach a delivery method, or answer two questions in the picker, which follows the same rules.

Six situations and what to reach for

Find the one closest to yours

  • “Our team ships features every Sprint, but the metrics don’t move.”

    Dual-track agile and OKRs: the team is busy but not effective, which usually means it is building the wrong things. Add discovery so ideas are tested before they are built, and set outcome-based OKRs so success is measured by results, not by features shipped.

  • “Our teams are working on different priorities and pulling against each other.”

    OKRs: the company needs shared goals. Set a few company objectives, and have each team write OKRs that contribute to them.

  • “Scrum events feel like overhead, and the team is burning out.”

    Fix the Scrum events first: check that each one has a clear purpose and keeps to its timebox. If the team still lacks long stretches of focused building time, try Shape Up, which has fewer scheduled meetings, gives the team more autonomy and leaves a cooldown of unscheduled time after every cycle.

  • “We’re setting up a new product team from scratch.”

    One framework per layer: OKRs for direction, dual-track agile for discovery, and either Scrum or Shape Up for delivery.

  • “We have 14 engineers on one product, the Daily Scrum runs past half an hour, and nobody can follow what everyone else is doing.”

    Scrum, split into smaller teams: the Scrum Guide describes a Scrum Team as typically 10 or fewer people. Form two teams that share one Product Owner, one Product Goal and one Product Backlog. Scaling frameworks such as LeSS and SAFe exist for coordinating many teams.

  • “Most of our week goes on incoming requests and incidents, so no plan survives.”

    A flow-based method: with no fixed iteration to protect, the team pulls the next request whenever it has capacity. Planned feature work can still run in Scrum or Shape Up alongside it.

One way of working in three layers

Goals choose what discovery tests, and discovery chooses what delivery builds

Worked example

How Larkwell’s goals, discovery and delivery connect

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app, wants more new learners to keep playing after their first week. D7 and D30 retention are the shares of new accounts still active 7 and 30 days after sign-up. All figures are illustrative.

  1. OKRs set the direction

    The company objective, “Make Larkwell the app adult beginners keep playing with”, includes an aspirational key result: D30 retention of new accounts from 9% to 11%. The Onboarding team supports it with its own objective, “Make a new learner’s first week feel like progress”. Two of its key results are D7 retention from 19% to 24% (aspirational) and median time to first note from 4 minutes to under 90 seconds (committed).

    Set the key results before choosing any solution.

  2. Dual-track agile tests the next idea

    To move the team’s D7 key result, its product trio asked whether early lessons should let learners play along. In Sprints 15 and 16 the Onboarding team kept delivering (the onboarding redesign from 7 steps to 3 and the item carried over from Sprint 14) while the trio ran the tests. In 8 interviews, 6 learners wanted to play “with someone”, which meant either live with a friend or along with a recording. The tech lead’s feasibility spike put live two-person sync at 6 months or more and a backing-track version at 3–4 weeks. A clickable prototype of the backing-track version, tested with 5 learners, was completed by 4 of them. The backing-track version joined the Product Backlog for a later Sprint, and live sync was dropped.

    An idea moves on only when the evidence supports it.

  3. Scrum or Shape Up delivers, one method per team

    The Onboarding team, seven people in two-week Sprints, uses Scrum. Its Sprint 14 goal, the Sprint before that discovery work, was to cut the median time to first note from 4 minutes to under 90 seconds, with 28 story points planned against an average velocity of 27 (story points are a practice many Scrum teams use, not part of the Scrum Guide). At the Sprint Review the median had fallen to 1 minute 15 seconds. Five of the six items, 20 points, met the Definition of Done; the unfinished inline microphone and keyboard check earned no points and went back to the Product Backlog.

    The Practice team uses Shape Up. It bet a six-week cycle on the Practice Mode pitch, with one designer and two programmers, aimed at the company’s D30 key result and measured by an A/B test from day one.

    Ship it, then check whether the key result moved.

Why the order matters

Each layer narrows the one below it. The key results decide which ideas discovery tests and which work delivery takes on, and an idea such as the backing-track version joins the backlog only once the tests support it. The two teams deliver differently because their work differs: the Onboarding team ships small changes on a steady rhythm, while Practice Mode needed one bounded six-week bet. Each team still uses only one delivery method.

When an interviewer asks how you run your team’s process, walk through the same layers from goals to discovery to delivery, with the North Star Metric or one of its inputs as a key result. The answer covers why the work matters, how you choose it and how you ship it.

Saying it in an interview

I set quarterly OKRs, with our North Star Metric or one of its inputs as a key result.
The same team runs discovery every week to test which ideas actually move those key results.
Ideas join our one backlog when the evidence supports them, and we build them in two-week Sprints with a clear Sprint Goal.

Scrum or Shape Up, not both

Layering works because each framework answers a different question. Running Scrum and Shape Up on the same team gives two answers to the same question, two calendars and two sets of meetings. Each team chooses one and switches only if its work changes; different teams in one company can choose differently, as Larkwell’s do.
