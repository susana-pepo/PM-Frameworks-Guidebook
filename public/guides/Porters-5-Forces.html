Five forces shape how profitable an industry is on average: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, and the bargaining power of buyers and of suppliers. The stronger they are, the harder it is for anyone in the industry to earn high returns.

In short

Rate each force Low, Medium or High from evidence in your market, then build the strategy around weakening the strongest. Draw the market where companies compete for the same customers, and re-rate it every 6–12 months, or sooner if the cost of entering changes.

Rivalry is the fight you can see; the other four shape it from outside

Competitive rivalry

More rivals with similar products means lower profits for everyone, because the easiest way to win a customer is a lower price.

Threat of new entrants

When barriers are low, newcomers keep arriving and take share. Even the threat of entry holds prices down.

Threat of substitutes

A substitute meets the same need in a different way, as a private teacher does for a piano app. A competitor offers the same kind of product for the same job. Substitutes cap what you can charge.

Buyer power

With many alternatives and no cost to switching, buyers set the terms and push prices down.

Supplier power

When suppliers are few or their inputs are unique, they control your costs and take a share of your margin.

Where the model comes from

Michael Porter set out the forces in “How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy” (1979) and revisited them in “The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy” (2008). The update keeps the same five. It treats industry growth, technology, government and complements (products that make yours more valuable when used together, such as apps for a phone) as factors that change how strong the forces are, so complements are not a sixth force.

Porter makes a related point elsewhere: strategy means choosing to be different (“What Is Strategy?”, 1996). Lasting profit comes from a set of activities your rivals don’t share, while competing to be the best on the same terms wears profit down for everyone. The forces tell you how much profit the industry leaves, who takes the rest and how much a company in your position can keep; the 1996 article is about standing apart from rivals within that.

What pushes each force up or down

Rate each force Low, Medium or High from the conditions in your market. High means the force takes a large share of the profit, by pushing prices down or costs up. Use the same three levels for every force so the ratings can be compared.

  1. 3HighStrong pressure on prices or costs; the force takes much of the profit
  2. 2MediumNoticeable pressure that a good strategy can manage
  3. 1LowLittle pressure; the force leaves profit in the industry

Few markets are low on all five

Almost every market has at least one high force. The ratings tell you which to address first and which to keep an eye on.

Larkwell’s market: app-based piano learning for adults

Worked example

A crowded subscription market where switching costs nothing

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), listens through the phone’s microphone or a MIDI keyboard and gives real-time feedback on each note. Its product team rated the five forces in that market. On new entrants the evidence pointed both ways, so that rating sits between Medium and High.

Larkwell’s five force ratings
Force Rating Why
Competitive rivalryHighSeveral established subscription apps offer similar lessons and feedback to the same beginners. Low differentiation makes it a crowded fight.
Threat of new entrantsMedium–highBuilding a basic learning app is cheap, so the capital barrier is low and network effects are weak. A deep lesson library (Larkwell has 320 lessons) and song licensing take years, which gives incumbents some protection.
Threat of substitutesHighLearners can meet the same need in other ways: free video tutorials, private teachers (who cost far more per hour than an app) and books. In Jobs to Be Done terms, learners can hire many alternatives for the same job. Free video tutorials are the strongest substitute, because they cost nothing.
Buyer powerHighLearners have many choices and no switching costs: they cancel a monthly plan and download another app. Price sensitivity is high in a consumer market, and public app store reviews make every option easy to compare.
Supplier powerMediumMusic publishers control licensed songs, and app stores take a commission of 15–30%. Lessons built in-house by Larkwell’s music educators, and a growing library of royalty-free music, reduce the dependence.

Rivalry, substitutes and buyers squeeze hardest, so Larkwell answers those first

With new entrants close behind, profit is squeezed from four sides. The analyzer has no Medium–high, but it calls this a tough market whether you enter new entrants as Medium or High.

Differentiate hard to ease rivalry. Go deep where rivals are shallow. Larkwell teaches piano only and competes on the accuracy of its real-time feedback, not on the size of its song library.

Reduce the threat of substitutes. Offer what a free video cannot, such as feedback on each note as the learner plays.

Raise switching costs to weaken buyer power. Saved progress and personalized learning paths give a learner a reason to stay.

Draw a new market for a new question. If Larkwell considers a version for children, that is a different market, with different buyers and substitutes, so it needs its own analysis.

Rate the forces in your market

The analyzer counts Low as 1, Medium as 2 and High as 3, then averages the five ratings. An average of 1.5 or less reads as attractive, up to 2.2 as moderately competitive, and anything higher as tough. It also names the strongest force, or the forces tied for strongest, so you know where to start.

    Back each rating with evidence

    Rate the market as it is today, not as you would like it to be. For each force, write down one piece of evidence, such as a rival’s price cut, your churn rate or a supplier’s new fee. A rating you cannot back up is a guess.

    The Five Forces is for decisions about a whole market

    Use it when

    • You are entering a new market and want to understand its competitive dynamics before committing resources
    • You are running an annual or quarterly strategy review
    • You are setting prices and need to know what the market will bear, and why
    • You are preparing an investor pitch and need to show you understand the competitive landscape
    • You are evaluating a partnership and need to weigh supplier or buyer power

    Skip it when

    • The decision is about a feature, such as whether to add dark mode. Use RICE instead.
    • The market is so early that the forces shift weekly and any analysis expires fast
    • The problem is internal, such as tech debt or team dynamics
    • You need to understand users. The forces describe the market around them; Jobs to Be Done describes what they are trying to get done.

    The Five Forces usually comes first in a strategy analysis. When most forces come out high, Blue Ocean Strategy is the step that looks for market space where they are weaker. Choosing a strategy framework shows how the four strategy frameworks hand off to each other.

    Five ways the analysis goes wrong

    1. Confusing competitors, substitutes and unrelated products

      Listing a music streaming service as a competitor to a piano-learning app. People use it to listen, while the app is for learning to play, so streaming is neither a competitor nor a substitute. For that app, a private teacher is a substitute and another piano app is a competitor.

      Fix: start from the job the customer wants done. A product that does a different job is neither a rival nor a substitute; of the rest, products like yours are rivals and every other route to the same result is a substitute.

    2. Treating the analysis as permanent

      Rating the forces once and never looking again. Entry barriers can change quickly, for example when new tools make an app cheaper to build, and an old analysis will then be wrong about new entrants.

      Fix: re-run the analysis every 6–12 months, or whenever a major market event happens, such as a change in what it costs to enter.

    3. Ignoring supplier power

      Product teams sometimes skip this force because “we build software, we don’t have suppliers.” They do: app stores, cloud providers, content licensors and payment processors. App stores take a commission of 15–30%, depending on the program and, for subscriptions, how long the subscriber has stayed. The rules are changing in several countries.

      Fix: list every external dependency. Each one has bargaining power over you.

    4. Defining the market too broadly

      Analyzing “the education market” instead of “app-based piano learning for adults.” A market drawn too broadly gives meaningless averages; one drawn too narrowly misses threats.

      Fix: define the market at the level where competitors actually compete for the same customers.

    5. Using it for feature decisions

      Trying to use the Five Forces to decide whether to build a metronome feature. The forces rate the market, and they read the same whether you ship the metronome or not.

      Fix: use the Five Forces to decide where to compete. Use RICE or Kano to decide what to build.

    Test yourself on the Five Forces

    1. What does a Five Forces analysis examine?

      Every force is a pressure on price or cost across a whole market, so together they add up to how much profit that market can hold on to. Why people pick a product and stay with it is a question about users, which Jobs to Be Done answers better.

    2. For a piano-learning app, free video tutorials are best classified as…

      Tutorials get a learner to the same goal by another route, and because they’re free they limit what any app can charge. A competitor would have to be an app offering lessons in the same form, such as another piano app.

    3. A PM says supplier power does not apply because the team only builds software. Which of these is a supplier with bargaining power over a subscription app?

      The store sells the app something it can hardly do without, distribution and billing, and sets the price of that through its commission. Subscribers who cancel in one tap put pressure on you too, but from the other side of the sale, as buyers.

    4. High buyer power means…

      Buyer power measures how easily customers can walk away, and plenty of options with nothing lost by leaving lets them push your price down. Raising prices without losing customers is what weak buyer power looks like.

    5. Your team rated the forces 18 months ago. Since then, new tools have made a basic learning app much cheaper to build. What should you do?

      Cheaper tools lower the cost of getting in, so the entry barrier is the rating they’ve moved, and a shift that size is reason to re-rate now instead of waiting for a review. Lowering supplier power is the tempting pick, but a cheaper build works on that barrier. It doesn’t change the bargaining power of the suppliers you already depend on.

    6. Your analysis shows a crowded market where most forces are high. Which framework helps you look for uncontested market space instead?

      Blue Ocean Strategy picks up where a high-pressure reading leaves off, by changing what you compete on so that rivals and substitutes have less hold. Design Thinking explores too, but it explores a user’s problem, not the shape of a market.
