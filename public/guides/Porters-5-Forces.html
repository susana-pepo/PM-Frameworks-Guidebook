Porter’s Five Forces judges how attractive an industry is by looking at the five sources of pressure on its profits. Use it to decide where to compete and which threats your strategy has to answer.

In short

Five forces shape how profitable an industry is on average: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, the bargaining power of buyers and the bargaining power of suppliers. The stronger the forces, the harder it is for anyone in the industry to earn high returns. Rate each one, find the strongest, and build your strategy around weakening it.

Five forces decide how much profit a market leaves

Rivalry is the fight you can see; the other four shape it from outside

1

Competitive rivalry

More rivals with similar products means lower profits for everyone, because the easiest way to win a customer is a lower price.

2

Threat of new entrants

When barriers are low, newcomers keep arriving and take share. Even the threat of entry holds prices down.

3

Threat of substitutes

A substitute meets the same need in a different way, as a private teacher does for a piano app. A competitor offers the same kind of product for the same job. Substitutes cap what you can charge.

4

Buyer power

With many alternatives and no cost to switching, buyers set the terms and push prices down.

5

Supplier power

When suppliers are few or their inputs are unique, they control your costs and take a share of your margin.

Industry structure sets the average profit

Porter’s central point is that the strength of the five forces decides how profitable an industry is on average. The analysis shows where the profit in your industry goes, and how much of it a company in your position can keep.

Where the model comes from

Michael Porter introduced the forces in the Harvard Business Review in 1979, in “How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy,” and revisited them in 2008, in “The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy.” The update keeps the same five forces. It treats industry growth, technology, government and complements (products that make yours more valuable when used together, such as apps for a phone) as factors that change how strong the forces are, so complements are not a sixth force.

Porter makes a related point elsewhere: strategy means choosing to be different (“What Is Strategy?”, 1996). A company earns lasting profit by choosing a different set of activities from its rivals, while competing to be the best on the same terms tends to wear profit down for everyone. The Five Forces tells you how hard the industry squeezes; the 1996 article is about how to stand apart within it.

What pushes each force up or down

Rate each force Low, Medium or High from the conditions in your market

A high rating means the force takes a large share of the profit, by pushing prices down or costs up. Use the same three levels for every force so the ratings can be compared.

Few markets are low on all five

Almost every market has at least one high force. The aim is not to beat all five. It is to see which forces shape your strategic choices, so you know which ones to address first and which ones to watch.

Larkwell’s market: piano-learning apps for adults

A crowded consumer market where four of the five forces run high or close to it

Worked example

App-based piano learning for adults

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app, teaches adults through the phone’s microphone or a MIDI keyboard and gives real-time feedback on each note. Its product team rates the five forces in this market. The ratings are a judgment made to show the method, not a published market study. The threat of new entrants sits between Medium and High; in the analyzer, pick the level the evidence supports more strongly.

The five forces rated for app-based piano learning for adults
Force Rating Why
Competitive rivalryHighSeveral established subscription apps offer similar lessons and feedback to the same beginners. Low differentiation makes it a crowded fight.
Threat of new entrantsMedium–highBuilding a basic learning app is cheap, so the capital barrier is low and network effects are weak. A deep lesson library (Larkwell has 320 lessons) and song licensing take years, which gives incumbents some protection.
Threat of substitutesHighLearners can meet the same need in other ways: free video tutorials, private teachers (who cost far more per hour than an app) and books. In Jobs to Be Done terms, learners can hire many alternatives for the same job. Free video tutorials are the strongest substitute, because they cost nothing.
Buyer powerHighLearners have many choices and no switching costs: they cancel a monthly plan and download another app. Price sensitivity is high in a consumer market, and public app store reviews make every option easy to compare.
Supplier powerMediumMusic publishers control licensed songs, and app stores take a commission of 15–30%. Lessons built in-house by Larkwell’s music educators, and a growing library of royalty-free music, reduce the dependence.

What the ratings tell us

This is a tough market. Three forces are rated High and one Medium–high, so profit is squeezed from several sides. The strategic response follows from the strongest forces.

Differentiate hard to ease rivalry. Go deep where rivals are shallow. Larkwell teaches piano only and competes on the accuracy of its real-time feedback, not on the size of its song library.

Reduce the threat of substitutes. Offer what a free video cannot, such as feedback on each note as the learner plays.

Raise switching costs to weaken buyer power. Saved progress and personalized learning paths give a learner a reason to stay.

Draw a new market for a new question. If Larkwell considers a version for children, that is a different market, with different buyers and substitutes, so it needs its own analysis.

    Back each rating with evidence

    Rate the market as it is today, not as you would like it to be. For each force, write down one piece of evidence, such as a rival’s price cut, your churn rate or a supplier’s new fee. A rating you cannot back up is a guess.

    When the Five Forces fits, and when it does not

    It answers questions about markets, not about features or teams

    Use it when

    • You are entering a new market and want to understand its competitive dynamics before committing resources
    • You are running an annual or quarterly strategy review
    • You are setting prices and need to know what the market will bear, and why
    • You are preparing an investor pitch and need to show you understand the competitive landscape
    • You are evaluating a partnership and need to weigh supplier or buyer power

    Skip it when

    • The decision is about a feature, such as whether to add dark mode. Use RICE instead.
    • The market is so early that the forces shift weekly and any analysis expires fast
    • The problem is internal, such as tech debt or team dynamics
    • You need user insights. The Five Forces looks at market structure, not user needs; use Jobs to Be Done instead.

    The Five Forces is usually the first step of a strategy analysis. The guide to choosing a strategy framework shows how its ratings feed the later steps.

    Five ways the analysis goes wrong

    Most come from drawing the market badly or treating it as fixed

    1. Confusing competitors, substitutes and unrelated products

      Listing a music streaming service as a competitor to a piano-learning app. It serves a different job (listening to music, not learning to play), so it is neither a competitor nor a substitute. A private teacher is a substitute: a different way of meeting the same need. Competitors offer the same kind of product for the same job, such as one piano app against another.

      Fix: start from the job. The same kind of product for the same job is a competitor; a different way of meeting the same need is a substitute; a product for a different job belongs in neither row.

    2. Treating the analysis as permanent

      Rating the forces once and never looking again. Entry barriers can change quickly, for example when new tools make an app cheaper to build, and an old analysis will then be wrong about new entrants.

      Fix: re-run the analysis every 6–12 months, or whenever a major market event happens, such as a change in what it costs to enter.

    3. Ignoring supplier power

      Product teams sometimes skip this force because “we build software, we don’t have suppliers.” They do: app stores, cloud providers, content licensors and payment processors. App stores take a commission of 15–30%, depending on the program and, for subscriptions, how long the subscriber has stayed. The rules are changing in several countries.

      Fix: list every external dependency. Each one has bargaining power over you.

    4. Defining the market too broadly

      Analyzing “the education market” instead of “app-based piano learning for adults.” A market drawn too broadly gives meaningless averages; one drawn too narrowly misses threats.

      Fix: define the market at the level where competitors actually compete for the same customers.

    5. Using it for feature decisions

      Trying to use the Five Forces to decide whether to build a metronome feature. The framework is about market structure, not product features.

      Fix: use the Five Forces to decide where to compete. Use RICE or Kano to decide what to build.

    Check your understanding

    Six questions on the forces and how to use them

    1. What does a Five Forces analysis examine?

      The Five Forces analyzes the competitive structure of an industry, to show where the profit goes and how lasting it is. User motivation, feature order and team throughput are questions for other frameworks.

    2. For a piano-learning app, free video tutorials are best classified as…

      Free video tutorials meet the same need, learning to play, in a different way, which makes them a substitute. Competitors are other piano-learning apps. A music streaming service serves a different job and is neither.

    3. A PM says supplier power does not apply because the team only builds software. Which of these is a supplier with bargaining power over a subscription app?

      The app store takes a commission of 15–30%, and the app cannot reach most phones without it. App stores, cloud providers, content licensors and payment processors are all suppliers. A rival app belongs to rivalry, users who can cancel are buyer power, and free tutorials are substitutes.

    4. High buyer power means…

      When buyers can easily switch and compare options, they push prices down. Pricing freely is a sign of low buyer power, costly inputs belong to supplier power, and few competitors means low rivalry.

    5. Your team rated the forces 18 months ago. Since then, new tools have made a basic learning app much cheaper to build. What should you do?

      The Five Forces is a snapshot, and markets shift as technology changes, competitors enter and leave, and regulation changes. Re-run it every 6–12 months, or after a major market event. Cheaper building lowers the barrier to entry, so the threat of new entrants is the first rating to revisit; check the others while you are at it.

    6. Your analysis shows a crowded market where most forces are high. Which framework helps you look for uncontested market space instead?

      The Five Forces describes the red ocean of competitive pressure. Blue Ocean Strategy is a method for creating market space where those forces are weaker. RICE Score ranks features, User Story Mapping plans releases and Design Thinking explores user problems; none of them looks for new market space.
