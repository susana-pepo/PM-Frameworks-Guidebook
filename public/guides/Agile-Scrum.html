Agile is a set of values and principles for building products in small steps and changing course as you learn. Scrum is one widely used framework that puts them into practice, with a fixed set of accountabilities, events and artifacts that a small team follows Sprint after Sprint.

In short

Every Sprint ends with a usable Increment, and the team inspects and adapts both the product and its way of working at fixed events. The Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer people: one Product Owner, one Scrum Master and the Developers. For a one-month Sprint the Scrum Guide caps planning at 8 hours, the review at 4 and the retrospective at 3; a two-week Sprint usually takes less time on each.

The Manifesto sets values; the Scrum Guide adds eleven fixed parts

Agile

How should we approach building software?

Its values and principles come from the Manifesto for Agile Software Development (Beck et al., 2001), usually called the Agile Manifesto: build iteratively, collaborate with customers and respond to change. It defines no roles, events or timeboxes.

Scrum

What exactly do we do each week?

Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland’s Scrum Guide defines it with three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), five events (the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) and three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment).

If Agile is eating healthily, Scrum is one specific meal plan. Other methods follow the same values with different rules.

Waterfall, before Agile

Plan everything up front, then design, build all of it, test and launch. A release takes months. When requirements change, much of the plan has to be redone, and the team learns whether it built the right thing only at the end.

Agile with Scrum

Plan a little, build a little, ship it, get feedback and adapt, every two weeks or so. Learning happens in every Sprint, and requirements can change between Sprints without throwing work away.

Three accountabilities share one team, and five events set its rhythm

Product Owner

What should we build next, and why?

Accountable for maximizing the value of the product and for managing the Product Backlog. Sets the Product Goal, orders the Product Backlog, makes sure everyone understands the items and represents customers and stakeholders. The Product Owner often writes items or delegates the writing, but stays accountable for them and decides the order.

Owns the what and the why

Scrum Master

Is Scrum helping this team work well?

Accountable for the team’s effectiveness. Coaches the team in Scrum and self-management, removes impediments, makes sure the events happen and stay inside their timeboxes, and protects the team from interruptions. The Guide describes the role as a true leader who serves the Scrum Team and the larger organization; the Scrum Master assigns no tasks.

Owns the process

Developers

How do we turn this into a usable Increment?

Everyone who does the work of each Sprint: engineers, designers, QA and anyone else the product needs. They are self-managing: they create the Sprint Backlog, decide how to deliver the Sprint Goal and hold each other to the Definition of Done.

Owns the how

The whole Scrum Team is typically 10 or fewer people, with no sub-teams and no hierarchy: small enough to stay nimble, large enough to finish meaningful work in a Sprint. A product that needs more people gets several Scrum Teams that share one Product Goal, one Product Backlog and one Product Owner.

Product manager and Product Owner are not the same job

Many product managers hold the Product Owner accountability, so the titles get used interchangeably. Product Owner is an accountability inside Scrum: the Product Goal and the Product Backlog. Product manager is a job, which also covers discovery, strategy, pricing and work with the rest of the business, and some organizations split the two.

The five events

The Sprint is the container for the other four events. It has a fixed length of one month or less, often two weeks, and the next Sprint starts the moment one ends.

The five Scrum events, with the Scrum Guide’s maximum timebox for a one-month Sprint
Event Purpose Who takes part Maximum timebox
The SprintTurns backlog items into at least one usable IncrementThe whole Scrum Team1 month
Sprint PlanningThe Product Owner brings the priorities, the team agrees on a Sprint Goal, and the Developers pick the items and plan the work. Output: the Sprint Goal and the Sprint BacklogThe Scrum Team, plus anyone invited for advice8 hours
Daily ScrumThe Developers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adjust the plan for the next day, in any format they chooseThe Developers15 minutes
Sprint ReviewA working session: stakeholders see the Increment and discuss progress toward the Product Goal and what to do next. Output: an adjusted Product BacklogThe Scrum Team and key stakeholders4 hours
Sprint RetrospectiveCloses the Sprint. The team looks at its people, interactions, tools, process and Definition of Done, and picks the most helpful improvements to act onThe Scrum Team only3 hours

The Guide sets only these maximums, all for a one-month Sprint, and shorter Sprints usually need less. As a rule of thumb, not a Guide figure, many teams with two-week Sprints spend 2–4 hours on planning, 1–2 on the review and 1–1.5 on the retrospective. The Daily Scrum happens at the same time and place every working day, and the 2017 Guide’s three questions (what I did yesterday, what I will do today, what is blocking me) remain one option. The review accepts or rejects nothing: an item is done when it meets the Definition of Done.

Refinement runs alongside the events

In backlog refinement, the Product Owner and the Developers review upcoming items, clarify requirements, split large items and estimate them. It is an ongoing activity rather than one of the events; how much time it takes is up to the team. Well-refined items make Sprint Planning short and calm.

Changing the plan during a Sprint

No changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal. Within that limit the Sprint Backlog is a living plan: as the team learns, the Developers and the Product Owner clarify and renegotiate scope, so new work that serves the goal can come in and the Sprint stays achievable. If the Sprint Goal stops making sense, only the Product Owner can cancel the Sprint.

Three artifacts, each tied to a commitment

The team measures each artifact against its commitment; many teams add user stories and story points on top

Product Backlog

The single ordered list of everything the product might need, owned by the Product Owner. Items near the top are refined: small and clear enough to finish in one Sprint. Items further down are vaguer and further out. The list changes constantly as the team learns.

Commitment: the Product Goal, the long-term objective the backlog works toward

Sprint Backlog

The items selected for this Sprint, plus the Sprint Goal and the plan for delivering them. It belongs to the Developers, who update it throughout the Sprint as they learn more.

Commitment: the Sprint Goal, the one objective for this Sprint

Increment

A usable step toward the Product Goal. Work becomes part of an Increment only when it meets the Definition of Done. Each Increment adds to the ones before it, a team can deliver several in one Sprint, and it can release them before the Sprint ends. The Sprint Review is where they are inspected, not a gate for releasing them.

Commitment: the Definition of Done, the quality bar every item must meet

Writing and sizing backlog items

Many Scrum teams write their items as user stories and size them in story points. Both are complementary practices; the Scrum Guide requires neither.

User stories

A common format for backlog items, written from the user’s point of view.

As auser type I wantaction so thatbenefit

Story
“As a piano beginner, I want to see my progress after each lesson so that I feel motivated to keep practicing.”
Acceptance criteria
The progress bar updates when a lesson is completed. It shows the percentage complete for the current course. It includes a streak counter.

Acceptance criteria are clear, testable conditions that define done for one story. They sit on top of the Definition of Done, which applies to every item.

Story points

Teams estimate with relative sizes rather than hours, often on the Fibonacci numbers 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 and 13. A point blends effort, complexity and uncertainty, so it measures size relative to other items, not time. An 8 is roughly twice a 5; the gaps widen as the numbers grow because larger items hide more unknowns.

  1. 13+Too bigMany teams split it before it enters a Sprint
  2. 8LargeRisky, with real unknowns
  3. 3–5MediumSome unknowns
  4. 1–2SmallClear and well understood

Velocity counts only finished work

Velocity is the total points of the items that met the Definition of Done in a Sprint. There is no partial credit: an unfinished item counts as zero, however close it is, and goes back to the Product Backlog, where the Product Owner orders it again and the Developers re-estimate what is left. After a few Sprints, the average becomes useful for planning. Use it to forecast the team’s own work. Using velocity to judge people leads teams to inflate estimates.

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), teaches through the phone’s microphone or a MIDI keyboard. Its Onboarding team works in two-week Sprints. Sprint Review

Stakeholders tried the new first session on real devices with the team. The median time to first note fell from 4 minutes to 1 minute 15 seconds, inside the goal, and the Android audio bug is fixed. Five items met the Definition of Done, worth 20 points. The inline microphone and keyboard check was mostly built but not done; it goes back to the Product Backlog, to be re-estimated for Sprint 15. The session ended with the Product Owner and stakeholders agreeing on what to do next, which kept the check near the top of the Product Backlog.

The goal was met on a plan that was over capacity

The Sprint Goal was the commitment; the items were a forecast. The Sprint stands or falls on time to first note, and the unfinished check shows only that the forecast was too big.

Velocity is 20, not 28. The microphone and keyboard check was mostly built but earns nothing until it is done. Recording 20 pulls the average down and keeps the next forecast honest.

The plan was 104% of average velocity. 28 points against an average of 27 is more than the team usually finishes, with no room for bugs or surprises. About 22–24 points would have kept a buffer of 10–20%, a common rule of thumb.

The largest item was also the riskiest. The 8-point check carried the most unknowns and is the one that slipped. Split during refinement into a microphone check and a keyboard check, at least one part could have finished.

The retrospective produced one concrete change, which the next retrospective can check.

    Saying it in an interview

    I’d run two-week Sprints, each with one Sprint Goal that moves us toward the Product Goal.
Every Sprint ends with a review, where stakeholders use the working product and help reorder the backlog.
Then the team holds a retrospective and picks the improvements it will act on in the next Sprint.

    Scrum suits a small product team whose requirements will change

    Use it when

    • You have a cross-functional team of 10 or fewer people
    • Requirements are evolving or uncertain
    • You need regular feedback from stakeholders
    • The team needs structure but also room to adapt
    • You are building a software product

    Skip it when

    • The team is only one or two people, where the events can cost more than they return
    • The work is mostly support or maintenance that arrives unpredictably; Kanban pulls each request in as a slot frees up, with no Sprint to protect
    • Projects need weeks of uninterrupted focus on work shaped in advance; Shape Up fixes a six-week cycle and lets the scope vary to fit it
    • You expect Scrum alone to decide what is worth building. It organizes delivery; to test ideas before the team builds them, add dual-track agile alongside it
    • You build hardware or work in a regulated industry, where long lead times or required documentation mean Scrum alone may not be enough

    Most Scrum failures keep the events and lose their purpose

    1. Using velocity to judge performance

      When velocity becomes a target, teams inflate estimates to look productive, and comparisons between teams make no sense because each team sizes points differently.

      Fix: use velocity only for the team’s own planning, and never compare it across teams.

    2. Turning the Daily Scrum into a status report

      People take turns reporting to the Scrum Master or a manager, the meeting becomes a review of the past, and nobody adjusts the plan.

      Fix: the Developers talk to each other about progress toward the Sprint Goal, adapt the plan for the next day, keep it to 15 minutes and take problem-solving to a follow-up conversation.

    3. Running a Sprint with no Sprint Goal

      A list of unrelated items gives the team nothing to protect when trade-offs come, and the review has no single result to show.

      Fix: agree on one Sprint Goal at Sprint Planning and choose the items that serve it.

    4. Giving partial credit

      Counting an item that is “mostly done” overstates velocity and hides the work that is still to come.

      Fix: count only items that meet the Definition of Done, and return the rest to the Product Backlog.

    5. Treating the Sprint Review as a demo for sign-off

      The team presents slides, stakeholders nod and nothing about the backlog changes, so the main chance to learn is lost.

      Fix: invite the people who use or pay for the product, let them try the Increment, and run the review as a working session that ends with an updated Product Backlog.

    6. Frozen or churning scope

      Some teams refuse every change once the Sprint starts, even after they learn the plan is wrong. Others let every urgent request in, nothing comes out, and they miss the Sprint Goal or quietly cut quality.

      Fix: check each change against the Sprint Goal with the Product Owner. Clarify and renegotiate scope as the team learns, and turn away only what would put the goal at risk.

    Test yourself on Scrum

    1. What is the relationship between Agile and Scrum?

      Agile names a philosophy and prescribes no events or accountabilities; Scrum is one concrete way to apply it. They aren’t interchangeable: Kanban and Shape Up follow the same values with different rules, and a team can use either without holding a single Scrum event.

    2. Who is accountable for ordering the Product Backlog?

      Ordering the backlog is part of the Product Owner’s accountability, and it stays with that one person even when others write the items. A senior stakeholder can argue for a different order, at the Sprint Review or anywhere else, but the decision is the Product Owner’s.

    3. What is the Sprint Retrospective for?

      The retrospective turns the team’s attention on itself: how it worked during the Sprint and which changes it will try next. Showing the Increment to stakeholders is the Sprint Review’s job, which comes just before it.

    4. What does velocity measure, and what should it not be used for?

      Velocity adds up the points of Done items, and its job is to help a team forecast its own next Sprints; once it becomes a score for people, the estimates creep up. It shouldn’t be compared across teams either, but the bugs-closed option fails earlier: velocity counts points, not bugs.

    5. Halfway through a Sprint, the CEO asks for an urgent feature. What is the best response?

      The Product Owner weighs the request against the Sprint Goal and, if it fits, renegotiates scope with the Developers to make room. Declining treats the Sprint Backlog as frozen, which the Scrum Guide never asks for, and asking for extra hours hides the cost of the change, which usually shows up later as lower quality.

    6. A Product Owner wants each backlog item to stay tied to who benefits and why. Which common format does that?

      Only the story format names a person and the benefit they get, so the item carries its reason for existing all the way through the Sprint. A bug report or a component spec describes the work without saying who it is for.

    7. A team plans 28 points and finishes every item except one 8-point item, which is mostly built. What is its velocity for the Sprint?

      There is no partial credit, so the 8 points of the unfinished item drop out entirely and 20 remain. Answering 24 gives the item half credit for being mostly built, which is the kind of inflation the rule exists to prevent.

    8. At each Daily Scrum, every Developer reports yesterday’s work to the engineering manager, who then hands out today’s tasks. What is the problem?

      The Daily Scrum belongs to self-managing Developers, who plan their own next day against the Sprint Goal instead of waiting for tasks. The three questions were one optional format in the 2017 Guide, and the 2020 Guide asks for no report to anyone.
