RICE and ICE give each idea a score and rank the list, Value vs Effort places ideas on a 2×2 grid of value against effort, and Kano asks users how they would feel about each feature and sorts the features into types. Which one fits depends on the answer you need and the evidence you can gather in the time you have.

In short

For a quarterly plan with 30 ideas or more, cut the list to about 10 with ICE or Value vs Effort, then rank the shortlist with RICE and take the top 3. RICE earns its 90 minutes only when you have usage data to estimate Reach; without it, ICE gets you a ranking in a third of the time. Kano answers a separate question, which features users expect and which would delight them, and it needs 30 or more survey responses collected over 1–2 weeks.

ICE and Value vs Effort run on judgment; RICE adds usage data and Kano asks users

RICE Score
Which features give the most impact for the effort? Each idea scores Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Reach comes from real usage data, so the ranking rests on more than a guess. It suits a quarterly roadmap of 10 or more features on a mature product with analytics.
Value vs Effort
Which ideas are quick wins and which should we avoid? Rate each idea for value and effort and plot it on a 2×2. Its quadrant (Quick wins, Big bets, Fill-ins or Avoid) suggests a next move rather than a verdict. It’s the most visual of the four, which makes it a good fit for backlog grooming, stakeholder workshops and getting a team to agree in about 45 minutes.
Kano model
Which features are expected, which delight, and which does nobody notice? Users answer a survey about each feature instead of the team scoring it. Their answers sort each one into Must-have (Kano: must-be quality), Performance (one-dimensional quality), Delighter (attractive quality), Indifferent or Reverse. It earns its place in product strategy, where the question is what type of feature to invest in.
ICE Score
Which experiment should we run first? Rate Impact, Confidence and Ease from 1 to 10 on the team’s judgment, multiply the three ratings and sort. Some teams average them instead; the ranking is often the same. Growth teams use it to choose experiments and to triage 20 or more ideas, and it works on an early-stage product that has no data at all.

Three rows of the table are rated. In those rows a fuller bar is better: more objective, better suited to executives, easier to learn.

RICE is the most rigorous way to rank, yet it rates only medium on objectivity. Reach comes from analytics, but Impact, Confidence and Effort are still the team’s estimates. Kano rates high because users supply the answers, though it sorts features into types and never puts them in order.

Each framework uses its own scale for effort, and the scales do not convert into one another. An effort of 3 on a Value vs Effort grid is not 3 person-months in RICE or an Ease of 3 in ICE, so compare scores only within one framework.

The rules behind the picker

The picker runs your three answers through these five questions in order and stops at the first answer that names a framework. It checks the goal before the data, and no path leads to RICE without data to estimate Reach.

  1. Do you need to know what type of feature each idea is, rather than which to build first?

    • YesGo to question 2
    • NoGo to question 3

  2. Can you survey at least 30 users and wait 1–2 weeks for their answers?

    • YesUse Kano. It separates Must-haves, Performance features and Delighters. Hold a 60-minute workshop once the answers are in.
    • NoUse Value vs Effort for now, and send the Kano survey so the answers are ready for the next planning cycle.

  3. Do you need a picture that stakeholders can agree on in the room?

    • YesUse Value vs Effort. Anyone can read a 2×2 grid, and a session takes about 45 minutes. If you have Reach data, rank the strongest candidates with RICE afterwards.
    • NoGo to question 4

  4. Do you need an order today, or are you choosing this week’s experiments?

    • YesUse ICE. Three numbers, multiplied: about 20 ideas in a 30-minute session, and keep the top 10 or so. If you have Reach data, rank those with RICE afterwards.
    • NoGo to question 5

  5. Do you have analytics data to estimate Reach for each idea?

    • YesUse RICE. It is the most rigorous ranking because it uses real user numbers. Plan a 90-minute session.
    • NoUse ICE. Without data, Reach would be a guess, so RICE loses its advantage. ICE ranks on judgment and its Confidence score marks the guesses.

Usage data points to RICE, and a room to align points to Value vs Effort

  • “We have 30 feature requests and need to pick five for next quarter. Our analytics are good.”

    RICE: with usage data and a long list, Reach separates ideas that would otherwise look alike, and a quarter’s plan is worth a 90-minute session.

  • “Our growth team has 15 experiment ideas and needs to choose this week’s tests.”

    ICE: weekly experiments need a fast score, and each test produces the evidence that corrects the guess.

  • “Stakeholders can’t agree on priorities. We need to align them in a workshop.”

    Value vs Effort: everyone understands a 2×2 grid at once, without learning a formula.

  • “We’re building a new product and need to know which features are table stakes and which set us apart.”

    Kano: it is the only one of the four that tells Must-haves apart from Delighters.

  • “We’re an early-stage startup. No analytics, no user base, just a list of ideas.”

    ICE: it needs no data, and the team’s judgment is the best evidence you have at this stage.

  • “We need to present our priorities to the CEO with a clear rationale.”

    RICE: a formula with measured Reach shows how each score was built, which holds up to questioning. Without usage data, show a Value vs Effort grid instead.

  • “Quick backlog grooming with the team: 20 minutes at most.”

    Value vs Effort: put sticky notes on the grid and read the quadrants. A rough sort fits in 20 minutes; a full session with discussion takes about 45.

  • “Users are churning, and we don’t know whether we’re missing basics or lacking features that impress.”

    Kano: the survey shows whether Must-haves are broken or Delighters are missing.

Chain them only when the stakes are high

Most decisions need one or two of the four, and running all of them every time costs more than it returns. Save the full sequence for a high-stakes, high-ambiguity decision such as a quarterly plan with a long list. Choosing what to build this sprint doesn’t need it.

A pipeline for big planning decisions

The first pass hands RICE a shorter list, and the grid can present the result at the end.

  1. ICE or Value vs EffortFirst pass: cut 30 ideas to about 10, by score or on the grid
  2. RICERank the shortlist of about 10 with Reach data and take the top 3 into the plan
  3. Value vs EffortOptional: plot the ranked shortlist on the grid to present it, since stakeholders read quadrants faster than scores

Kano runs alongside the pipeline, not as a step between ICE and RICE. Add it when you need to know how each shortlisted feature affects satisfaction. A Kano survey works best with 8–12 features, so it suits the shortlist of about 10 but not the full 30. A missing Must-have moves to the front whatever its score, and RICE then ranks the rest within each type.

Common pairings

ICE, then RICE

ICE filters a long list fast, and RICE ranks the survivors with Reach data. Best for large backlogs.

Kano alongside RICE

Kano says what type each feature is; RICE says which ones to build within each type. Each answers a question the other cannot.

Value vs Effort, then RICE

The grid gets stakeholders to agree on a first sort, and RICE then puts the strongest candidates in a precise order.

Kano, then Value vs Effort

Kano sorts features by type. Plot the Performance features on a Value vs Effort grid and take the Quick wins into the sprint.
