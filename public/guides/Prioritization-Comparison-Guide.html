RICE, Value vs Effort, the Kano model and ICE all help you decide what to build next, but each answers a different question and asks for different evidence. This guide sets them side by side so you can pick the one that fits your data, your time and the decision in front of you.

In short

Pick by the evidence you have and the answer you need. ICE and Value vs Effort run on the team’s judgment and take under an hour; RICE needs usage data and gives the most rigorous ranking; Kano needs a user survey and tells you what kind of feature each idea is. Most decisions need one or two of them, not all four.

Four frameworks for four different questions

Two rank ideas, one sorts them into a grid and one sorts them by type

R × I × C ÷ E

RICE Score

Which features give the most impact for the effort?

The most rigorous way to rank, because Reach comes from real usage data rather than a guess.

Best for quarterly roadmap planning with 10 or more features, on a mature product with analytics.

Speed: slow · Rigor: high

2×2 grid

Value vs Effort

Which ideas are quick wins and which should we avoid?

The most visual: rate each idea for value and effort and plot it on a 2×2. Its quadrant (Quick wins, Big bets, Fill-ins or Avoid) suggests a next move, not a verdict.

Best for aligning a team in about 45 minutes, backlog grooming and stakeholder workshops.

Speed: fast · Rigor: low

Survey, then sort

Kano model

Which features are expected, which delight, and which nobody notices?

The most user-centered: instead of the team scoring ideas, users answer a survey about each one.

The answers sort each feature into one of five types: Must-have (Kano: must-be quality), Performance (one-dimensional quality), Delighter (attractive quality), Indifferent or Reverse.

Best for product strategy, when you need to know what type of feature to invest in.

Speed: slowest · Rigor: high

I × C × E

ICE Score

Which experiment should we run first?

One of the fastest ways to score: rate Impact, Confidence and Ease from 1 to 10 on the team’s judgment, multiply the three ratings and sort. Some teams average them instead; the ranking is often the same.

Best for growth experiments, rapid triage of 20 or more ideas and early-stage products with no data.

Speed: fastest · Rigor: lowest

Pick by what you need most

Use ICE when you need speed, Value vs Effort when you need a visual, RICE when you need precision and Kano when you need to understand users.

RICE rates medium on objectivity, not high. Reach comes from analytics, but Impact, Confidence and Effort are still the team’s estimates. Kano rates high because users, not the team, supply the answers.

Each framework uses its own scale for effort, and the scales do not convert into one another. An effort of 3 on a Value vs Effort grid is not 3 person-months in RICE or an Ease of 3 in ICE, so compare scores only within one framework.

Speed falls as evidence rises

From fastest to slowest the order is ICE, Value vs Effort, RICE, Kano. ICE and Value vs Effort run on judgment, RICE needs analytics and Kano needs answers from users. RICE is the most rigorous way to rank; Kano gathers the most direct user evidence, but it sorts features into types instead of ranking them. Match the framework to the stakes of the decision.

Start from the question you need answered

Follow the path until it names a framework

  1. Do you need to know what type of feature each idea is, rather than which to build first?

    • YesGo to question 2
    • NoGo to question 3

  2. Can you survey at least 30 users and wait 1–2 weeks for their answers?

    • YesUse Kano. It separates Must-haves, Performance features and Delighters. Hold a 60-minute workshop once the answers are in.
    • NoUse Value vs Effort for now, and send the Kano survey so the answers are ready for the next planning cycle.

  3. Do you need a picture that stakeholders can agree on in the room?

    • YesUse Value vs Effort. Anyone can read a 2×2 grid, and a session takes about 45 minutes. If you have Reach data, rank the strongest candidates with RICE afterwards.
    • NoGo to question 4

  4. Do you need an order today, or are you choosing this week’s experiments?

    • YesUse ICE. Three numbers, multiplied: about 20 ideas in a 30-minute session. If you have Reach data, rank the top 10 with RICE afterwards.
    • NoGo to question 5

  5. Do you have analytics data to estimate Reach for each idea?

    • YesUse RICE. It is the most rigorous ranking because it uses real user numbers. Plan a 90-minute session.
    • NoUse ICE. Without data, Reach would be a guess, so RICE loses its advantage. ICE ranks on judgment and its Confidence score marks the guesses.

When the path does not fit

Look for a situation like yours in the scenarios, or answer three questions in the picker. The picker follows the same rules as this path.

Eight situations and the framework for each

Find the one closest to yours

  • “We have 30 feature requests and need to pick five for next quarter. Our analytics are good.”

    RICE: with usage data and a long list, Reach separates ideas that would otherwise look alike, and a quarter’s plan is worth a 90-minute session.

  • “Our growth team has 15 experiment ideas and needs to choose this week’s tests.”

    ICE: weekly experiments need a fast score, and each test produces the evidence that corrects the guess.

  • “Stakeholders can’t agree on priorities. We need to align them in a workshop.”

    Value vs Effort: everyone understands a 2×2 grid at once, without learning a formula.

  • “We’re building a new product and need to know which features are table stakes and which set us apart.”

    Kano: it is the only one of the four that tells Must-haves apart from Delighters.

  • “We’re an early-stage startup. No analytics, no user base, just a list of ideas.”

    ICE: it needs no data, and the team’s judgment is the best evidence you have at this stage.

  • “We need to present our priorities to the CEO with a clear rationale.”

    RICE: a formula with measured Reach shows how each score was built, which holds up to questioning. Without usage data, show a Value vs Effort grid instead.

  • “Quick backlog grooming with the team: 20 minutes at most.”

    Value vs Effort: a quick, visual method. Put sticky notes on a grid and read the quadrants; a rough sort fits in 20 minutes, while a full session with discussion takes about 45.

  • “Users are churning, and we don’t know whether we’re missing basics or lacking features that impress.”

    Kano: the survey shows whether Must-haves are broken or Delighters are missing.

Chain them only when the stakes are high

A first pass narrows the list, then RICE puts it in order

A pipeline for big planning decisions

For an important decision, such as a quarterly plan with a long list and a lot of uncertainty, run the frameworks in sequence. The first pass hands RICE a shorter list, and the grid can show the result.

  1. ICE or Value vs EffortFirst pass: cut 30 ideas to about 10, by score or on the grid
  2. RICERank the shortlist of about 10 with Reach data and take the top 3 into the plan
  3. Value vs EffortOptional: plot the ranked shortlist on the grid to present it, since stakeholders read quadrants faster than scores

Kano runs alongside the pipeline, not as a step between ICE and RICE. Add it when you need to know how each shortlisted feature affects satisfaction. A Kano survey works best with 8–12 features, so it suits the shortlist of about 10 but not the full 30. A missing Must-have moves to the front whatever its score, and RICE then ranks the rest within each type.

Common pairings

ICE, then RICE

ICE filters a long list fast, and RICE ranks the survivors with Reach data. Best for large backlogs.

Kano alongside RICE

Kano says what type each feature is; RICE says which ones to build within each type. Each answers a question the other cannot.

Value vs Effort, then RICE

The grid gets stakeholders to agree on a first sort, and RICE then puts the strongest candidates in a precise order.

Kano, then Value vs Effort

Kano sorts features by type. Plot the Performance features on a Value vs Effort grid and take the Quick wins into the sprint.

One or two is usually enough

Running all four for every decision costs more than it returns. Most situations need one or two. Save the full pipeline for high-stakes, high-ambiguity quarterly planning, not for choosing what to build this sprint.
