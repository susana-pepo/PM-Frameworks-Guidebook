CIRCLES is a fixed order of seven spoken steps for a “design a product for X” prompt: settle on one user and one need before you propose anything, then put three options side by side and recommend one. Lewis C. Lin set it out for PM interviews, and the same order helps when a stakeholder asks you to build something before anyone has framed the problem.

In short

The seven steps take about 20 minutes. List gets the most, about 5; Comprehend and Evaluate get about 3 each. In a 35-minute round that leaves about 15 minutes for follow-up questions (the minute plan). End on one metric that would show the option you picked worked.

Each step depends on the one before it

A product design prompt is left vague on purpose. Whether the asker is an interviewer or a stakeholder, they want to watch you get from that prompt to a clear recommendation. With the order fixed, your attention goes to the problem instead of to what to say next.

Skipping ahead breaks the chain. You can’t rank needs until you know whose needs they are, and you can’t weigh solutions until you have more than one. A solution proposed in the first minute has no user or need to be judged against.

The figure groups the steps by what they do to the problem, and each group hands the next one something specific. The four narrowing steps end with one user segment and one need, written as a problem rather than a feature. List, the only step that widens, turns that need into three candidates of different scope, so there is something to compare. Evaluate and Summarize close the comparison into a decision: the option you pick, what it costs you, the metric that would show it worked and a small test to run before you build the full version.

CIRCLES and design thinking follow a similar path, from users to needs to solutions, but they were developed separately and do different jobs. CIRCLES structures a spoken answer of about 20 minutes with no real users, so the users and needs in it are assumptions you state out loud. Design thinking is a research and prototyping process with real users that runs for days or weeks.

The detective’s order

If the letters will not stick, remember how a detective works: understand the scene, identify the person, discover the problem, narrow the lead, explore the options, weigh the evidence and present the case.

What to do and say at each step

  1. Comprehend the situation

    What exactly are we solving?

    Clarify the prompt before you touch any solution. Pin down the goal, the company and its context, the constraints (time, budget, platform) and what success would look like.

    Say: “Before I jump in, let me make sure I understand the problem.”

    Questions to ask:

    • What metric are we optimizing for?
    • Is this mobile, web or both?
    • Who is the target audience?
    • What is the timeline?
    • Are competitors already doing this?

  2. Identify the customer

    Who are we building for?

    List 2–3 user segments. For each, note their context, their goals and how comfortable they are with technology. Then pick one to focus on and say why. You cannot design for everyone, so choose.

    Say: “Let me think about who uses this.”

    Template: “I see three types of users: [A], [B] and [C]. I’ll focus on [A] because they feel the most pain and are the largest segment.”

  3. Report the customer’s needs

    What are their pain points?

    List the 3–5 biggest needs or pain points of the user you chose, and rank them by how severe and how frequent they are. Ground each one in a concrete situation the user is in, say which ones you would check against data, and write it as “They need to [verb] because [reason].”

    Say: “For this user, the biggest pain points are…”

    Needs, not features: “They need to find friends easily” is a need. “They need a search bar” is a solution in disguise.

  4. Cut through prioritization

    Which need do we solve first?

    Pick one pain point and give a clear reason: it has the highest impact, affects the most users, is the most urgent, fits the company’s goals or closes the biggest gap with competitors.

    Say: “If I had to pick one, I’d focus on…”

    Template: “I’ll focus on [need] because it affects the most users and directly moves [metric].”

  5. List solutions

    How could we solve this?

    Generate three solutions: one simple and fast, one medium, one ambitious. Describe each briefly in terms of what the user sees and does. Do not judge them yet; this is the creative step.

    Say: “Here are a few ways we could solve this.”

    Template: “Quick win: [simple]. Better: [medium]. Best: [ambitious].” The range shows you can trade scope against ambition, which a single idea cannot.

  6. Evaluate tradeoffs

    Which solution wins, and why?

    Compare the options on user impact, implementation effort, risk and fit with the goal. Pick a winner and say what you are giving up. Naming what you give up is what separates a recommendation from a preference.

    Say: “Comparing these options…”

    Template: “I’d go with [B] because it delivers [X impact] for [Y effort]. [A] is faster but does not solve the core problem. [C] is ideal in the long run but too risky for now.”

  7. Summarize your recommendation

    What do we build, and how will we know it worked?

    Recap in about 30 seconds: who the user is, which problem you are solving, the solution you recommend, why it wins and how you would measure success. Add the next step, usually a small test.

    Say: “To wrap up…”

    Template: “We’re solving [problem] for [user] by building [solution]. I’d measure success with [metric] and test it with [MVP approach].”

From “elderly users” to three preset buttons

Worked example

“Design a feature for elderly users to use ride-sharing apps more easily.”

  1. Comprehend the situation

    Clarify: is this for an existing app such as Uber or Lyft, or a new product? What counts as “elderly”? Are we focused on booking, navigation or the whole journey?

    Say: “I’ll assume we’re designing for Uber, for users aged 70 and over, and focusing on booking, since that is where I expect this group to hit the most friction.”

  2. Identify the customer

    Three segments: (A) tech-comfortable seniors who use a smartphone every day, (B) reluctant tech users who stick to basic apps, and (C) seniors who do not use technology and depend on family for help.

    Say: “I’ll focus on group B, the reluctant tech users. They have a smartphone but find complex interfaces overwhelming. I’d expect them to be a large group with the most room to improve, and I’d check that against usage data.”

  3. Report the customer’s needs

    The top pain points: (1) booking takes too many steps, (2) small text and a confusing map, (3) anxiety about getting into a stranger’s car, (4) difficulty communicating with drivers, and (5) no easy way for family to follow a trip.

    Say: “These users need to book without working through many screens, to read what is in front of them, to feel safe with a driver they do not know, and to keep their family in the loop.”

  4. Cut through prioritization

    Pain point 1, too many steps, is the gateway problem. If they cannot book the ride, nothing else matters.

    Say: “I’ll focus on booking friction because it blocks everything after it. If more of these users complete a first ride, they have a reason to come back, so this is where retention starts.”

  5. List solutions

    (A) One-tap favorites: save frequent destinations such as the doctor, the grocery store and home, with one large button for each. (B) Voice booking: say “Take me to Dr. Smith” to book. (C) Family booking: a relative schedules rides remotely from their own app.

    Say: “I see three options, from a simple interface change to voice input to coordinating two users: one-tap favorites, voice booking and family booking.”

  6. Evaluate tradeoffs

    Three solutions for reluctant tech users, compared
    OptionScopeStrengthWeakness
    A. One-tap favoritesQuick winEasiest to build and test; low effort with a clear impactCovers only destinations the user has saved
    B. Voice bookingMediumNo screens to work throughVoice interfaces are hard to get right and error-prone for this group
    C. Family bookingAmbitiousBuilds on the help many of these users already get from familyNeeds a second person to adopt the feature

    Say: “I’d recommend one-tap favorites as the MVP. It removes the core friction with the least complexity, and we can add voice and family booking later as the user base grows.”

  7. Summarize your recommendation

    Recap the chain in one breath: user, problem, solution, metric, test.

    Say: “We’re solving ride-booking friction for reluctant tech users aged 70 and over by adding one-tap favorite destinations: large buttons for Home, Doctor and Grocery that book a ride in one tap. I’d measure success with the first-ride completion rate for users aged 70 and over and with weekly active bookings. For the MVP, I’d add three preset buttons to the home screen and test them with 500 users in one city for four weeks.”

The answer narrows, widens, then chooses

It opens on every elderly rider, closes in on reluctant tech users and then on booking friction alone, opens out to three solutions of different size, and settles on the simplest one that removes the friction. It ends on a number someone could check: first-ride completion for users aged 70 and over.

CIRCLES fits a design prompt nobody has framed yet

Use it when

  • A PM interview asks you to “design a product or feature for X”
  • A stakeholder says “We should build X” and you want to think the request through out loud before committing to research or a build
  • You need a quick first framing of an unfamiliar problem before any research starts
  • You are teaching junior PMs how to approach product design

Skip it when

  • You already understand the problem well and only need solutions
  • You are fixing an urgent bug or outage, which is no time for a structured exercise
  • You are choosing between features you already have. Use RICE or ICE instead.
  • The work is technical design, such as architecture or APIs, which needs its own design methods
  • The question is behavioral (“Tell me about a time when…”). Use the STAR Method instead.
  • The question is a business case (“Should we enter this market?”). Lead with the answer and grouped reasons, as the Pyramid Principle does.
  • You need evidence of what real users need. CIRCLES runs on stated assumptions; run design thinking or Jobs to Be Done research instead.

Six ways a CIRCLES answer goes wrong

Half of them happen before a single solution is on the table

Candidates often rush the first two steps, and that is where many answers go wrong.

  1. Skipping Comprehend

    Jumping straight to solutions. The interviewer left the prompt open to see whether you would pin it down, and an answer that skips the questions shows you would not.

    Fix: ask 3–5 questions before anything else, then restate the problem in one sentence. The restatement also buys you a minute to think.

  2. Designing for everyone

    Not picking a specific user. An answer meant for everyone can’t say whose pain it removes, so none of its later choices can be checked.

    Fix: name 2–3 segments, pick one and give your reason in a sentence: “I’ll focus on segment B because…”

  3. Listing solutions as needs

    “They need a reminder notification” is a solution. “They forget to log a workout until the day is over” is the need, and it leaves room for answers that are not notifications.

    Fix: write needs as “They struggle to…” or “They need to [verb]…”, and never name an interface element in the Report step.

  4. Offering only one solution

    Going straight to your best idea makes it look as if you cannot think broadly. Interviewers want to see range.

    Fix: put three on the table, from a quick win to an ambitious bet, even when you already know which one you will pick.

  5. Evaluating without tradeoffs

    “Solution B is the best” with no comparison gives the listener no way to check your reasoning or push back on it.

    Fix: set each option’s gain against its cost before you pick: “B has the bigger impact but takes a quarter to build. A ships in two weeks and leaves part of the problem open.”

  6. Summarizing without a metric

    A strong recommendation with no way to measure it leaves the obvious question open: how would you know it worked?

    Fix: close on one number you would watch and the smallest test that could move it. In the ride-sharing answer, that is first-ride completion for users aged 70 and over, tested with 500 users in one city.

A 35-minute plan for a product design question

Product design rounds vary in length; this plan assumes a common 35-minute slot for one question. For a longer or shorter round, scale each step in proportion and keep List as the longest. Don’t linger on any one step.

  1. 0–3 min

    Comprehend the situation

    Listen to the prompt and ask 3–5 clarifying questions. Restate your understanding: “So I’m designing [X] for [Y] to achieve [Z].”

  2. 3–5 min

    Identify the customer

    Name 2–3 user segments and pick one: “I’ll focus on [segment] because [reason].” Pause for the interviewer to agree.

  3. 5–8 min

    Report the customer’s needs

    List 3–5 pain points for the user you chose, each tied to a concrete situation: “When they try to [action], they struggle because…”

  4. 8–10 min

    Cut through prioritization

    Pick one pain point with a brief justification: “I’ll focus on [need] because it has the highest impact on [metric].”

  5. 10–15 min

    List solutions

    The creative part of the answer, and the longest step. Describe three solutions in enough detail that the interviewer can picture them.

  6. 15–18 min

    Evaluate tradeoffs

    Compare the solutions on impact and effort, and state the tradeoff plainly: “I recommend [B] because [reasoning]. The tradeoff is [what you give up].”

  7. 18–20 min

    Summarize your recommendation

    A 30-second recap: user, problem, solution, metric, MVP. Keep it crisp and confident.

  8. 20–35 min

    Follow-up discussion

    The interviewer probes your choices. Be ready to defend them or change course: “If we weigh [X] more heavily, I would shift to…”

Habits interviewers notice

  • Signal each step out loud: “Now let me identify the users…” It shows structure and lets the interviewer follow your thinking.
  • Check in after step 2: “Does this user focus make sense, or would you like me to explore a different segment?” It shows you collaborate.
  • Internalize rather than memorize. Interviewers can spot a rehearsed answer. Practice until the order feels natural instead of scripted.

What interviewers are scoring

Signals strength

  • Asking clarifying questions
  • Choosing a specific user
  • Explaining tradeoffs
  • Mentioning metrics
  • Structured thinking

Signals weakness

  • Jumping to solutions
  • Designing for “everyone”
  • Offering only one idea
  • No tradeoffs
  • No success metrics
  • A robotic, memorized delivery

Test yourself on CIRCLES

  1. You have just heard a product design prompt. What should you do first?

    You can’t judge segments or sketches until you know what success means and what limits apply, so the questions come first. Listing segments feels like progress, but which segments matter depends on the goal you have not asked about yet.

  2. “They need a search bar.” Is this a need or a solution?

    A search bar is one possible answer to the user’s problem, which is that they can’t find what they came for. “Both” is tempting because users do ask for features, but a feature request is a clue to the need behind it, and you still have to name that need.

  3. How many solutions should you put forward in the List step?

    Three options of different size give the interviewer a real comparison. A single idea, however well argued, has nothing to be weighed against, and a long list eats the minutes Evaluate needs.

  4. What makes an Evaluate step convincing?

    A choice convinces when the listener can see what it costs next to the alternatives. Build time is one of those costs, so estimating it alone leaves out user impact, risk and fit with the goal.

  5. How should your summary end?

    A number and a small test tell the interviewer how you would find out whether you were right. Recapping every option only repeats what you said in Evaluate. Risks are worth raising, but they belong in the tradeoffs, before you choose, rather than trailing after the summary.

  6. A month into a new PM job, you inherit a backlog of 20 feature ideas, each with usage data. Which framework ranks them?

    Ranking 20 ideas needs a score for each one on a common scale. RICE can take Reach from the usage data you already have, and ICE gives the same kind of score without Reach. Running CIRCLES 20 times would give you 20 separate designs with no way to order them, and Kano sorts features by their effect on satisfaction without ranking them.

  7. Which step should get the most time in a 35-minute interview?

    Turning one need into three concrete ideas takes longer than any other step, about 5 of the 20 minutes. Comprehend feels as if it should be longest because everything rests on it, but a few sharp questions fit in about 3.

  8. A colleague calls CIRCLES “design thinking squeezed into 20 minutes.” What is the main difference between the two?

    CIRCLES runs in one sitting on assumptions you say out loud, while design thinking goes out and tests ideas with real people over days or weeks. Evaluate compares options out loud; it never prototypes or tests anything with users. Nor is CIRCLES a faster run of Define and Ideate: the two were developed separately, for different purposes.
