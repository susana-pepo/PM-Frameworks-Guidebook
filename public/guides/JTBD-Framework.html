Jobs to Be Done treats a purchase as a hire: people bring in a product to make progress in a particular situation, and fire it when something else serves them better. Your real competition is everything else they could hire for that job, including doing nothing.

In short

Keep every product and feature out of the job story. A quick test: if the sentence would have made sense decades ago, it describes a real job. To find that job, interview 8–12 people who switched recently and listen for the four forces, since people switch only when frustration with the old way (Push) and the draw of the new one (Pull) outweigh their worries (Anxiety) and their comfort with the present (Habit).

People hire products to make progress

For a product team, the useful question changes from “what features should we add?” to “what job is the user hiring this product to do?”

The idea has an old shorthand, usually credited to the marketing professor Theodore Levitt, who in turn credited Leo McGinneva: people don’t want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. The drill is hired for the hole, and if something else made the hole more easily, people would fire the drill and switch. Your product is in the same position: users want the progress, and you’re only the best candidate they’ve found so far.

Product thinking asks

  • What features does our product have?
  • What do users do in our app?
  • How can we improve our product?

Jobs thinking asks

  • What progress is the user trying to make?
  • What did they fire to hire us?
  • Why would they switch away?

The milkshake study

Clayton Christensen and his colleagues studied why people bought milkshakes from a fast-food chain in the morning, a case they describe in their book Competing Against Luck (2016). Demographics were no help: the buyers had nothing in common. Their job was the same, though. They faced a long, boring commute and wanted something that lasted about 20 minutes, could be consumed with one hand, made the drive less dull and kept them full until lunch.

Seen that way, the milkshake’s competitors were bananas, bagels, boredom and Snickers bars. Once you see the job, you see the real competition, and it is often not who you think.

Three dimensions of every job

A job is rarely only a task. People also want to feel a certain way and to be seen a certain way while they get it done, and those two sides often weigh more in the decision to buy than the task does.

Functional

What practical task needs doing?

“Get from A to B.” “Learn a song.”

Emotional

How do they want to feel?

“Feel confident.” “Reduce anxiety.”

Social

How do they want to be seen?

“Look like a good parent.” “Seem professional.”

Write the job as a story

A job story fits a job into one sentence: “When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].” The opening words of each clause are fixed, and the rest is yours to fill in.

The format came from Paul Adams and the product team at Intercom as an alternative to persona-based user stories, and Alan Klement later developed it further.

Three job stories

Four rules for a good job story

  1. Solution-free. No product names, no features, no UI elements.
  2. Longer-lived than your product. The underlying job is stable over long periods; it existed before your product and will exist after it. What changes is the situation people meet it in and the ways they get it done.
  3. Grounded in a situation. “When I…” ties the job to a real moment.
  4. Open to emotional and social outcomes. “so I can feel confident” is as valid as “so I can save 10 minutes.”

Job stories and job statements

You will also meet the job statement from Tony Ulwick’s Outcome-Driven Innovation. It has a different shape: a verb, an object and a contextual clarifier, such as “listen to music while commuting” or “get to a hotel in an unfamiliar city,” with no When, I want to or so I can. A job story and a job statement can describe the same job; the statement is terser and suits quantitative research, while the story keeps the situation and the feeling. The two names aren’t interchangeable. The examples and the builder here use job stories.

The competition is wider than the category

Larkwell competes with other piano apps, and also with whatever else fills 15 free minutes. Starbucks is up against a walk or a nap as much as the café across the street.

The assumptions describe the product; the job stories describe the person, which is where new ideas and real competitors come from.

      Jobs to Be Done is a discovery tool

      Use it when

      • You are defining product strategy and asking what business you are really in.
      • Users are churning and you don’t know why.
      • You are entering a new market and need to know who the real competitors are.
      • You are building messaging or positioning around the progress users care about.
      • You are evaluating an acquisition or partnership.
      • A PM interview asks “How would you improve X?” Frame the job first, then structure the rest of the answer with the CIRCLES Method.

      Skip it when

      • You need to prioritize a known backlog. Use RICE or ICE instead, scoring the features against the job. The Kano model can classify the features that serve a job by how they affect satisfaction, but it does not rank them.
      • You need specific UI decisions. Run user testing instead.
      • You are short of time. Sprint planning is not the moment for JTBD research.
      • The job is obvious and the whole team already understands it.
      • You are optimizing rather than discovering. Run an A/B test instead.

      Six ways JTBD work goes wrong

      Most of them come from drifting back toward the product or the persona

      1. Writing solutions instead of jobs

        “When I open the app, I want to see my dashboard, so I can track my progress” is a feature request phrased as a job. The job underneath it might be “When I’m trying to build a new habit, I want to see evidence that I’m improving, so I can stay motivated,” which exists whether or not your app does.

        Fix: remove all product and feature language, then test the job story: would it have made sense decades ago? If so, it describes a real job.

      2. Staying too abstract

        “I want to live a better life” is true but useless for product decisions. A job story needs a specific situation.

        Fix: always include “When I…”. The situation makes the job actionable: “When I get home exhausted” is far more useful than “I want to relax.”

      3. Ignoring emotional and social jobs

        Focusing only on the functional task. “Get from A to B” cannot explain why someone chooses a BMW over a Toyota.

        Fix: for every functional job, ask how the user wants to feel and how they want to be seen. Those answers often drive the decision.

      4. Defining the job by who the customer is

        “Millennials want…” or “Enterprise users need…” describes the person doing the job (in Ulwick’s terms, the job executor), not the job. The same person hires different products for different jobs in different contexts.

        Fix: define jobs by situations, not personas. A CEO and a student can share a job: “When I’m stuck on a problem, I want a quick second opinion.”

      5. Missing the competing hires

        Looking only at direct competitors. A music-learning app competes with other music apps, and also with Netflix, YouTube and going for a walk.

        Fix: ask what the user was doing before they hired your product, and what else they could hire instead. Map the whole set of alternatives.

      6. Treating JTBD as a one-time exercise

        One session, one poster, then nothing. The job itself holds steady for years, while the situations people meet it in, their alternatives and how well you serve it keep shifting.

        Fix: revisit your job stories when the context changes (for example once a quarter) and use them as a lens in every product review. Has the situation changed? Have the alternatives changed? Are we still serving the job well?

      Running switch interviews to find the job

      Reconstruct one real decision, from first doubt to commitment

      The switch interview, developed by Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, is a core JTBD research technique. You talk to people who recently switched, either to your product or away from it, and rebuild the timeline of their decision step by step: what pushed them away from the old solution, what pulled them toward the new one, and what anxieties and habits almost stopped them.

      Four forces decide whether people switch

      Moesta and Spiek also described the forces of progress, and the interview is built to listen for them. Every decision to switch, whether to your product or away from it, is a contest between these four.

      Push+Pull >Anxiety+Habit

      What a product team can change

      Your product can increase Pull and reduce Anxiety, for example by making the benefit obvious and making it safe to try. You cannot create Push or remove Habit, so you need to understand all four to know where your effort will count.

      Planning the interviews

      Who to interview
      People who switched recently, ideally in the last 3 months, to your product or away from it. Memory fades fast, and you want specific moments rather than tidy summaries.
      How many
      For a full study, 8–12 switch interviews is a common practitioner guideline. For a quick read, 5–6 conversations are a starting point, not a conclusion. Stop when new interviews stop turning up new forces.
      Length
      About 45 minutes, in five phases
      1. 0–5 min

        Set the scene

        “I’d love to understand your experience choosing [product]. There are no right or wrong answers. I’m interested in the full story.”

      2. 5–15 min

        First thought: the push

        “Tell me about the first time you thought about switching. What was happening? What were you frustrated with?” Dig into the specific moment: where they were and what triggered it.

      3. 15–25 min

        Looking around: the pull

        “What did you look at? Who did you talk to? What was appealing about the alternatives?” Map every option they considered, including doing nothing.

      4. 25–35 min

        Anxiety and habit

        “What almost stopped you? What worried you about switching? Was there anything you’d miss from the old way?” This shows the barriers you need to reduce.

      5. 35–45 min

        The decision and after

        “What was the final tipping point? Has it met your expectations? Would you go back?” Capture the moment of commitment and whether the job is being done well.

      Questions for each force

      To find the push

      • “What was the hardest part about how you were doing it before?”
      • “When did you first feel frustrated enough to look for something else?”
      • “Can you walk me through that specific moment?”

      To find the pull

      • “What caught your attention about the alternative?”
      • “What did you imagine your life would be like after switching?”
      • “What made you decide this was the one?”

      To find the anxiety

      • “What almost stopped you from switching?”
      • “Were you worried about losing anything?”
      • “Did anyone try to talk you out of it?”

      To find the habit

      • “What did you like about the old way?”
      • “Was there anything comforting about how you used to do it?”
      • “Did you ever think ‘this is good enough, why bother’?”

      Ask about moments, not opinions

      “Why did you switch?” gets you a rationalized summary. “Where were you when you first thought about it?” gets you the situation, and the situation is the first part of the job story.

      Test yourself on JTBD

      1. “People don’t want a quarter-inch drill. They want a…”

        The hole is the outcome the buyer is after, and the drill is only today’s way of getting it. A stronger or cheaper drill is tempting, but it improves the current solution without telling you anything about the job.

      2. Which of these is a well-formed job story?

        The story about finding a specific recipe names a moment, the progress wanted and why it matters, and none of it depends on an app. The search-bar story has the right shape, but filters and tags are features, so it describes a solution.

      3. What are the three dimensions of a job?

        A job is the practical task plus how people want to feel and be seen while doing it. Speed, quality, cost and convenience sound like needs, but they are ways to judge a solution.

      4. In the milkshake study, what was the real competition?

        The commuters wanted something filling and slow to finish for a dull drive, and a banana or a bagel can do that too. Other milkshake brands are the obvious answer, but they are only the rivals you would list by product category.

      5. Which four forces decide whether someone switches?

        Push and Pull draw people toward a new way of doing things, while Anxiety and Habit hold them back. Awareness, interest, desire and action also describes a decision, but it is a marketing funnel for moving people toward a purchase.

      6. In switch interviews, several new Larkwell subscribers say they had been learning from free video tutorials and gave up because they could never tell whether they were playing the right notes. Which force is that?

        The complaint is about the free tutorials they were leaving, so it pushes them away from the old way. It can sound like pull because Larkwell fixes the problem, but pull is something attractive about Larkwell itself, such as feedback on every note.

      7. Your team wants to understand why subscribers cancel in their first months. Whom should you recruit for switch interviews first?

        Recent cancelers made the very decision you want to understand and can still recall the moments around it. Loyal weekly users are easier to reach, but they have never decided to leave, so they can only guess why others do.

      8. Halfway through a switch interview, the person says, “I switched because it was just better.” What is the most useful next question?

        Asking where they were sends the person back to the real situation, which is where the push lives. A question about favorite features feels natural, but it steers the talk toward the solution and away from the decision.

      9. How many switch interviews is a common guideline for a full JTBD study?

        8–12 conversations usually show which forces keep coming back, and a quick read can start with 5–6. Thirty or more sounds more rigorous, but these interviews trade volume for depth, and that many is survey territory.

      10. JTBD has found the job, and the team has a list of features that might serve it. What can the Kano model add?

        Kano sorts a feature into a category such as Must-have, Performance or Delighter, which is a classification. Ranking the list from first to last is the tempting answer, but that takes a scoring method such as RICE or ICE. A release plan covering the whole job is what a story map produces.
