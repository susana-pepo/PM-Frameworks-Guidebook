A growth loop is a closed system in which the output of one cycle, such as a new user, an invite or a piece of content, becomes the input of the next. Use it to design growth that compounds, instead of growth you have to keep buying.

In short

Every loop has an input, an action that creates value for someone else, and an output that becomes new input. What one cycle returns is K: amplitude × conversion, such as 3 invites × 20% = 0.6 new users per user. K above 1 is rare, but even K = 0.2 cuts effective CAC by about 20%, and the shorter the cycle, the faster the loop compounds. Fix retention before you amplify anything.

Where a funnel ends, a loop starts again

AARRR (pirate metrics) draws growth as a funnel: users enter at the top and some leak out at every stage. That tells you where you lose people. It can’t explain why some products grow faster the bigger they get.

Brian Balfour, Casey Winters, Kevin Kwok and Andrew Chen put a name to the reason in “Growth Loops are the New Funnels” (Reforge, 2018). When what users produce at the last step brings in the people for the first, each cycle can be bigger than the last: 10 users create content that attracts 15, who create content that attracts 22, and so on.

The funnel doesn’t disappear inside a loop. Each step of a loop is still measured as a conversion, from the people at one step to the people who complete the next.

Three parts of every loop

Step 2, the gray one, is the step growth plans tend to skip: people have to get value from the product before they invite anyone or post anything. The card numbers match the step numbers in the figure.

1

Input

What enters the loop?

A new user, a new piece of content or new revenue. The first cycle has to be seeded from outside the loop: marketing, PR or the founders’ own effort.

3

Action

What does the user do that creates value for others?

Something people do inside the product: share, create, invite, review or publish.

4

Output becomes new input

How does the result bring in the next cycle?

New people discover the product, new content attracts visitors, or revenue funds more acquisition. This is the feedback mechanism, and without it you have a funnel.

A waterfall and a flywheel

A funnel works like a waterfall, flowing one way and downhill. A loop works like a flywheel, where each push makes the next one easier. A marketplace shows it: lower prices bring more customers, more customers attract more sellers, more sellers improve selection, and a bigger business can lower prices again.

Three kinds of loop

Viral, content and paid loops differ in what feeds the next cycle

Viral loops

Users invite or attract other users as a natural byproduct of using the product.

  1. A user signs up
  2. They invite friends
  3. Friends sign up
  4. They invite their friendsFeeds step 1 again
Examples
A messaging app that is only useful once your contacts are on it, a file-sharing service where sharing a file invites the recipient, a team chat tool you invite colleagues into so you can work together
Key metric
K-factor: invitations sent per user × the share of invitations that convert, which is the number of new users each user brings in per cycle. Above 1, each cohort brings in more than itself, so growth is exponential while it lasts.
Limit
K above 1 is rare and usually temporary. Most viral loops run below 1, which still lowers what each user costs.

Content loops

Content gets indexed or shared, attracts new users, and those users lead to more content. Reforge separates two kinds. In a user-generated loop, the new users create the next round of content themselves. In a company-generated loop, your team writes the content, and the users it attracts fund or direct the next piece.

  1. A user creates content
  2. Search or social feeds surface it
  3. A new user discovers it
  4. They sign up and create contentFeeds step 1 again
Examples
User-generated: an image-pinning site whose boards search engines index, a question-and-answer site, a video platform. Company-generated: a team that publishes how-to pages that rank in search
Key metric
Content created per user, search impressions
Limit
Slow to build. The content has to rank or surface before the loop turns at all.

Paid loops

Revenue from users funds the acquisition of more users.

  1. Ads acquire a user
  2. The user pays a subscription
  3. Revenue funds more ads
  4. More users arriveFeeds step 1 again
Examples
Consumer subscription apps and many SaaS businesses
Key metric
Lifetime value to customer acquisition cost (LTV:CAC) ratio and payback period
Limit
A paid loop compounds only if LTV exceeds CAC and the payback period is short enough to reinvest. CAC tends to rise at scale as the cheapest audiences run out.

Each type trades cost against durability

Paid loops are the easiest to start and the easiest to lose. They stop when spending stops, and anyone with a budget can buy the same audience. While the unit economics hold, a paid loop can still be a sound main engine. Content loops are the reverse: slow to build, then hard to copy once the pages rank. Viral loops cost little per user, but invite mechanics are easy to copy as well. A viral loop gets hard to compete with only when it rests on a network effect, where each new user makes the product better for the others.

Loops can feed each other

Many products run more than one loop, and the loops can reinforce one another: content brings in users who invite friends, and the revenue they generate pays for ads that seed both.

What makes a loop strong

Four measures, two of which multiply into what each cycle returns

Amplitude

How much output does each input create?

The invites, shares or views each user produces in one cycle. If each user sends 3 invites on average, amplitude is 3. The word means size here; it has nothing to do with Amplitude, the analytics company behind the North Star Playbook.

Conversion rate

What share of that output becomes new input?

If 20% of invited people sign up, conversion is 20%. The higher it is, the more of each cycle’s output comes back.

Cycle time

How long does one full loop take?

A photo-sharing app can turn in minutes: post, seen, follow. A travel-booking marketplace turns in weeks: stay, review, new booking. The shorter the cycle, the faster the loop compounds.

Defensibility

Can competitors copy this loop?

Content loops build a search moat over time. Network effects build a user moat. Paid loops are the easiest to copy, although scale and better targeting data can give some edge.

Amplitude times conversion is what each cycle returns

Amplitude ×Conversion =K per cycle

For a viral loop this product is the K-factor. With 3 invites per user and 20% acceptance, K is 3 × 20% = 0.6: every user brings in 0.6 new users per cycle. Content loops work the same way: if 100 users’ posts bring in 30 new users, the loop returns 0.3 new users per user per cycle.

A K below 1 still matters. With K = 0.6, every 100 users you acquire bring in 60, who bring in 36, and so on, until the 100 have become about 250. That is why a small loop on top of paid acquisition lowers what each user costs: with K = 0.2, counting the invites of invited users too, effective CAC falls by 20%.

Cycle time decides how fast it compounds

K tells you what one cycle returns; cycle time tells you how many cycles you get. Take two loops with K = 1.2. One turns every 3 days and runs 10 cycles a month; the other turns every 15 days and runs 2. After a month, the fast loop’s newest group is 1.210, about 6.2× the starting group. The slow loop’s is 1.22, or 1.44×.

No published formula combines these measures into one loop-strength score, so treat them as this guide’s working heuristic: a loop gets stronger when more people take part, more of them convert and each turn is faster.

Look at the clock before the invites

Teams often try to raise amplitude first: more invites, more shares. Shortening the cycle, for example by prompting a share at the first moment of value instead of a week later, is often the bigger improvement.

Mapping Larkwell’s three growth loops

Worked example

A piano-learning app that buys most of its new learners

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), is the same company as in the AARRR guide. It sells a monthly plan at $14.99 and an annual plan at $89.99, which averages $9.00 a month per subscriber (ARPPU). Each month it gets 100,000 installs: 60,000 from paid ads, 30,000 from organic app store search and 10,000 from referrals and shared clips. Organic search is its best channel per install: it costs nothing and brings learners with high intent, but it is not one of its loops.

Running now: the paid loop

  1. Learners pay for a subscription$9.00 a month on average
  2. Revenue funds app store and video ads$150,000 a month, $2.50 per install
  3. New people install60,000 paid installs a month
  4. Some of them subscribe7.5% of new accounts; feeds step 1 again

Status: active, and it pays back. With a blended CAC of $30 ($180,000 of marketing ÷ 6,000 new subscribers), a 75% gross margin and 7% monthly churn, LTV is about $96 ($9.00 × 75% ÷ 7%). LTV:CAC is about 3.2:1, above the 3:1 that is a widely cited rule of thumb, and payback is about 4.4 months: $30 ÷ ($9.00 × 75%). That is short enough to reinvest, so the loop can keep compounding while those numbers hold. Blended CAC mixes paid subscribers with those from organic search and referrals, so check the paid channel’s own CAC before raising the ad budget. It still stops when spending stops, and costs can rise as more apps bid for the same beginners.

Barely turning: the first-song clip loop

  1. A learner plays a first songA moment of pride
  2. The app offers to share a 15-second clipAbout 3% of activated learners share one today
  3. Friends see the clipAnd think “I could do that”
  4. Some installThey reach their own first song, and the loop starts again

Status: potential, a viral loop. Shared clips and the “Give a friend 30 days free” referral offer together give Larkwell a K-factor of 0.10: 0.5 invites per new account × 20% acceptance. That is about 8,000 new accounts a month (80,000 × 0.10), which matches 10,000 referral and clip installs at an 80% sign-up rate. With only 3% sharing, the clip loop contributes little so far. A better share at the first-song moment would cost almost nothing per new learner and comes from a real moment of achievement, but it needs investment in the sharing experience.

Not built yet: the song tutorial pages

  1. Larkwell’s educators publish song pages“How to play Für Elise on piano”
  2. The pages bring in search traffic
  3. Visitors install the app
  4. Learners request songsTheir requests pick the next pages

Status: potential, slow but durable. This is a company-generated content loop: Larkwell’s content team writes the pages, and the learners the pages bring in close the loop by asking for the next song and paying for the work. It builds a search moat over time at a low cost per page.

Fix retention, then grow the clip loop

Larkwell relies on its paid loop. Most new learners are bought. The loop pays back today, but it stops when the budget does and gives rivals nothing they cannot buy too.

Retention comes first. Larkwell’s AARRR funnel puts its biggest leak at retention: 45% of new accounts activate, but only 9% are active on day 30, and four in five activated learners are gone within a month. Any loop Larkwell adds now would mostly bring in learners who leave, so the retention work goes first.

The first-song clip loop is the largest untested opportunity. Larkwell already has the moment of pride and a loop that turns at 3% sharing, so raising the share rate is likely the cheapest way to move growth from bought to earned.

The song pages are the long-term base. They will not move numbers this quarter, but each page can keep bringing in beginners long after it is written.

      Six ways loops underperform

      Some never close; others close but return too little or turn too slowly

      1. Calling a funnel a loop

        If no output of the last step becomes a new input, it is a funnel. The link from output back to input is the critical one, and “users love it and tell people” describes hope, not a mechanism.

        Fix: trace the output of each cycle back to the start of the next before you draw the loop. Write the link out in full: “…which causes [these new people] to [enter the loop this way].” If you cannot, the loop is not closed.

      2. Assuming virality

        Growth plans often assume each user will bring in more than one more. A K-factor above 1 is rare and usually temporary, so a plan built on it overstates growth.

        Fix: measure invitations per user, acceptance and cycle time, and plan as if K is below 1, where most loops run.

      3. Scaling a paid loop without its unit economics

        Teams raise the ad budget because the loop pays back at today’s spend. Each extra dollar reaches a less interested audience, so CAC climbs until the loop stops paying back, and growth ends the day the budget does.

        Fix: compute LTV:CAC and payback for the paid channel before you scale it, and layer a viral or content loop on top. Even a small K lowers effective CAC, as Mechanics shows.

      4. Bolting on a share button

        A “share with friends” button added to the end of a flow is not a viral loop. The strongest viral loops happen when sharing is the product, as when you invite colleagues into a team chat tool to work together.

        Fix: find the action that is core to the experience and makes the product better when someone else joins.

      5. Mislabeling a content loop

        A loop where the company writes the content behaves differently from one where users do: the company pays for every piece, while users create theirs for free. Either way, a content loop only turns if the content is discoverable through search, social feeds or the platform’s own search. Content that stays behind a login brings no one in.

        Fix: name who creates the content and cost it accordingly, and check that each piece will rank or surface on its own. If your team writes it, check that new users give something back, such as revenue or demand data, that funds the next piece.

      6. Tuning amplitude and ignoring cycle time

        Teams push for more invites per user and never measure how long one cycle takes. Two loops with the same K grow at very different speeds if one turns in a week and the other in a quarter.

        Fix: time one full loop, from a user’s arrival to the arrival of the users they bring, and look for the step that takes longest.

      Test yourself on growth loops

      1. What is the key difference between a funnel and a growth loop?

        Only a loop sends what it produces, such as a new user, a post or revenue, back to its own start, and that return is what lets it compound. A funnel is not an older name for the same thing: it stops at its last stage and has to be refilled from outside.

      2. What are the three types of acquisition loop?

        Each type is named for what brings in the next round of users: invites, content that strangers find, or revenue spent on more acquisition. Acquisition, retention and referral sound close, but they are stages of the AARRR funnel.

      3. What advantage does a viral loop have over a paid loop?

        Once built, a viral loop brings users in through other users instead of a media budget, so it keeps turning after spending stops. Building it still takes real product work, and K rarely stays above 1.

      4. Each user sends 4 invites on average and 15% of invited people sign up. What is the loop’s K-factor?

        Multiply the two: 4 invites × 15% acceptance gives 0.6 new users for every user in a cycle. 0.15 is the acceptance rate alone and ignores how many invites each user sends.

      5. Two loops both have K = 1.2. Loop A turns every 3 days and loop B every 15 days. What happens over a month?

        Equal K means equal growth per turn, and the 3-day loop gets five times as many turns in a month: 1.2 compounded ten times is about 6.2×, against 1.44× from two turns. Any K above 1 compounds; it doesn’t have to pass 2.

      6. Why is it a mistake to run a growth loop on a product with poor retention?

        A loop multiplies whatever happens to the people it brings in. If most of them leave, it just uses up the market faster, viral or paid, so retention comes first.

      7. On an image-pinning site, users save pictures to public boards, search engines index the boards, and people who find them sign up and save more. What kind of loop is this?

        The site’s users make the boards, and search engines carry them to new people who then save boards of their own. It would be company-generated only if the site’s staff wrote the boards, and no one here sends an invite.

      8. Larkwell spends revenue on ads. Its LTV is about $96, its blended CAC is $30 and payback is about 4.4 months. What does this paid loop need to keep compounding?

        A paid loop keeps compounding while LTV beats CAC and the money comes back fast enough to spend again. Larkwell’s LTV:CAC of about 3.2:1 ($96 ÷ $30) and its 4.4-month payback pass today. A K-factor above 1 is the test for a viral loop, not a paid one.
