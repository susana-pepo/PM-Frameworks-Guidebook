AARRR, or pirate metrics, gives each of five lifecycle stages one metric: Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral and Revenue. Startups and growth teams use it to choose the stage to work on next, and it is a common structure for growth questions in PM interviews.

In short

Follow one cohort through the stages: the step that keeps the smallest share of the users who reach it is where to start. Fix activation and retention before you pay for more acquisition, because more traffic will not fix a funnel that leaks in the middle. On the money side, an LTV:CAC ratio of 3:1 or better is the usual sign that acquisition is worth paying for.

Compare the share each step keeps

Many teams report totals that only grow, such as sign-ups or downloads, and those say nothing about whether anyone stays. AARRR replaces them with one rate per stage across the full user lifecycle, so the work goes into the leaks and more of the people you already attract make it through.

Dave McClure introduced the model in 2007, in a talk called “Startup Metrics for Pirates: AARRR!”, to give startups a simple way to think about growth that is more than “get more traffic”. Read the initials aloud and you get a pirate’s “AARRR”, hence pirate metrics. His talk lists the stages as Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral and Revenue, the order this guide uses. Many later write-ups put Revenue before Referral. The letters are the same either way, and measuring both stages matters more than their order.

Each stage gets one number, measured on a cohort: a group of users who share a start event in the same period, such as everyone who signed up in one month. Tracking a single cohort means every rate describes the same people. Raw counts would mislead, because the early stages hold the most users and so lose the most people even when they keep a healthy share of them.

Start in the middle of the funnel

People often read AARRR top-down and start with acquisition. Users who never activate or never come back are wasted spend, so activation and retention come first and paid acquisition waits until users stay. No stage matters most in general: treat this as a heuristic, and let your own step comparison pick the stage.

Five stages, each one a place to lose people

  1. Acquisition

    How do users find your product?

    Channels include SEO, paid ads, social media, the app store, partnerships, word of mouth and content marketing. Measure the cost of each new user by channel, not only as one total.

    Rank channels by how many of their users activate and stay; volume alone flatters cheap sources.

  2. Activation

    Do users have a great first experience?

    Activation is the first time a new user experiences the product’s core value, the aha moment, defined as a specific event in a time window. For Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults (figures are illustrative), it is playing a first song with real-time feedback. For a team chat tool, it might be sending a first message and getting a reply. Users who never reach the aha moment rarely come back.

    Define your aha moment, then remove every point of friction between sign-up and it.

  3. Retention

    Do users come back?

    Measure D1, D7 and D30 retention: the share of a sign-up cohort that is active 1, 7 and 30 days after signing up. Retention is an output metric: a team moves it by changing what users do in the product, not directly.

    D1 and D7 give the earliest warning; D30 shows who has formed a habit.

  4. Referral

    Do users tell others?

    Organic growth through NPS, referral programs, social sharing and the viral coefficient or K-factor: invitations sent per user multiplied by the share of invitations that become new users. Above 1, each cohort brings in more than itself and growth compounds on its own. Sustained K above 1 is rare and usually temporary; at a K-factor of 0.10, every 100 new users bring in about 10 more.

    Referral is free acquisition, but people only recommend products they keep using.

  5. Revenue

    Do users pay, and how much?

    Monetization through subscriptions, in-app purchases, ads or upsells. The key metrics are conversion to paid (free-to-paid or trial-to-paid), ARPU and ARPPU, LTV, and LTV compared with CAC: a customer has to bring in more than it cost to win them.

    Revenue depends on retention: users who stay keep paying, so revenue compounds.

Terms that are easy to mix up

Acquisition cost comes in three forms, each dividing spend by something different. Where Larkwell has the numbers, an entry ends with its calculation.

CAC
Customer acquisition cost: total sales and marketing spend in a period divided by the new paying customers won in that period. Say whether it is blended (all channels) or per channel. Larkwell: $180,000 ÷ 6,000 = $30 blended.
CPI
Cost per install: paid spend divided by installs from paid channels. Lower than CAC, because not every install becomes a customer. Larkwell: $150,000 ÷ 60,000 = $2.50.
CPA
Cost per acquisition: paid spend divided by the number of a defined action, such as a sign-up or a trial start. Always name the action. Larkwell: $150,000 ÷ 48,000 paid new accounts, about $3.13 per account.
LTV
Lifetime value: the expected gross profit from a customer over the whole time they stay. Simple form: ARPPU × gross margin ÷ monthly churn. Larkwell: $9.00 × 75% ÷ 7%, about $96.
ARPU
Average revenue per user: revenue in a period divided by all users in the base you state, paying or not. Larkwell: $1.35 a month per monthly active user.
ARPPU
Average revenue per paying user: revenue in a period divided by paying subscribers. Larkwell: $9.00 a month.
K-factor
Invitations sent per user multiplied by the share of invitations that become new users. Larkwell: 0.5 × 20% = 0.10.
Churn
The share of paying subscribers at the start of a month who cancel during it. Retention, by contrast, is measured on a cohort of all new users. Larkwell: about 4,200 of 60,000 subscribers cancel each month, 7%.
DAU/MAU
Daily active users divided by monthly active users: how often people come back within a month.

LTV:CAC, the check on whether growth pays

  1. 3:1+HealthyEach customer returns at least three times what it cost to win them. 3:1 or better is a widely cited rule of thumb for subscription businesses, for example in David Skok’s writing on SaaS metrics. There is room to invest more in acquisition.
  2. 1–3:1Hard to pay backCustomers earn back what they cost, but slowly and with little to spare. Below about 3:1, acquisition spending is hard to pay back, so improve retention or pricing before you scale spend.
  3. <1:1Losing moneyEvery new customer costs more than they bring in. Stop spending and fix the funnel.

Larkwell sits just above the line: about $96 of lifetime value against a $30 CAC is roughly 3.2:1. Its payback period, the months of gross profit needed to recover CAC, is $30 ÷ ($9.00 × 75%), about 4.4 months.

Acquisition
100,000 installs a month: 60,000 from paid app store and video ads, 30,000 from organic app store search for “learn piano” and 10,000 from referrals and shared clips. 80% of installs become accounts, which gives 80,000 new accounts. Paid installs cost $2.50 each. Organic search costs nothing per install, and those learners arrive with high intent; whether it is the best channel depends on how many of them activate and stay.
Activation
Aha moment: playing a first song with real-time feedback. Metric: the share of new accounts that complete a first song lesson within 24 hours of sign-up, 45% today. Blocker: new users who never connect their instrument.
Retention
D1 40%, D7 19% and D30 9% of new accounts. Of activated learners, about 42% are active on day 7 and 20% on day 30, so four in five are gone within a month, most of them in the first week. Levers: practice reminders, streaks, progressive difficulty and songs learners already know. Key metrics: D7 and D30.
Referral
Built in: a “Give a friend 30 days free” program. Organic: about 3% of activated learners share a clip of their first song. Each new account sends 0.5 invitations on average and 20% are accepted, a K-factor of 0.10, or about 8,000 new accounts a month. NPS is likely to be highest among learners who pass the 30-day mark.
Revenue
Model: a 7-day free trial, then $14.99 a month or $89.99 a year ($7.50 a month). 25% of new accounts start a trial and 30% of trials convert, so 6,000 new subscribers a month, 7.5% of new accounts. With 80% of subscribers on the annual plan, ARPPU is $9.00 a month. Lever: show the paywall after the aha moment, not before.

Retention keeps only one in five activated learners

Activation keeps 45% of new accounts and Retention keeps 20% of activated learners, the smallest share of any step. Activation is the second-largest loss, with 55% of new accounts never completing a first lesson. It also loses more people in raw numbers (44,000 against 28,800), but only shares can be compared across stages of different sizes.

Revenue is not the constraint: 30% of trial starts convert, and each subscriber is worth about $96 in gross profit against a $30 CAC. Too few learners stay long enough for that to matter. No industry range was needed to see it; the step comparison points at retention, and Larkwell’s own earlier cohorts are the benchmark to beat.

Larkwell spends $150,000 a month on paid installs, which buys 60,000 installs and 48,000 new accounts. If those accounts retain like the rest, about 4,320 are still active on day 30, at about $35 per retained learner. Raising D30 retention from 9% to 11% would keep 5,280 for the same spend, about $28 each.

The fix is habit formation: make progress visible and make practice feel rewarding rather than like homework, then measure D30 against the 11% target.

      Six ways to misread the funnel

      1. Buying users the funnel cannot keep

        Spending on ads and marketing while activation or retention is broken is like pouring water into a bucket with a hole in the bottom. You pay for every user, including the ones who leave in the first week. A blended view makes it worse, because cheap volume from a weak channel can look like success.

        Fix: compute each stage’s conversion first and fix the largest relative loss before you raise acquisition spend. Split the funnel by channel, so a cheap source whose users leave shows up as a leak instead of hiding in the blended total.

      2. Reporting vanity metrics

        Installs and sign-ups can grow while the value users get does not. A running total rises even in a month when most new users leave, so it cannot show where people drop out.

        Fix: pick one actionable metric per stage, defined on a cohort and a time window, and report the share of each cohort that moves from one stage to the next.

      3. Leaving the aha moment vague

        “Users like it” or “active” without a specific event and window cannot be measured, and onboarding cannot be designed toward it. A related mistake is to put the paywall in front of it: people asked to pay before they have felt the value leave at Activation, before the product has had a chance to prove itself.

        Fix: define the aha moment from data, for example the action that best separates users who stay from users who leave, such as “completed a first song lesson within 24 hours”. Remove every step between sign-up and it, and show the paywall only after it.

      4. Mixing up CAC, CPI and CPA

        The three divide spend by different things: new paying customers, installs and a chosen action. A $2.50 cost per install is not a $2.50 customer; at Larkwell the blended CAC is $30.

        Fix: say which denominator you mean every time, measure acquisition cost by channel as well as blended, and use CAC in the LTV:CAC ratio.

      5. Borrowing someone else’s benchmarks

        Industry figures rarely match your product, channel mix or metric definitions, and most of the ranges that circulate have no named source. A stage can look fine against a borrowed range and still be your biggest leak.

        Fix: compare each cohort with your own earlier cohorts, and use the step comparison to choose where to work.

      6. Treating a looping product as a straight line

        A funnel ends at its last stage, so it misses how users bring in other users: referred learners, shared clips and content all feed new users back to the top. Reforge’s criticism of funnel-only thinking makes this point.

        Fix: map the product’s growth loops alongside the funnel, and measure each step of a loop as a conversion.

      Test yourself on AARRR

      1. Which five stages make up AARRR?

        The five are Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral and Revenue; McClure’s talk puts Referral fourth, and many later write-ups swap the last two. Awareness and Adoption are marketing-funnel stages, not AARRR’s.

      2. A team sees 45% activation and 9% D30 retention, and the marketing lead wants to double the ad budget. What does the AARRR heuristic suggest?

        Out of every 100 new users this team keeps 9 past day 30, so doubling the budget mostly doubles the number who leave. Acquisition does feed every later stage, but most of what it feeds in is gone by day 30; a referral program or a price rise hits the same leak, since both depend on users who stay.

      3. What is the aha moment in Activation?

        It is the event where a new user first feels what the product is for, such as a Larkwell learner hearing live feedback on a first song. Completing a profile is a common onboarding step, but it gives the user nothing of value on its own, so it makes a poor activation event.

      4. Which LTV:CAC ratio is usually called healthy?

        Three dollars of lifetime value for every dollar spent winning a customer is the usual line for growth worth paying for. Anything above 1:1 does earn its cost back, but so slowly that little is left over to fund the next customer.

      5. Why is spending heavily on acquisition while retention is poor a mistake?

        This is the leaky bucket. If only 9% of new accounts are active on day 30, most acquisition spend buys users who leave; raising retention makes every acquisition dollar return more.

      6. Larkwell pays $2.50 per paid install, yet its blended CAC is $30. What explains the gap?

        The two divide by different things: $2.50 is spend per paid install, while $30 spreads all $180,000 of spend over the 6,000 people who end up paying. Store commissions come out of revenue, not acquisition spend, so they cannot explain the gap.

      7. How does AARRR relate to a North Star Metric?

        The funnel gives every lifecycle stage a metric, while a North Star Metric picks the one measure of customer value the whole team aims at; the funnel then shows which stages feed it. They work together, so a North Star does not retire the funnel as a product matures.

      8. An app keeps 50% of new users through Activation, 20% of activated users through Retention, and converts 40% of retained users to paid. Where is the biggest leak?

        Measured against the users who reach it, Activation loses 50%, Retention 80% and Revenue 60%. Revenue’s 40% conversion can look like the weak spot, but Retention lets go of a larger share.
