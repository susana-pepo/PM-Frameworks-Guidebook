The ICE Score ranks a list of ideas, most often growth experiments, by rating each one from 1 to 10 on Impact, Confidence and Ease. It is one of the fastest ways to score, because it trades precision for speed: a small team can score 20 ideas in about 30 minutes.

In short

Rate each idea from 1 to 10 on how much it would move your key metric, how sure you are of that, and how easy it is to ship, then multiply the three. Run the highest scores first. ICE is for fast triage; when it matters how many users an idea reaches, move up to RICE.

Three ratings, all multiplied

Each factor is a 1–10 judgment the team makes together

I

Impact

How much will this change our key metric?

The expected effect on the metric you are optimizing, such as revenue, retention, engagement or NPS. It is a judgment about the size of the outcome.

From 1 (a tiny nudge) to 10 (massive)

C

Confidence

How sure are we about the Impact and Ease ratings?

The check on optimism. Evidence earns a high rating and a hunch earns a low one, so guesses are discounted and ideas backed by data are rewarded.

From 1 (gut feel) to 10 (proven by a test in this product)

E

Ease

How simple is this to ship?

The inverse of effort: the easier the work, the higher the rating. In RICE, Effort divides the score. In ICE, Ease multiplies it, so easy ideas are rewarded directly.

From 1 (months of work) to 10 (an afternoon)

Every factor points the same way

Because Ease replaces Effort, all three ratings read “higher is better” and the score is a plain product. ICE also has no Reach factor: it asks how much impact an idea has, not how many users it touches. That makes it faster than RICE and less precise.

Multiply or average

ICE comes from Sean Ellis and the growth community around GrowthHackers, and it appears in Hacking Growth (Ellis and Morgan Brown, 2017). This guide multiplies the three ratings, because a product punishes one weak rating much harder. Some teams average them instead, and the ranking is often the same. The two forms part ways on lopsided ideas: ratings of 9, 9 and 1 average 6.3 and beat 5, 5 and 5 (an average of 5), but multiplied they score 81 against 125. Pick one form and use it for every idea in the batch.

One 1–10 scale for every factor

Anchor each band so a 7 means the same thing to everyone in the room

All three factors use the same ten points. The bands below give each point a meaning. Agree on them before you score, and write anchors specific to your product where these are too general.

Impact

How much will this move your key metric?

  1. 9–10MassiveShifts the key metric on its own
  2. 7–8HighA clear, measurable lift
  3. 5–6MediumA noticeable lift
  4. 3–4SmallA modest lift
  5. 1–2TinyBarely shows in the metric

Most ideas are a 4–6

If nearly everything scores 8 or more on Impact, the team is inflating it.

Confidence

What evidence supports your Impact and Ease ratings?

  1. 10ProvenAn A/B test or a measured launch in this product
  2. 8–9Strong evidenceQuantitative data, such as analytics or a large survey, or a test elsewhere
  3. 6–7Good dataQualitative research or consistent feedback, such as interviews or usability tests
  4. 4–5Some signalsAnecdotes, a few customer requests or competitor examples
  5. 1–3Gut feelAn opinion with no data behind it

If you want a more detailed rubric, Itamar Gilad’s Confidence Meter is a common reference. It lists kinds of evidence, from personal conviction up to launch data, and gives each one a confidence value.

Ease

How simple is this to ship? A higher rating means less work.

  1. 9–10HoursAn afternoon of work
  2. 7–8DaysA few days
  3. 5–6About a weekOne working week
  4. 3–4WeeksSeveral weeks
  5. 1–2MonthsA month or more

Count all the work: design, engineering, QA and documentation. An idea that is easy for engineering but needs two weeks of design is not a 9. These anchors are ICE’s own. RICE measures Effort in person-months on a different scale, and the two are not meant to be converted into each other.

Ten points cut both ways

The 1–10 scale is quick to use, which is ICE’s strength. It is also its weakness: ten options leave more room for personal judgment than the three to five fixed values RICE uses for Impact and Confidence.

What the scores tell us

Simplifying the signup form wins, at 576. It has high impact, strong evidence from analytics and little work: a Quick win, in Value vs Effort terms.

Social proof badges come second, at 441. They are also easy to ship and backed by good qualitative data, which makes them a good second experiment.

Personalized onboarding has the highest Impact but ranks third, at 216. A Confidence of 6 (good data, but qualitative) and an Ease of 4 (weeks of work) pull it down. Because the ratings multiply, one weak factor drags down the whole score.

The referral program scores 160. Its Impact is high, but its Confidence of 4 rests on a few anecdotes. A few customer interviews that confirmed the demand would raise Confidence to 7, good data, and lift it to 280.

The AI chatbot scores 36. It is hard to build, nobody is sure it works and its impact is moderate. Park it.

Averaging the ratings instead gives the same order here: 8.3, 7.7, 6.3, 5.7 and 3.7.

Three experiments to try

  1. Set Confidence to 1. With 5 for Impact and Ease the score falls to 25: low confidence sinks an idea.
  2. Set all three to 10. The score reaches 1,000, the maximum. Very few ideas deserve it.
  3. Compare 9 × 3 × 3, a big idea that is unproven and hard, with 5 × 8 × 8, a smaller one that is proven and easy. The second scores 320 against 81, almost 4× as much.

When ICE fits, and when it does not

Built for fast triage, not for final decisions

Use it when

  • You are scoring growth experiments or A/B test ideas, especially when the team picks new ones every week
  • The product is early and has no analytics data yet
  • You have a long backlog to triage and only one short meeting for it
  • You are in a hackathon or design sprint and need a quick order
  • The team is small and everyone shares the context

Skip it when

  • Leadership will examine the reasoning behind the ranking. Use RICE instead.
  • You have analytics data for Reach, which makes RICE the better fit
  • How many users an idea affects is a central question
  • Scores need to be comparable across teams
  • People keep gaming the 1–10 scale

ICE for speed, RICE for precision

Both rank ideas by expected value and by how sure you are, but they suit different moments.

Choose ICE for speed

You need a rough order for a long list in one short meeting, and roughly right is good enough.

Choose RICE for precision

The decision has to stand up to scrutiny, and you have usage data to estimate Reach.

Use both: ICE to filter, RICE to rank

The two work well in sequence. ICE makes a fast first pass over a long list, and RICE ranks the shortlist with more care.

  1. Long listAbout 30 ideas
  2. ICEThe first pass cuts it to about 10
  3. RICEScoring the shortlist picks the top 3

Value vs Effort can make the same first pass when the team wants to see the trade-offs on a grid, and the grid is useful again at the end to present the result. The Kano model is not a step in this sequence: it runs alongside when you need to know how a feature affects satisfaction, and it classifies features rather than ranking them. Choosing a prioritization framework sets all four side by side.

Five ways ICE goes wrong

The method is simple, so its mistakes are easy to miss

  1. Scoring without anchors

    My Impact 7 is your Impact 4. Without agreed definitions, ratings from different people cannot be compared.

    Fix: write anchor definitions before scoring, such as “Impact 8 means the key metric moves by 10% or more”, and share the rubric with everyone.

  2. Ignoring how many people an idea reaches

    ICE has no separate Reach factor. Two ideas rated Impact 9, one for 100 users and one for 100,000, get the same score unless the team has agreed that Impact includes breadth.

    Fix: decide up front whether Impact covers how many people are affected; many teams fold it in. If the number of users is central to the decision, switch to RICE.

  3. Inflating Confidence

    Everyone gives their favorite idea a Confidence of 8. Real confidence comes from evidence, not enthusiasm.

    Fix: hold every rating to the Confidence bands: 1–3 gut feel, 4–5 some signals such as anecdotes, 6–7 good data such as qualitative research or consistent feedback, 8–9 strong evidence such as quantitative data or a test elsewhere, and 10 proven, only for an A/B test or a measured launch in this product.

  4. Clustering every rating at 5–7

    People avoid the ends of the scale. When every rating is a 5, 6 or 7, small differences decide the ranking: the gaps between ideas are often no bigger than the disagreement between two scorers about a single idea. Near-ties and exact ties are common too (6 × 6 × 5 and 5 × 6 × 6 both make 180), and they hide the real differences between ideas.

    Fix: use the full range. If nothing scores below 4 or above 8, ask the team which ideas really are a 2 or a 9.

  5. Using ICE for high-stakes decisions

    ICE is a triage method, not a planning method. A quarterly roadmap built on three quick judgments per idea rests on very little.

    Fix: use ICE for the first pass and RICE for the final decisions. Move to the more rigorous framework when the stakes rise.

Running a 30-minute scoring session

Score silently, reveal, average, then sort

Must attend
PM, engineering lead and anyone who owns one of the ideas
Useful to have
Designer, data analyst, growth marketer
Prepare
A spreadsheet with every idea listed, the anchor definitions for the three scales posted where everyone can see them, and an agreed key metric to score against
  1. 0–5 min

    Set the context

    Explain ICE, share the anchor definitions and name the metric: “Today we are scoring against [key metric].”

  2. 5–10 min

    Score silently

    Everyone rates every idea on Impact, Confidence and Ease, 1–10 each, with no discussion. Five minutes, with a timer running.

  3. 10–20 min

    Reveal and average

    Go idea by idea. Everyone reveals their ratings and you average each factor. Flag any idea where ratings differ by 3 points or more and discuss it briefly.

  4. 20–30 min

    Sort and commit

    Calculate I × C × E, sort from highest to lowest and pick the top 3–5 experiments to run.

Keep it to 30 minutes

The session is short because the method is. Speed is the point, so do not let the meeting stretch to an hour. If the debate on one idea runs past two minutes, that idea probably needs RICE instead.

Check your understanding

Six questions on the factors, the arithmetic and when to use ICE

  1. What does the E in ICE stand for?

    E is Ease, the inverse of Effort: the easier the idea, the higher the score. RICE divides by Effort; ICE multiplies by Ease.

  2. Impact 8 × Confidence 3 × Ease 7 = ?

    8 × 3 × 7 = 168. With a Confidence of 10 the same idea would score 560, so a rating of 3, gut feel, cut the score by more than two thirds.

  3. What is the main thing ICE leaves out compared with RICE?

    ICE has no Reach factor, so unless Impact is defined to include breadth, it cannot tell an idea for 100 users from one for 100,000. Confidence is in both frameworks, and the work an idea needs is still counted: ICE rates it as Ease, the inverse of Effort. If the number of users matters, use RICE.

  4. A growth team picks new experiments every week. This week it has 25 ideas, each cheap to test, and one 30-minute meeting to choose the first few to run. What fits best?

    ICE is built for this: a long list, a short meeting and ideas that are cheap to test, where roughly right is good enough and each test corrects the guess. A RICE session takes about 90 minutes and needs usage data to estimate Reach. A Kano survey takes 1–2 weeks, and it classifies features by their effect on satisfaction rather than ranking experiments.

  5. Everyone on your team rated every idea between 5 and 7. What is the problem?

    When all ratings sit between 5 and 7, the gaps between ideas are often no larger than the disagreement between scorers, and near-ties are common, so the order says little. Switching to RICE does not fix it on its own. Ask the team to use the full range: some ideas really are a 2 or a 9.

  6. Five customer interviews all describe the problem an idea would solve. There is no analytics data or test yet. Which Confidence rating fits the bands in this guide?

    Consistent qualitative research, such as a set of interviews that agree, is good data: a 6 or 7. It is more than an opinion, so it is above gut feel. Strong evidence (8–9) needs quantitative data or a test elsewhere, and 10 is reserved for an A/B test or a measured launch in this product.
