Shape Up fixes the time and lets the scope vary. Senior people shape a problem into a rough, bounded pitch with an appetite, a betting table chooses which pitches get a 6-week cycle, and a small team builds each one, cutting scope as needed to ship on time.

In short

Decide how much time a problem is worth, shape a rough but bounded solution that fits that time, and bet one 6-week cycle on it. The team owns the whole project and cuts scope to ship on time; if it does not ship, the project stops instead of running over. Two weeks of cooldown follow, and the next bets are placed during them.

Fix the time, let the scope give way

The appetite sets how long; the team decides how much fits

Shape Up is a way of working developed at Basecamp (now 37signals) and published in 2019, when Ryan Singer described it in his book Shape Up: Stop Running in Circles and Ship Work that Matters. The book is free to read online. The next chapter walks through shaping, betting and building; the chapter after it covers the key ideas that keep a cycle on time.

The idea that holds it together is fixed time, variable scope. Before any work starts, the business decides how much time a problem deserves. That time does not move. What moves is the scope: inside the boundaries the pitch sets, the team decides which parts are essential and cuts the rest so the core ships on time.

Scope gives way, quality does not

When time runs short, a Shape Up team drops nice-to-haves and simplifies the solution. It does not ship the core work half-tested or half-finished. The appetite caps the time, the pitch caps the ambition, and the team chooses what to leave out.

The problems it was designed to solve

The method answers four problems that many product teams will recognize.

The backlog only grows

Ideas pile up faster than any team can build them, and the list turns into an obligation nobody can meet.

Shape Up: there is no central backlog. An idea that is not pitched and bet on is let go. Good ideas come back.

Short iterations leave little room for meaningful work

Larger features rarely fit an iteration of a week or two, so teams either cut them too thin or carry work across several iterations.

Shape Up: a 6-week cycle is long enough to build something substantial and short enough to keep the end in sight.

Estimates start from the scope

Asking how long a piece of work will take fixes the scope first and lets the time follow, so the schedule moves every time the scope grows.

Shape Up: an appetite replaces the estimate. Instead of asking how long it will take, it asks how much time the problem is worth: “We are willing to spend 6 weeks on this, no more.”

Refining work that may never ship

Teams refine and re-refine backlog items that may never be built.

Shape Up: the thinking happens once, up front, when senior people shape a pitch before anyone bets on it.

Shaping turns a raw idea into a pitch

Shaping is done by one or two senior people, often a product manager or designer who also understands the technical side. It runs on its own track while teams build, so it has no fixed duration: a pitch is ready when it is shaped well enough to bet on. The result is a pitch, not a spec. It is specific enough for a team to build and rough enough to leave the team room to make design decisions.

Rough

Is there room left for the team?

Sketches show the main elements and flow, not finished screens. The building team works out the details.

Solved

Do the parts fit together?

The main elements and how they connect are worked out, and the known risks have answers.

Bounded

Is it clear what is out?

An appetite caps the time, and explicit no-gos say what the team should not build.

Four steps of shaping

  1. Set boundaries

    Narrow the raw idea to a specific problem and decide the appetite: how much time solving it is worth.

  2. Rough out the elements

    Sketch a solution at the right level of detail with breadboards and fat-marker sketches, described under key ideas.

  3. Address risks and rabbit holes

    Look for the unknowns that could swallow the cycle, and decide how the solution steps around each one.

  4. Write the pitch

    Package the problem, appetite, solution, rabbit holes and no-gos into a document leadership can read before the betting table.

The five parts of a pitch

Problem
The situation that is broken for users today, and why it matters now
Appetite
How much time the problem is worth: a small batch or a big batch
Solution
The main elements and flow, shown as breadboards or fat-marker sketches, not wireframes
Rabbit holes
Known risks in the solution, resolved or ruled out in advance, such as “use the existing library, do not build a custom audio engine”
No-gos
What the pitch explicitly excludes, such as “no social features, no custom themes”

A bet replaces the backlog

At the betting table, the people who can commit teams’ time review the pitches and choose which ones to fund for the next cycle. Each choice is a bet: “We believe this pitch is worth 6 weeks of a team’s time.” A bet has a payout if it works and a capped cost if it does not, because the cycle ends either way. Not every pitch gets picked.

Who
The senior people who decide product direction and can commit a team’s time. The book describes Basecamp’s table as the CEO, the CTO, a senior programmer and a product strategist.
When
During cooldown, before the next cycle starts
Comes in
Pitches shaped since the last cycle, plus any older idea someone has chosen to re-pitch
Goes out
The plan for the next cycle: which team takes which project

Unpicked pitches are let go

Pitches that are not chosen do not go into a central backlog. Anyone can keep their own notes and bring an idea back, and important ideas tend to return on their own. Each betting table starts from what matters now instead of from a long list that needs grooming.

The team gets a whole project, not a list of tasks

A build team is small: one designer and one or two programmers. It receives the pitch and the full cycle, and it has full autonomy over how to execute. Nobody hands down tasks from above and there are no daily status meetings with managers. The team discovers the detailed design and the implementation as it builds: the tasks it imagined at the start are only part of the tasks it finds along the way.

  1. Get one piece done

    In the first days, build one small slice end to end, with real interface and working code, rather than designing everything before coding anything.

  2. Map the scopes

    Group the work into scopes: parts of the project that can be finished and shown on their own, named in the project’s language (“Tempo control”, not “Front end”). The map changes as the team learns.

  3. Show progress on the hill

    Place each scope on a hill chart and move it as the work moves, so everyone can see where the unknowns are.

  4. Hammer the scope

    Scope hammering means cutting scope to fit the fixed time. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves (the book marks nice-to-haves with a tilde, ~) and cut nice-to-haves as time runs short.

    Judge against the baseline, what users have today, not against an ideal version.

  5. Ship by the end of week 6

    What ships is the hammered scope. Anything left over is a candidate for a future pitch, not an extension.

Five ideas that keep a cycle on time

Appetite, rough sketches, the circuit breaker, cooldown and the hill chart

Appetite instead of an estimate

An estimate asks, “How long will this take?” An appetite asks, “How much time is this worth?” Appetite is a business decision, not an engineering guess, and it comes before the solution: shapers start from the budget and design something that fits, rather than starting from a scope and asking how long it will run. There are two sizes.

  1. 6 weeksBig batchOne team spends the whole cycle on one project
  2. 1–2 weeksSmall batchA team completes several small projects within one 6-week cycle

Two ways to sketch without designing

A breadboard uses words instead of pictures. It names the places a user can go (screens, dialogs), the affordances on each place (buttons, fields, controls), and lines showing where each affordance leads. It settles the flow without drawing an interface.

A fat-marker sketch is a drawing made with a pen so thick that no fine detail is possible. It shows how the elements sit on a screen without specifying pixels, spacing or copy. Use it when the idea is visual and a breadboard cannot capture it. Leaving the detail out is deliberate.

The circuit breaker caps every bet

No extensions by default

If a project does not ship within its cycle, it stops. There is no “just one more week.” If the idea is still worth doing, someone reshapes it and pitches it for a future cycle, starting fresh. This keeps shapers realistic about the appetite and pushes teams to make hard scope decisions early.

The book allows rare exceptions for work that is very close to done: if every remaining task is known execution work (the project is all downhill) and the remaining items are true must-haves, the betting table may give it a little of the cooldown. That is a deliberate decision by leadership, never the default.

Uninterrupted time, then cooldown

Once a team takes a bet, it keeps the full 6 weeks. Leadership does not pull people onto other work mid-cycle, because even one day away costs the team more than a day: it loses the momentum it had built.

Cooldown is the 2 weeks after each cycle. It gives the team unscheduled time for bugs and small ideas, and gives leadership time to decide the next bets. Nothing is due at the end of it: people tidy up what they shipped or explore ideas, which often feed the next round of shaping, while the betting table meets to choose what comes next.

Reading a hill chart

A hill chart replaces percent complete. The left slope is figuring it out: the team still has unknowns and does not yet know exactly how to do the work. The top of the hill is the moment the approach is settled. The right slope is making it happen: only known execution work remains.

Push the unknowns uphill first

Work on the riskiest scopes early, while there is time to change course. A scope that stays on the uphill side is the real risk to the cycle: the team has not yet found a way to do it. Moving dots show progress honestly in a way a percentage cannot.

Larkwell’s pitch for Practice Mode

One big-batch pitch, written to be bet on

Worked example

A piano-learning app that wants learners to practice

Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults, runs its Practice team in 6-week cycles with 2-week cooldowns. Each bet gets one designer and two programmers. All figures are illustrative. Here is the pitch Larkwell’s shapers took to the betting table.

Problem
70% of learners who finish a lesson never return to practice it. The current Practice button only replays the lesson. Learners need a dedicated way to practice, with tempo control, looped sections and a view of their mistakes. A beta with 50 learners, run before the bet, tripled practice sessions per learner from 0.4 to 1.2 a week.
Appetite
Big batch, 6 weeks, for one designer and two programmers. Practice is core to retention and worth a full cycle, but not a full practice suite. The pitch is a focused first version: a tempo slider, section looping and basic mistake highlighting.
Solution
A new Practice screen, opened from any finished lesson, with three elements: a tempo slider (50%–150%), tap-to-set loop markers, and a mistake heatmap, built from existing lesson data, that shows where the learner struggled. One screen; the breadboard under key ideas shows the flow.
Rabbit holes
Do not build real-time audio analysis to detect mistakes. Use the lesson data the app already records to show where errors happened.
No-gos
No recording, no sharing, no custom practice playlists. Each could be a future pitch if this one works.

Why the pitch is ready for the betting table

It is bounded. The appetite caps the time at 6 weeks and the no-gos say plainly what is out.

It is rough. It names three elements and the flow between two places, and leaves the exact interface to the team.

It is solved. The riskiest part, finding mistakes, already has an answer. Calling out the audio rabbit hole keeps the team from losing weeks to audio processing.

How the cycle went

The team mapped four scopes. At week 3, the hill chart under key ideas showed the mistake overlay stuck uphill: a per-note heatmap was harder than expected. The team hammered it to highlighting the bars with the most errors, marked the heatmap as a nice-to-have, and shipped all four scopes in week 6. The bet’s target is the company’s key result, D30 retention from 9% to 11%, and an A/B test running from the first day of release measures whether Practice Mode moves it.

    When Shape Up fits, and when it does not

    It assumes small, senior teams, people with time to shape, and projects measured in weeks

    Use it when

    • Teams are small and senior: a designer and one or two programmers per project
    • Work can be sized in weeks: a 1–2 week small batch or a 6-week project, not a quarter-long program
    • The backlog is overwhelming and full of stale items
    • Teams can execute on their own, without daily direction
    • Leadership wants to make fewer, bigger bets

    Skip it when

    • There are too few senior people with time to shape work alongside each cycle, as often happens in a large organization with many teams. Without shaping, 6-week cycles turn into unbounded projects.
    • Work arrives continuously, as in live operations or support. A flow-based method such as Kanban (not covered in this guide) suits it better than fixed cycles.
    • A regulated environment requires detailed specs and documentation up front
    • The team needs more structure and frequent check-ins
    • External clients expect a demo every week or two. Use Scrum instead.

    Six ways Shape Up goes wrong

    Most of them quietly bring back the habits the method removes

    1. A pitch that is a spec, or a wish

      Wireframes and full specs turn the team into order-takers and hide the decisions it is best placed to make. A pitch with no stated boundaries goes wrong the other way: scope creeps, and the team can lose weeks on an unknown the shaper could have spotted.

      Fix: shape at fat-marker level, settling the elements and the flow, and do not take a pitch to the betting table until it names its rabbit holes and no-gos.

    2. Extending the cycle “just one more week”

      One extension removes the pressure to cut scope and soon becomes a habit. The appetite stops meaning anything, and shapers stop taking it seriously.

      Fix: let the circuit breaker stop the project. Reshape it and pitch it again if it is still worth doing.

    3. Treating scope as fixed

      Reading the pitch as a contract leaves the team two bad options when time runs short: miss the deadline, or skip tests and polish on the core work and ship something worse, not something smaller.

      Fix: hammer the scope throughout the cycle. Cut nice-to-haves and judge what is left against what users have today.

    4. Treating the appetite as an estimate

      Asking engineers how long a scope will take, then calling the answer the appetite, brings back estimation by another name.

      Fix: decide first what the problem is worth, then shape a solution that fits that time.

    5. Reporting percent complete

      A scope can sit at “80% done” for weeks because the hard unknown is still unsolved, and nobody sees the risk until it is late.

      Fix: track scopes on a hill chart, and treat a dot that stops moving uphill as a reason to talk.

    6. Filling cooldown with scheduled work

      Planned work in cooldown leaves teams no time for bugs or exploration, and leaves the betting table no room to think.

      Fix: protect the 2 weeks as unscheduled time. Let people choose what to work on, and leave the betting table time to decide the next bets.

    Check your understanding

    Eight questions on shaping, betting and building

    1. What are the three phases of Shape Up?

      Senior people shape ideas into pitches, the betting table bets on some of them, and a small team builds each bet within a 6-week cycle. Two weeks of cooldown follow.

    2. What is an appetite in Shape Up?

      Appetite reverses estimation. Instead of asking how long something will take, you decide how much time it deserves, then shape a solution that fits. It is a business decision, not an engineering guess.

    3. A project reaches the end of its cycle without shipping. What happens by default?

      The circuit breaker stops unfinished projects at the end of the cycle, which caps the cost of every bet. If the idea is still worth it, someone reshapes it for a future cycle and it starts fresh. Extending is a rare, deliberate exception for work that is all downhill.

    4. On a hill chart, a scope has sat low on the left slope for 2 weeks. What does that tell you?

      The left slope is for figuring out how to do the work; the right slope is for doing it. A dot that stays uphill shows a problem the team has not yet solved, which is the real risk to the cycle and a reason to talk.

    5. Why does Shape Up have no central backlog?

      Pitches that are not picked are let go rather than stored. Anyone can keep their own notes and re-pitch an idea later. Each betting table starts from what matters now, and nobody spends time grooming a list of stale items.

    6. Which statement describes Shape Up’s core tradeoff?

      The appetite fixes the time. The pitch sets the boundaries, and inside them the team hammers scope, cutting nice-to-haves to protect the core. Quality is not what gives way.

    7. What does a breadboard show?

      A breadboard uses words, not pictures: places such as screens, the things a user can act on there, and lines showing where each action goes. It settles the flow without designing the interface.

    8. At week 4 a team sees it cannot finish everything in the pitch. What should it cut first?

      Scope hammering cuts nice-to-haves so the must-haves ship within the cycle. Teams compare against the baseline, what users have today, rather than an ideal version. Testing and quality are not what gets cut, and the cycle does not get longer.
