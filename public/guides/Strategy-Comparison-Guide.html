Porter’s Five Forces, Blue Ocean Strategy, Playing to Win and Wardley Mapping each answer a different strategic question at a different stage of the work. This guide sets them side by side so you can pick the one your question needs, or chain them when the decision is big enough.

In short

Use the Five Forces to judge whether a market is worth entering, Blue Ocean to find space where nobody competes, Playing to Win to commit to specific choices and Wardley Mapping to decide what to build, buy or outsource. Run in that order, each one’s output feeds the next, but most decisions need only the one that matches your question.

Four frameworks for four stages of strategy

From diagnosing a market to planning what to build

Diagnose

Porter’s Five Forces

How attractive is this market?

Rates the five forces that shape how profitable an industry is on average: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, the bargaining power of buyers and the bargaining power of suppliers. The stronger the forces, the harder it is for anyone in the industry to earn high returns.

Its verdict sounds like “Here’s why this market is tough.”

Typical time for a full analysis: 2–4 hours

Discover

Blue Ocean Strategy

Where is the uncontested space?

Aims to make the competition irrelevant through value innovation: a leap in value for buyers while lowering the company’s cost. A strategy canvas shows how offerings compare on the factors the industry competes on, and the four actions framework, captured in an ERRC grid, decides which factors to eliminate, reduce, raise and create.

Its verdict sounds like “Here’s where nobody is competing.”

Typical time for a full analysis: 1–2 weeks

Decide

Playing to Win

What integrated choices will we make?

Treats strategy as five linked choices, the strategy choice cascade: winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities and management systems. Where to play and how to win sit at the core, and each choice must fit the ones above and below it.

Its verdict sounds like “Here are our specific choices.”

Typical time for a full analysis: 1–4 weeks

Plan

Wardley Mapping

What is evolving, and where should we invest?

Places the components that serve a user need by how visible each one is to the user and how evolved it is, from Genesis through Custom-built and Product to Commodity. Because components evolve, the map shows what to build, buy or outsource.

Its verdict sounds like “Here’s what’s changing and how to position for it.”

Typical time for a full analysis: 2–8 hours per map

A workshop gives a first draft in hours

The times above are typical for a full analysis, including the research it needs. A workshop can produce a first draft of any of the four in a few hours, which is often enough to see whether the full analysis is worth doing.

Seven ways the four differ

What each one asks, what it produces and where it falls short

Two rows are judgment calls rather than rules. The criticisms are the ones most often raised, not settled facts. The interview ratings judge how well each framework holds up in a few minutes of talking, with no time to research. The Five Forces is the quickest structure for a market question, and Playing to Win gives the most specific answer to a strategy question. Blue Ocean’s ERRC grid shows creative thinking but takes longer to set up. A Wardley map is too visual to build out loud, so mention it and do not draw a full map.

Time scope is the clearest split

The Five Forces looks at the market as it is today, and Blue Ocean looks ahead at demand nobody serves yet. Playing to Win turns what they find into choices for the next few years. Wardley Mapping then looks ahead on the supply side, at how the components behind those choices will evolve.

Start from the question in front of you

Follow the path until it names a framework

  1. Are you asking whether a market is worth entering, or why it is so hard to make money in it?

    • YesUse the Five Forces. Rate each force to see how attractive the market is and where the pressure comes from.
    • NoGo to question 2

  2. Is the market crowded, and are you looking for the opportunity nobody has taken?

    • YesUse Blue Ocean. Draw the strategy canvas, then use ERRC to find uncontested space.
    • NoGo to question 3

  3. Do you need to commit to the specific choices that define your strategy?

    • YesUse Playing to Win. Work through the five choices, starting with where to play and how to win.
    • NoGo to question 4

  4. Are you deciding what to build, what to buy and what to outsource?

    • YesUse Wardley Mapping. Place each component by evolution stage to see where custom work pays off.
    • NoStart with Porter’s Five Forces. A diagnosis of the market usually shows which question comes next.

Five situations and the framework for each

From a CEO’s new-market idea to an annual offsite

  • “Our CEO wants Larkwell to launch a version for children.”

    Five Forces: Larkwell, a fictional piano-learning app for adults, has no data on this market yet, so the first question is whether it is attractive and which forces it would face.

    How: rate competitive rivalry, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, buyer power and supplier power, then name the force that decides the case. Larkwell’s current where-to-play choice rules children out, so the ratings feed a Playing to Win decision on whether that choice should change.

  • “Every piano app looks the same and subscribers cancel after their first month.”

    Blue Ocean and the ERRC grid: this is a red ocean, and you need to find what to eliminate, reduce, raise and create to break out of it.

    How: draw a strategy canvas against your competitors, talk to people who tried a piano app and left, then build an ERRC grid to find the white space.

  • “In a PM interview: what’s your strategy for this product?”

    The Playing to Win cascade: interviewers want structured thinking with specific choices, not vague ambitions.

    How: state the winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, the capabilities and the management systems in 2–3 minutes.

  • “Our CTO wants to build a custom content management system instead of buying one.”

    A Wardley map: you need to show that a content management system has evolved to Product or beyond, so buying one beats building it, and that engineering should focus on the components that set the product apart.

    How: plot the content management system on the evolution axis, at Product or Commodity, and show where custom building creates real advantage.

  • “We’re planning our annual product strategy offsite.”

    All four, in sequence: you need the full picture of the competitive landscape, the opportunity, the choices and the plan for execution.

    How: the Five Forces on the morning of day 1, Blue Ocean in the afternoon, Playing to Win on day 2 and a Wardley map as a follow-up. Two days allow only a first pass of each, using research prepared beforehand, so gather what a full Blue Ocean or Playing to Win analysis needs before the offsite and finish the work after it.

Each framework feeds the next

Run in order, the four make a strategy pipeline

The forces you diagnose show where to look for open space. The open space gives you options to choose between. The choices tell you which capabilities matter, and the map shows which of them to build yourself.

Worked example

Larkwell’s strategy, through all four steps

Larkwell is a fictional piano-learning app for adults, and every detail here is illustrative. Each step quotes the output that the next step starts from.

  1. Five Forces

    Diagnose: “Rivalry is high: several established subscription apps offer similar lessons and feedback to the same beginners. Substitutes are a high threat: free video tutorials, private teachers and books. Buyer power is high, with monthly plans, no switching costs and public reviews. New entrants are a medium–high threat, because a basic app is cheap to build even though a deep lesson library and song licensing take years. Supplier power is medium: publishers control licensed songs, and app stores take a 15–30% commission.”

  2. Blue Ocean (ERRC)

    Discover: “Eliminate the race for the largest song library and ‘learn any instrument’ positioning. Reduce badges that do not drive learning, the breadth of the catalog and video production cost. Raise the accuracy of real-time feedback, the depth of the piano-only learning path and practice habit design. Create practice plans built from each learner’s own mistakes, milestones tied to real songs they can play, and Play Along with a backing track.”

  3. Playing to Win

    Decide: “Aspire to help every adult who has wanted to play the piano play the songs they love. Play with adult beginners (25–55) in English-speaking markets, on iOS and Android, piano and keyboard only: not children, advanced players, schools or other instruments. Win through differentiation on learning outcomes, not the biggest library or the lowest price. Build capabilities in note detection through microphone and MIDI, lesson design by music educators, habit design and song licensing. Manage by Weekly Active Learners and D30 retention, with every lesson reviewed by an educator and hiring that favors educators and audio engineers.”

  4. Wardley map

    Plan: “The lesson experience is Custom-built and sets Larkwell apart, so build it in-house. Practice plans are at Genesis, so explore them with small bets; they are the next place to set Larkwell apart as note detection becomes a product. Gamification and the cross-platform mobile framework are at Product, so adopt them. Payments and cloud hosting are at Commodity, so use a provider. Note detection is Custom-built but moving toward Product; adopt a library when it matches ours.”

The pipeline stops where execution starts. Its output goes next into OKRs, not into a fifth step: OKRs set quarterly targets for the metrics the management systems choice names. Larkwell’s company key results this quarter raise Weekly Active Learners (learners who complete at least 3 lessons in a calendar week) from 40,000 to 50,000, and D30 retention of new accounts (the share still active on day 30 after sign-up) from 9% to 11%.

Naming the pipeline in an interview

You will rarely use all four in an interview, but naming the pipeline shows that you know how the pieces fit together. A sentence like this one is enough:

Saying it in an interview

I’d start with a Five Forces analysis of the competitive landscape, look for blue ocean opportunities with an ERRC grid, then define specific strategic choices with a Playing to Win cascade. For execution, I’d use a Wardley map of the value chain, which I’d describe rather than draw here, to decide what to build, buy or outsource.

Where the frameworks connect

Concepts in one framework that inform another
FromConnects toWhat it tells you
Five Forces: the threat of substitutesBlue Ocean: noncustomersThe substitutes people choose instead of your product reveal the jobs they are actually trying to get done, and point to the noncustomers worth winning.
Playing to Win: capabilitiesWardley Mapping: the components you buildThe capabilities you choose to build in-house should be the Custom-built or Genesis components of your map, as Larkwell’s note detection and lesson design are. A capability can also rest on a Product-stage component: Larkwell’s song licensing is skill at negotiating standard publisher deals, not something to build. If a “capability” turns out to be a Commodity, rethink it.

ERRC and Wardley work on different things

ERRC works on value factors that buyers see, such as the enological terminology that Casella Wines’ [yellow tail] eliminated in Kim and Mauborgne’s case. A Wardley map places supply-side components you depend on. So an eliminated factor is not a Commodity, and a created factor is not automatically at Genesis: a Commodity component can be essential, and you buy or outsource it rather than remove it. Neither one can confirm a choice made in the other.
